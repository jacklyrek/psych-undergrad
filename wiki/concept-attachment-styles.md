# The Four Attachment Styles

This is the **discrimination page** for the unit's prime confusable set. Ainsworth's Strange Situation ([[theory-attachment]]) sorts infants into three organized patterns; Main & Solomon later added a fourth, *dis*organized, one. The exam trap — and the clinical trap — is confusing them, because the surface behavior can mislead: the *least* distressed baby is not the most secure, and the *clingiest* is not the most attached. **What classifies is the reunion — how the baby uses the caregiver to recover — not the intensity of separation distress.** [S9]

## The discrimination table

| Style (infant) | Strange Situation behavior | Exploration & the secure base | Reunion (the tell) | Typical caregiving [S9] | Adult echo [S8] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Secure (B)** ~70% | distressed at separation, but recovers | explores freely, checks back | seeks comfort, is soothed, returns to play | **sensitive, consistent** responsiveness | **Secure** (low anxiety / low avoidance) |
| **Avoidant (A)** ~15% | little visible separation distress | explores, but doesn't reference the caregiver | **ignores or avoids** caregiver at reunion; treats stranger similarly | consistently **unresponsive / rejecting of distress** | **Dismissing** (low anxiety / high avoidance) |
| **Resistant / Ambivalent (C)** ~15% | intense distress; wary even before separation | too anxious to explore; clings | **seeks contact *and* resists it** — reaches to be picked up, then arches away, stays angry, not soothed | **inconsistent** — sometimes responsive, sometimes not | **Preoccupied** (high anxiety / low avoidance) |
| **Disorganized (D)** | contradictory, odd, or fearful | strategy breaks down | **no coherent strategy** — freezing, approaching then backing away, dazed/trance-like, fear of the caregiver | frightening / frightened parenting; abuse; unresolved parental loss/trauma | **Fearful** (high anxiety / high avoidance) |

### How to keep the two insecure-organized styles apart

The most-confused pair is **avoidant vs. resistant**, so pin the contrast:

- **Avoidant** = **deactivation.** The baby has learned that showing need gets nothing, so it *turns the need down* — looks independent, self-soothes, doesn't bother seeking comfort that won't come. The caregiver was consistently *un*available, so the strategy is: **don't ask.**
- **Resistant/ambivalent** = **hyperactivation.** The baby has learned that need *sometimes* works and sometimes doesn't, so it *turns the need up* — clings, protests loudly, and can't settle even when comfort arrives (because comfort is unreliable, so keep signaling). The caregiver was *inconsistent*, so the strategy is: **ask louder and don't let go.**

Same underlying alarm, two opposite strategies, matched to two different caregiving patterns. Avoidant and resistant are both **organized** — coherent (if costly) strategies. That's what separates them from disorganized.

### Disorganized is a different kind of category

Secure, avoidant, and resistant are all *strategies* — organized ways of managing a caregiver. **Disorganized is the absence of a working strategy.** [S7] It appears where the caregiver is *both* the source of alarm *and* the only possible source of comfort — "fright without solution." The baby's attachment system says *go to the caregiver* and its fear system says *get away from the caregiver* at the same time, and the behavior collapses into contradiction (freezing mid-approach, a dazed look, approaching backward). It is most associated with frightening/frightened parenting, maltreatment, and a parent's own unresolved loss or trauma — and it is the classification most robustly linked to later difficulty.

## Disorganized attachment: a risk factor, held carefully

Of the four, disorganized carries the most predictive weight — it is a **broad, transdiagnostic relational risk factor**, linked to later internalizing and (especially) externalizing problems, dissociation, and adult borderline features. [S7] But the same caution as the whole unit applies with extra force here, because the material is heavy:

- The link is **probabilistic and moderated by later context**, not deterministic. Many children classified disorganized in infancy do *not* develop disorders; later relationships, including therapy, can revise the internal working model. [S3][S7]
- Disorganized attachment is **not a diagnosis** and not "reactive attachment disorder" (a separate, much rarer DSM condition). It's a research classification of infant behavior, not a label to hang on a client.
- Early parenting interventions can **reduce** rates of disorganization in at-risk dyads — it is a target, not a sentence. [S7]

## Adults: the same map, two dimensions

Adult attachment ([[theory-attachment]]) rebuilds these four on **two continua** — **anxiety** (about abandonment/worth) and **avoidance** (of closeness/dependence) — rather than four boxes: [S8]

- **Secure** — low/low: comfortable with closeness and with autonomy.
- **Dismissing** — low anxiety, high avoidance: "I'm fine on my own," minimizes needing anyone (adult avoidant).
- **Preoccupied** — high anxiety, low avoidance: craves closeness, fears abandonment, seeks reassurance (adult resistant/ambivalent).
- **Fearful** — high/high: wants closeness *and* fears it, the approach–avoidance bind (nearest adult echo of disorganized).

Because these are **dimensions**, adults sit on a gradient and can shift by relationship and over time — which is exactly why the alliance can be corrective (see [[theory-attachment]]).

## In the room: reading the style, resisting the type

- The **dismissing** client who says "I don't really do feelings" and keeps sessions abstract is not disengaged — they're *deactivating*, doing exactly what worked in childhood. Reading that as "not a good therapy candidate" repeats the original rejection.
- The **preoccupied** client who floods, texts between sessions, and can't be reassured for long is *hyperactivating* — the pull to either rescue or withdraw both confirm the model; steadiness disconfirms it.
- **STANCE:** name the pattern to *yourself* to understand the client; do **not** announce "you have an avoidant attachment style" as if it were a verdict. Attachment is a lens for empathy and prediction, not a category you file a person under. (See [[unit05-development]] on risk vs. destiny.)

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **The percentages are from a small US middle-class sample.** Cross-culturally, secure is still modal everywhere, but the avoidant/resistant split shifts, and the Strange Situation may be an "imposed etic" — German independence can be scored as avoidance, Japanese separation-distress as resistance, because those infants are rarely left alone. [S6]
- **Caregiving is not the sole cause** of a style — temperament and the transmission gap complicate the clean story (see [[theory-attachment]]). [S3][S9]
- **Adult self-report "style" is not the AAI.** Popular attachment-style quizzes measure conscious romantic beliefs, not the unconscious state of mind the AAI codes. [S8]
