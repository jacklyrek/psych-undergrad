# Death Notification — Telling Someone Their Person Has Died

The hardest sentence in acute care: someone asks *"Where is she?"* and the answer is that she is
dead. This page covers how that news is delivered — and how to answer the question honestly when
you *don't* yet know. It sits between [[concept-pfa-core-actions]] (this is Core Action 2, Safety
and Comfort, in its most extreme form) and [[concept-supporting-the-bereaved]] (everything that
happens *after* the words).

**The one rule that governs everything here: never lie, and never stall with soothing vagueness.**
False reassurance feels kind for thirty seconds; when the truth arrives it retroactively poisons
everything else you said and did. The NCTSN field guide is explicit that premature reassurance
about a missing loved one is a harm to avoid. [S2]

## Who delivers it (role boundary first)

Formal notification belongs to whoever holds confirmed knowledge and the authority to speak it —
medical staff, police, a chaplain or medical examiner's office. Law-enforcement practice codifies
five principles: **in person, in time, in pairs, in plain language, with compassion.** [S18]
"In time" matters more than comfort: the family should hear it from a person, not the news or
social media. "In pairs" exists because the reaction may need two sets of hands — one for the
bereaved, one for logistics or for another family member who collapses.

If you are a lay helper and the death is not confirmed *to you*, notification is not your job —
but stonewalling isn't either. Your job is the honest-uncertainty script below, plus visibly
fetching the person who does know.

## In plain language: the "D-words"

Every protocol converges on the same instruction: say **"died"** or **"dead."** Not "passed,"
"lost," "didn't make it," "gone," "expired." [S17][S18] This is not bluntness for its own sake —
under acute shock, people take euphemisms literally or refuse to parse them (*"lost? then go find
her"*), and ambiguity forces the bereaved to do the inferential work of concluding their child is
dead, which is crueler than the word. The MADD/DOJ curriculum calls the euphemisms what they are:
**"words of denial."** [S18]

## The clinical protocol: GRIEV_ING

The emergency-medicine standard, from Hobgood et al. — training built on it measurably improved
physicians' notification skill and confidence. [S16][S17] The underscore is a step:

| Letter | Step | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **G** | Gather | Get the family together, seated, in a private space |
| **R** | Resources | Call in support — chaplain, other family, friends |
| **I** | Identify | Yourself, the deceased **by name**, and what the family already knows |
| **E** | Educate | Briefly: what happened, what was done |
| **V** | Verify | Say it: **"died" / "dead."** No euphemisms |
| **_** | (Space) | Stop talking. Let it land. Tolerate the reaction |
| **I** | Inquire | Invite and answer every question |
| **N** | Nuts and bolts | Viewing the body, belongings, funeral steps, organ donation |
| **G** | Give | Your contact info — questions will come later |

Note the shape: the actual news is one plain sentence in the middle, wrapped in preparation before
and presence after. Most of the protocol is *not* the sentence.

## The three scenarios (the discrimination that matters)

What you say to *"Where is my child?"* depends on which situation you are in — and confusing them
is where helpers do damage.

**1. Death is confirmed, and they don't know yet.** Warning shot, then plain words, then the name:

> "Mrs. Alvarez, I have very hard news. … Sofia died in the accident. She's dead. I am so sorry."

Then **stop talking and stay.** Collapse, screaming, flat denial, anger at you — all are normal
first responses; none need correcting. If the person is flooded past function, this is exactly the
stabilization skill of [[concept-pfa-core-actions]] (Core Action 3): calm presence, time,
grounding — not more information.

**2. You genuinely don't know** (missing person, unconfirmed). Honesty about uncertainty, in both
directions — no *"I'm sure she's fine"* and no grim hinting: [S2]

> "I don't know where Sofia is right now. Here is what I do know: rescue teams are searching that
> building. Here is what I'm going to do: I'll find the person keeping the family list, and I will
> come back to you either way."

Then actually do it. Addressing the concrete concern *is* the stabilization.

**3. They were told, and they're asking again** — shock, dissociation, or the first crash of
denial. Answer truthfully every time, gently, without escalating detail and without irritation:

> "She died, remember — she's at the hospital now. I'm right here with you."

Repetition is not a failure to hear you; it is the mind metering out something unbearable
([[concept-grief-models]] — accepting the reality of the loss is Worden's *task*, not an event).

**Side-by-side — instinct vs. practice:**

| Instinct says | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "She's being taken care of." *(she's dead)* | "I have very hard news. Sofia died." |
| "I'm sure they'll find him." | "I don't know yet. Here's what I know, and I'll come back to you either way." |
| "She passed on." / "We lost her." | "She died." |
| Answering "where is she?" with silence or a subject change | The truth, then presence. |
| Re-explaining the medical details when asked again | The same plain sentence, again, kindly. |

## "Can I see her?" — viewing the body

Answer the literal question too: where the body is, and whether they can see their person. The
evidence supports an **offered, prepared, genuine choice** — neither pushing viewing nor shielding
people from it. In Chapple & Ziebland's study of 80 people bereaved by suicide or other traumatic
death, most who viewed said it was the right decision (35 of 49; only 2 regretted it, both with
badly damaged bodies and no real choice), while some who didn't view were later haunted by not
knowing. Their conclusion: relatives should have the **opportunity** to view, with preparation for
what they will see, time to decide, and room for different family members to choose differently —
and "clinicians should not assume that relatives will be harmed by seeing a bruised or damaged
body." [S19][S20] For a bereaved parent specifically, being able to see and hold their child is
often part of grasping that the death is real — but it is their choice, made with honest
preparation, never a prescription. [S2][S19]

## After the words

The notification ends; the accompaniment begins. Everything in
[[concept-supporting-the-bereaved]] applies from this minute — presence over fixing, the child's
name, concrete help, no timelines. Do not hand a freshly notified parent a pamphlet and leave;
*linkage* (Core Action 8) means a warm connection to a person, and GRIEV_ING's final *Give* step —
a name and number for the questions that arrive at 2 a.m. — is the clinical version of "keep
showing up." [S16][S17]
