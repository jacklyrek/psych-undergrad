# The Cognitive Triangle & the Thought Record

This is the single most portable idea in the whole CBT toolkit, and the one clients remember. **Events don't directly cause feelings — our *interpretation* of events does.** The counselor's job is to make that interpretation visible and testable. The **cognitive triangle** is the diagram of this claim; the **thought record** is the worksheet that operationalizes it. Together they are the practical core of [[theory-cbt-practice]] (the theory is Unit 2's [[theory-cbt]]).

## The triangle: thoughts ⇄ feelings ⇄ behaviors

The three points influence each other, but CBT's founding move is to insert a step between the *situation* and the *feeling*:

> **Situation → automatic thought → emotion / behavior** [S1][S4]

The canonical example [S4]: a friend walks past you in a hallway without saying hello.
- Thought *"He hates me / I did something wrong"* → feeling: hurt, anxious → behavior: withdraw, ruminate.
- Thought *"He's in a hurry / didn't see me"* → feeling: neutral → behavior: text him later.

**Same event, different thought, different feeling.** The event didn't change; the interpretation did. This is liberating for a client because it locates a point of leverage: you can't always change the situation, but you can examine the thought. Behavior is on the triangle too — how you act feeds back into what you think and feel, which is the bridge to [[concept-behavioral-activation]].

## Three levels of cognition (why the same situation hits some people harder)

Automatic thoughts don't come from nowhere; they're generated by deeper beliefs [S2]:

1. **Automatic thoughts** — immediate, unbidden, surface-level ("I'm going to fail this"). The most accessible; where a thought record starts.
2. **Intermediate beliefs** — conditional rules and assumptions ("If I don't do everything perfectly, I'm a failure"). Often unstated.
3. **Core beliefs / schemas** — global, absolute self-judgments formed early ("I am inadequate," "I'm unlovable," "the world is dangerous"). They act as a lens that generates the automatic thoughts.

Beginning CBT works at the **automatic-thought** level (accessible, testable); deeper work reaches for the schemas over time. You don't open with "let's examine your core belief that you're unlovable."

## Cognitive distortions: the catalog of thinking errors

Teaching a client to *name* systematic distortions lets them catch thoughts in real time [S3]:

- **All-or-nothing / dichotomous thinking** — no middle ground ("if I'm not perfect I'm a failure").
- **Catastrophizing** — assuming the worst-case and treating it as certain.
- **Overgeneralization** — one event becomes "always / never / everyone."
- **Mind reading** — assuming you know what others think (usually badly).
- **Personalization** — taking responsibility for things outside your control.
- **Emotional reasoning** — "I feel worthless, so I must be."
- **"Should" statements** — rigid rules that generate guilt and pressure.

Naming is not dismissing: the point isn't "your thought is a distortion, so ignore it," but "this *looks like* a catastrophizing pattern — let's check it against the evidence."

## The thought record (the workhorse)

The thought record turns the triangle into a repeatable procedure — the tool you'll actually assign as homework [S3]. Columns:

| Situation | Automatic thought | Emotion (0–100) | Evidence *for* | Evidence *against* | Balanced alternative | Re-rate emotion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Friend walked past | "He hates me" | anxious 80 | he didn't wave | he was on his phone; he texted later; we're fine | "He probably didn't see me; one moment isn't the friendship" | anxious 30 |

The move is **collaborative empiricism** ([[theory-cbt-practice]]): you don't hand the client the "balanced alternative," you use **Socratic questioning** to help them generate it — *"What's the evidence on the other side? What would you tell a friend who thought this? Is there another explanation?"* [S3]. A client who produces their own reframe believes it more (the **generation effect** from the learning-science rulebook) than one who is told.

## The stance traps

- **Correcting instead of testing.** "That thought is irrational" argues with the client and re-enacts the fix-it reflex. The move is to *test together*, not to overrule. Distortion labels are hypotheses, not verdicts.
- **Toxic positivity / "just think positive."** CBT is about **accurate** thinking, not cheerful thinking. Sometimes the evidence supports the negative thought — and then the work shifts to problem-solving or acceptance, not to a forced silver lining.
- **Invalidating real adversity.** If a client's situation genuinely is bad (poverty, discrimination, loss), reframing the *thought* without acknowledging the *reality* is a way to miss the person. The thought record complements, not replaces, seeing the situation clearly (cf. Unit 4 on not psychologizing structural harm).
