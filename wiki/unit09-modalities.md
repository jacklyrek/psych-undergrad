# Unit 9 — Evidence-Based Modalities (a practical toolkit)

Units 1–8 built the room: the relationship (Unit 1), the map of theories (Unit 2), the rules (Unit 3), the cultural lens (Unit 4), what's normal (Unit 5), what goes wrong (Unit 6), how to assess it (Unit 7), and how to handle danger (Unit 8). This unit hands you the **tools you actually reach for** once you're in the chair with a real person and a real problem. It is deliberately concrete: the syllabus frames it as "hands-on familiarity with a few methods you'll likely use directly." These are the structured, protocol-like interventions — the part of the work most legible to a technically-minded helper — which is exactly why the unit opens and closes with the same warning: **a technique is only ever as good as the relationship it runs on** ([[theory-common-factors]]).

- **→ the electives.** The **[[aux-addiction]]** module applies MI and CBT to substance use specifically; the change-stages map ([[concept-stages-of-change]]) is the terrain MI moves a client across. This unit is the general-tools home those modules pointed forward to.

## What "evidence-based" actually means here

The unit's title is a small trap. **Evidence-based practice (EBP)** is not "run the manual with the biggest effect size." The APA definition is the integration of three things: **(1) the best available research, (2) clinical expertise, and (3) the client's characteristics, culture, and preferences** [S21]. A method with strong research support that the client won't engage, or that ignores their culture (Unit 4) or their stage of readiness (Unit 8's crisis stance, the electives' [[concept-stages-of-change]]), is not "evidence-based practice" for *this* person. You are assembling a toolkit and choosing from it, not ranking techniques and always grabbing #1.

And the humbling backdrop from Unit 1: across bona-fide therapies, **specific-technique differences are usually small, and the alliance carries a large share of the outcome** — the "dodo-bird" finding [S22]. This does not make technique pointless; it means technique works **through** the relationship, not instead of it. Every "which modality fits this client?" item in this unit sits under that caveat.

## The six tools, and the three "waves"

CBT-family therapies are often described in three historical **waves** [S20], which is the cleanest way to organize the unit:

| Wave | Targets | This unit's tools |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1st — behavioral** | observable behavior; conditioning, reinforcement, avoidance | **Behavioral Activation** ([[concept-behavioral-activation]]) |
| **2nd — cognitive** | the *content* of thought; is it accurate? | **CBT / cognitive restructuring** ([[theory-cbt-practice]], [[concept-cognitive-triangle]]) |
| **3rd — contextual** | your *relationship to* thought; acceptance over control | **ACT** ([[concept-act]]) and **DBT** ([[concept-dbt-skills]]) |

Two more tools sit outside the wave scheme because they're **styles / stances** rather than content models:

- **Motivational Interviewing** ([[theory-motivational-interviewing]], [[concept-oars]]) — not a treatment so much as a *way of talking* that resolves **ambivalence**. It's what you use *before* any of the above will land, when the client isn't sure they want to change.
- **Solution-Focused Brief Therapy** ([[theory-solution-focused]]) — a postmodern *toolkit* that ignores the problem's origins and builds on exceptions and a described future.

| Tool | The one idea | Reach for it when… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[theory-cbt-practice]] / [[concept-cognitive-triangle]] | events don't cause feelings — *interpretations* do; test the thought | distorted thinking maintains distress (depression, anxiety, panic) |
| [[concept-behavioral-activation]] | act your way into feeling, not feel your way into acting | depression, withdrawal, avoidance — even without touching thoughts |
| [[theory-motivational-interviewing]] / [[concept-oars]] | let the *client* voice the arguments for change | ambivalence: substance use, behavior change, "I know I should, but…" |
| [[theory-solution-focused]] | build the solution, don't excavate the problem | brief work, strengths, clients tired of problem-talk |
| [[concept-act]] | stop fighting inner experience; move toward values | chronic pain/anxiety, rumination, "control agenda" has failed |
| [[concept-dbt-skills]] | hold **acceptance and change** together; teach skills | emotion dysregulation, self-harm, BPD, crisis-prone clients |

## The stance thread runs straight through this unit

This whole curriculum keeps flagging the beginner's urge to "fix." Unit 9 is where that urge is most tempting — these *are* the fixing tools — and, remarkably, where two of the methods make **resisting the fix** their central technique:

- **Motivational Interviewing names the trap by its name.** The **righting reflex** — your impulse to jump in, warn, and correct the client toward health — reliably makes the client defend the *status quo* [S10]. MI's entire method is to hold that reflex and let the client argue for their own change. This is the Unit 1 stance shift, formalized into a technique.
- **The third-wave therapies invert "fix" into "accept."** ACT and DBT stop trying to *change or eliminate* difficult thoughts and feelings and instead change the client's **relationship** to them (acceptance, defusion, distress tolerance) [S15][S19]. The paradox — you change by stopping the struggle to change — is the opposite of the debugging reflex.

Even the two most protocol-like tools carry the caution: CBT is **collaborative empiricism** (you test thoughts *with* the client, you don't correct them) [S1], and behavioral activation is done *with* a depressed client's buy-in, not prescribed at them.

## What's contested (hold these carefully)

- **"Evidence-based" ≠ "best." ** The alliance and other common factors account for much of the outcome; between bona-fide therapies, technique differences are typically small [S22]. Choosing a modality matters most for *fit and engagement*, less as a horse race.
- **The cognitive part of CBT may not be the active ingredient.** Jacobson's **dismantling** trial found behavioral activation *alone* worked as well as full CBT with cognitive restructuring added [S5]; Dimidjian 2006 found BA matched medication and beat cognitive therapy for severe depression [S8]. This genuinely complicates the intuitive "you must fix the thoughts" story.
- **Third-wave ≠ proven-superior.** ACT and DBT are evidence-based, but they have **not** been shown to outperform standard CBT for most conditions; "third wave" is a family and a philosophy, not a ranking [S20].
- **Allegiance and publication bias inflate the numbers**, especially in the SFBT literature — the largest reported effect sizes come from proponent sources; the peer-reviewed meta-analytic estimate is more modest [S12][S13].
