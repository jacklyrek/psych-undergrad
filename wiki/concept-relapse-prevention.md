# Relapse Prevention: Marlatt's Model, Lapses, and the AVE

Getting someone to stop is one problem; helping them **stay** stopped is a different one, and it has
its own evidence-based model. **Marlatt & Gordon's Relapse Prevention (RP)** is a cognitive-behavioral
framework that treats relapse **not as a random collapse of willpower** but as a **predictable process
with identifiable triggers and decision points** — which means it can be anticipated, rehearsed for,
and interrupted. [S7] Its most important single idea is the difference between a **lapse** and a
**relapse**, and the psychological trap (**the AVE**) that turns the first into the second.

## The model: what actually drives a return to use

RP distinguishes **immediate determinants** from **covert antecedents.** [S7]

**Immediate determinants**
- **High-risk situations** — the proximal trigger. Marlatt's classic three: **negative emotional
  states** (the biggest — anger, anxiety, boredom, sadness), **interpersonal conflict**, and
  **social pressure.** Also celebration, cues, and testing control. This is where a slip is decided.
- **Coping response** — the hinge. If the person has a coping skill *and* uses it, **self-efficacy**
  ("I can handle this") rises and relapse risk drops. If coping is absent or fails, self-efficacy
  falls and the odds of use climb.
- **Outcome expectancies** — what the person *believes* the drug will do for them in that moment
  (remembering the relief, not the wreckage — "positive outcome expectancies").

**Covert antecedents** (the slow setup)
- **Lifestyle imbalance** — too many "shoulds," too few "wants"; stress with no restoration.
- **Urges and cravings** — often cue-triggered (see [[concept-neuroscience-of-addiction]]).
- **Seemingly irrelevant decisions** — the small, deniable choices ("I'll just keep his old number,"
  "I'll take the route past the bar") that quietly walk a person into a high-risk situation.

## Lapse vs. relapse — the distinction the whole model turns on

- **Lapse** — a **single** slip, one instance of use after a period of change.
- **Relapse** — a **return to the prior pattern** of problematic use.

The point: **a lapse does not have to become a relapse.** What determines which way it goes is less
the slip itself than the person's **reaction** to it. [S7]

## The Abstinence Violation Effect (AVE)

The **AVE** is the destructive reaction that turns a lapse into a relapse. After a slip, a person who
holds a rigid all-or-nothing rule feels a wave of **guilt, shame, and failure** ("I've blown it, I'm
a failure, I've ruined everything") and makes a **global, internal, stable attribution** ("this proves
I can't do it"). That collapse of self-efficacy — plus the "well, I've already broken it" logic —
drives continued use, converting one drink into a bender. [S7] RP defuses the AVE **in advance** by
reframing a lapse as a **single, situational, learn-from-able event** — an expected turn of the
[[concept-stages-of-change]] spiral, not a verdict on the person. A client told beforehand that a slip
is *data for the plan, not proof of doom* is far less likely to free-fall.

## What relapse prevention actually does

RP is skills work built on this map: [S7]
- **Identify each client's specific high-risk situations** and rehearse concrete coping for them
  (refusal skills, urge-surfing, leaving, calling support).
- **Build self-efficacy** through mastery of small, real challenges.
- **Correct outcome expectancies** — weigh the *whole* consequence, not the remembered relief.
- **Manage the lapse in advance** — a written "if I slip" plan that heads off the AVE.
- **Rebalance the lifestyle** — add genuine sources of reward so abstinence isn't pure deprivation.

A common bedside version of the high-risk cues is **HALT** — beware being **H**ungry, **A**ngry,
**L**onely, or **T**ired, the everyday states that quietly load the gun. (Widely used clinically;
consistent with Marlatt's "negative emotional states" as the top trigger.)

## Cravings are waves
A practical corollary from the neuroscience: **cravings crest and pass** (typically minutes, not
hours). The RP skill of **"urge surfing"** — noticing the craving, and riding it out without acting,
knowing it will recede — rests on this. The catastrophic belief is "this craving will only get worse
until I use," which is false; teaching the wave shape is itself an intervention.

## Why this matters in the room
Relapse prevention is where the **"relapse ≠ failure"** principle stops being a slogan and becomes a
plan. It lets a helper do two things at once: take a slip **seriously** (adjust the plan, tighten
support) while refusing to let it become a **shame spiral** (which is itself the mechanism of relapse).
The clinical move after a lapse is curiosity, not condemnation: *what was the situation, what coping
was available, what do we change* — treating the person as a collaborator debugging a plan, not a
sinner who fell.
