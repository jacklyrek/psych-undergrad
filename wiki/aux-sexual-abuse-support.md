# Elective Module — Supporting Survivors of Sexual Abuse & Assault

> **This is an off-syllabus elective module, not a numbered unit.** It lives in the `aux-` namespace (see the *Ad-hoc / elective modules* section of [`../CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md)) and cross-links into **[[unit08-crisis-trauma | Unit 8 — Crisis, Risk Assessment & Trauma-Informed Care]]** (the trauma stance and the risk screen), **[[unit03-ethics-law | Unit 3 — Ethics, Law & Professional Identity]]** (mandated reporting and the limits of confidentiality), and **[[unit06-psychopathology | Unit 6 — Psychopathology & the DSM-5-TR]]** (PTSD and the trauma- and stressor-related disorders). Built on request: *how do you actually help someone who has been sexually abused or assaulted* — both as a counselor and as a person who loves them.

> **The one-line version:** what you do in the first few minutes after someone tells you measurably changes how they recover — and the research says the helpful move is to **believe, not investigate**. Negative reactions to a disclosure predict worse PTSD; positive ones predict a greater sense of **control over recovery**, which is the thing that actually predicts getting better. [S14][S15] Your job is not to establish what happened, extract the story, or decide what they should do next. It is to receive the disclosure without flinching, hand control back, and connect them to people trained for the rest.

## Deliberate boundary: this module is not trauma-informed care

**Trauma-informed care is a separate body of material and is deliberately not rebuilt here.** SAMHSA's three E's, four R's, and six principles, the definition of re-traumatization, and the trauma-*informed* vs. trauma-*treatment* distinction all live in Unit 8's [[concept-trauma-informed-care]] — and, since **2026-09-14**, are developed in depth in the **[[aux-trauma-informed-care]]** elective module. [S40] (The Unit 8 page was retained on the spine rather than moved; this module's links to it never needed rewiring.) This module assumes that material and applies it to one population; it does not restate it. If you have not read [[concept-trauma-informed-care]], read it first — everything here sits on top of it.

What *is* specific to this module, and would not be covered by a general trauma-informed-care unit: the epidemiology and definitional structure of sexual violence, the grooming and delayed-disclosure dynamics of child sexual abuse, the evidence on **what a first response does to outcomes**, tonic immobility, the medical-forensic and reporting systems a survivor has to navigate, and the specific treatments built for this trauma.

## Why the first response is the clinical act

Most of this module reduces to one empirical finding and its consequences. Sarah Ullman's research program measured what happens to survivors after they tell someone, and found that social reactions to disclosure predict PTSD symptoms — **negative reactions predict more**, and positive reactions predict **greater perceived control over recovery**, which in turn predicts fewer symptoms. [S14] Perceived control is the mediator. That is the mechanism, and it explains why the effective response is not warmth alone: sexual violence is an event that took away a person's control over their own body, and a response that *takes more decisions away from them* — even kindly, even to protect them — replays the injury. [S15][S19]

The corollary is uncomfortable for a helper: **the urge to do something is the thing to manage.** Calling the police for them, driving them to the hospital before asking, telling their family, deciding they need therapy, or pressing for details so you can "understand properly" are all moves that feel like help and take control away. The syllabus's standing warning about the fix-it reflex ([[unit01-helping-skills]]) is sharper here than anywhere else in the curriculum, because here the reflex reproduces the structure of the harm.

## The stance, in one paragraph

You are not an investigator, a detective, or a fact-finder, and the moment you behave like one you have changed what the conversation is for. [S13] A survivor telling you is not presenting a case; they are taking a risk with someone whose reaction they cannot predict. The response that helps is small and unglamorous: say you believe them, say it was not their fault, thank them for telling you, and then **ask what they want** rather than deciding. [S17] Do not ask what they were wearing, whether they fought, why they waited, or whether they are sure. Do not promise a child secrecy you cannot keep. [S12] Do not extract a narrative — for a child, repeated untrained questioning **contaminates** the account that a trained forensic interviewer will need [S13]; for an adult, it re-enacts the loss of control that the assault created and is not what makes people better anyway. [S14] Tolerate the silence, tolerate not knowing, and route to the people trained for the parts you are not.

## What's genuinely contested / easy to over-learn

- **CSAAS is half-right, and the half matters.** **Delayed and partial disclosure are well-supported** — children commonly do not tell, or tell in fragments, over time. [S10][S11] But **routine recantation is not supported**: most children who disclose in a competent interview do not later retract. [S10] And CSAAS **cannot be used as evidence that abuse occurred** — it is clinically derived and, as applied, treats disclosure *and* non-disclosure as equally consistent with abuse, which is why appellate courts including the New Jersey Supreme Court in *State v. J.L.G.* have held it inadmissible for that purpose. [S8][S9] Learn the disclosure dynamics; do not learn CSAAS as a detector. — see [[concept-child-sexual-abuse]].
- **Trauma memories may or may not be "fragmented."** Brewin's position (trauma memories are disorganized and incoherent) and Rubin's (they are as coherent as other vivid autobiographical memories) are both actively defended in the peer-reviewed literature, and reviews call the evidence inconclusive partly because "coherence" is measured inconsistently. [S26] The clinically usable consequence: **an inconsistent or non-chronological account is neither evidence of trauma nor evidence of fabrication**, and you are not equipped to adjudicate which. — see [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]].
- **The recovered-memory question is unsettled; the practice rule is not.** Whether memories of real abuse can become inaccessible and later return is genuinely disputed. [S36] What both camps accept is that **suggestive techniques — hypnosis, guided imagery, age regression, repeated suggestive questioning, symptom-checklist-driven conviction that abuse "must have" happened — can create confident false memories**, and have produced malpractice liability and de-licensure. Never try to help someone "recover" a memory. — see [[concept-sexual-trauma-treatment]].
- **Behavioral self-blame is probably not protective.** Janoff-Bulman's influential distinction between **behavioral** ("I shouldn't have walked home alone") and **characterological** ("I'm the kind of person this happens to") self-blame proposed that the behavioral form is adaptive because it preserves a sense of control. [S24] Subsequent survivor-sample research found **both** forms associated with greater depression and distress. [S25] Keep the taxonomy — it tells you what kind of blame you are hearing — and drop the claim that one kind is good for people.
- **"Rape trauma syndrome" is history, not a diagnosis.** Burgess and Holmstrom's 1974 two-phase description predates PTSD's 1980 entry into DSM-III, which largely absorbed it. Survivors today are assessed and treated under the PTSD framework; RTS persists mainly as a courtroom term, and courts describe it as **"a therapeutic concept"** with limited evidentiary use. [S23]
- **Prevalence numbers are only as good as their definitions.** CDC's NISVS (behaviorally-defined experiences, telephone self-report) and BJS's NCVS (crime-framed incidents) measure different things and **do not reconcile** — that is a definitional gap, not a contradiction. [S2][S4] NISVS's 2016/2017 sample was also **too small to estimate victimization of transgender people**, which is a limitation of the instrument and not a finding about prevalence. [S2] Quote the survey with the number or do not quote the number.
- **Title IX's rules are in flux and should not be memorized.** The 2024 Final Rule was **vacated nationwide on January 9, 2025**, and the Department of Education reverted to the 2020 regulations. [S30] The durable lesson is structural: on a campus, some employees are **confidential** and some must report to the Title IX Coordinator — and **which one you are determines what you can honestly promise a student before they begin talking.** Verify the current rule; learn the structure. — see [[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]].
