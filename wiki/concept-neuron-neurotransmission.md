# The Neuron & Neurotransmission (the substrate)

Everything else in this unit — the stress response, the medications, the way therapy changes the brain — is built out of one repeating event: **one neuron passing a signal to the next across a tiny gap.** You need a working picture of that event, and a working vocabulary for the chemicals that carry it, because that is the level medications act on and the level the "chemical imbalance" story either does or doesn't hold up at. This page is the substrate; the rest of the unit stands on it.

## The neuron and the synapse, in one pass

The brain is roughly **86 billion neurons**, each a cell specialized to receive, integrate, and send signals. A neuron receives input on its branching **dendrites**, sums it in the **cell body**, and — if the input crosses a threshold — fires an electrical impulse (the **action potential**) down its **axon**. The signal is *electrical* **within** a neuron but **chemical between** neurons: axons don't touch the next cell. They stop just short, at a junction called the **synapse**, separated by a gap (the **synaptic cleft**).

Here is the whole handoff, and every drug in this unit acts somewhere along it:

1. The action potential reaches the axon terminal and triggers **calcium-evoked release** of tiny sacs (**vesicles**) of **neurotransmitter** into the cleft [S1].
2. The neurotransmitter diffuses across and **binds receptors** on the next (postsynaptic) neuron — like a key in a lock. Binding either **excites** that neuron (makes it more likely to fire) or **inhibits** it (less likely).
3. The signal is then **cleared** so it doesn't fire forever: the neurotransmitter is either **broken down by enzymes** in the cleft or **pumped back** into the sending neuron by **reuptake transporters** to be recycled [S8].

That third step is worth holding onto. **Reuptake** is how most transmitters are switched off — and blocking reuptake is exactly how the most-prescribed antidepressants work (see below and [[concept-psychotropic-classes]]).

## The neurotransmitters worth knowing

You do not need the full catalog. You need the handful that psychiatric medications target and that clients hear names for. The single most important distinction is **excitatory vs. inhibitory** — the brain runs on a balance of accelerators and brakes.

| Neurotransmitter | Excitatory / inhibitory | Working gloss | Clinical association |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glutamate** | **the** master excitatory | the brain's main "go" signal; central to learning/memory and **plasticity** [S1] | too much = **excitotoxicity** (Alzheimer's, stroke); implicated in schizophrenia, and the target of ketamine |
| **GABA** | **the** master inhibitory | the brain's main "stop" signal; ~40% of inhibitory processing [S1] | the calming system **benzodiazepines** and alcohol enhance; anxiety, insomnia, epilepsy |
| **Dopamine** | modulatory | reward/motivation, movement, executive function [S1] | **too much** (mesolimbic) → psychosis; **too little** (nigrostriatal) → Parkinson's; reward circuit in [[concept-neuroscience-of-addiction | addiction]]; ADHD |
| **Serotonin** | modulatory | mood, sleep, appetite, **and gut** (~90% is in the GI tract) [S1] | depression/anxiety; the target of **SSRIs** — though the causal story is contested (below) |
| **Norepinephrine** | modulatory | arousal, alertness, attention, the stress response [S1] | anxiety, PTSD, ADHD, depression; the "NE" in **SNRIs**; the sympathetic transmitter ([[concept-autonomic-nervous-system]]) |
| **Acetylcholine** | excitatory (mostly) | muscle movement, **memory/learning**, the parasympathetic transmitter [S1][S2] | Alzheimer's (memory); the ANS's dominant chemical |

Two of these — **glutamate** and **GABA** — are the workhorses (the accelerator and the brake that most of the brain actually runs on). The famous ones — **dopamine** and **serotonin** — are **modulators**: they don't carry most of the traffic, they *tune* it. That distinction matters for the myth-busting later: "your serotonin is low" treats a fine-tuning knob as if it were the main power supply.

## How drugs act: agonist, antagonist, reuptake blocker

Almost every psychotropic works by nudging one of the steps above, and there are only a few ways to do it. This vocabulary carries the whole medication page:

- **Agonist** — *mimics or boosts* a neurotransmitter's action (turns the system **up**). E.g., a direct dopamine agonist in Parkinson's.
- **Antagonist** — *blocks* a receptor so the neurotransmitter can't act (turns the system **down**). E.g., **antipsychotics are dopamine (D2) antagonists** [S10].
- **Reuptake inhibitor** — *blocks the recycling pump*, so the transmitter lingers in the cleft and keeps acting (an **indirect** way to turn the system up). E.g., **SSRIs block serotonin reuptake**; **SNRIs** block serotonin *and* norepinephrine reuptake [S8].
- **Positive allosteric modulator** — binds a *different* site and makes the receptor **more responsive** to the real transmitter (doesn't act on its own). E.g., **benzodiazepines** make GABA-A receptors more responsive to GABA [S9].

Notice these are *mechanisms*, not outcomes: "turns the serotonin system up" is not the same as "fixes depression." Which brings us to the honest caveat.

## The honest caveat: the immediate-effect / delayed-benefit gap

Here is a fact that quietly undermines the simple "low chemical, top it up" story. An **SSRI blocks serotonin reuptake within hours** — synaptic serotonin rises almost immediately. But the **antidepressant effect takes 2–6 weeks** [S6][S8]. If depression were simply low serotonin and the drug simply raised it, the mood should lift in a day. The lag is the tell: the *benefit* appears to come not from the raised chemical itself but from **slow, downstream adaptations** — receptor changes, increased **BDNF** (a growth factor), and **synaptic plasticity/neurogenesis** [S8]. In other words, the medication may work by the *same* route therapy does — by changing the brain's wiring over weeks ([[concept-neuroplasticity-therapy]]) — not by refilling a tank. This is the neurochemical seed of the "chemical imbalance is a myth, but medication can still help" position developed in [[concept-psychotropic-classes]].
