# The Intake / Clinical Interview

The intake is the **first full session with nearly every client**, and it does two jobs at once that beginners think are separate: it **gathers the information** you'll build everything on, and it **begins the relationship** that makes the information trustworthy. Sommers-Flanagan's frame is that the clinical interview is the **foundational assessment method** — not one tool among many but the one all the others hang from — and that rapport and data-collection are **not a trade-off**: a client who feels judged, rushed, or processed hands you a thinner, more defended history [S7]. The interview *is* an intervention. Being carefully heard is the first dose of the common factors from [[unit01-helping-skills]].

## How structured? A dial, not a switch

Interviews range along a spectrum [S7]:

- **Unstructured** — you follow the client, few fixed questions. Maximal rapport and richness; weak coverage (you may miss a whole domain) and poor reliability (two clinicians get different pictures).
- **Structured** — a fixed script (research and some diagnostic instruments work this way). Maximal coverage and reliability; can feel like an interrogation and starve the alliance.
- **Semi-structured** — a checklist of domains you *will* cover, asked conversationally and in whatever order the client's story opens them. This is where most skilled clinical intakes live: the structure lives in your head, not on the page between you.

The skill is holding a mental map of what must be covered while staying responsive to the person — structure serving the relationship, not overriding it.

## What an intake covers: three areas, one history

Sommers-Flanagan collapses the intake into **three core areas** [S7]:

1. **The presenting problem** — what brings them in, *in their words*, and its story.
2. **The biopsychosocial history** — the context the problem grew in.
3. **Current functioning** — how they're doing right now, including the [[concept-mental-status-exam | mental status exam]] and a risk screen.

### 1. The presenting problem

Start here, and start in the client's language, not the diagnostic translation. Useful structure without leading:

- **What** is the problem (their description, then a specific recent example)?
- **When** did it start, and **why now** — what changed that made today the day they called? (This is your first read on **precipitating** factors — see [[concept-case-conceptualization]].)
- **Course** — getting better, worse, or cycling? Frequency, intensity, duration.
- **Impact** — what can't they do now that they could before? (The **impairment** that Unit 6's definition of "disorder" hinges on, and the thing insurers reimburse — [[concept-treatment-planning]].)
- **What have they tried**, and what happened?
- **Why help-seeking now** may itself be culturally loaded — for some clients "telling a stranger your feelings" is foreign or shameful ([[concept-acculturation]]); the *fact* of arriving can be data.

### 2. The biopsychosocial history

The organizing frame is [[concept-biopsychosocial-model]] from Unit 5 — the three domains keep you from tunneling on symptoms:

- **Biological** — medical history and current conditions, medications, substance use (ask *everyone*, matter-of-factly — substances mimic and co-travel with nearly everything; [[concept-substance-use-disorders]]), sleep/appetite, family history of mental illness, pregnancy/ birth/developmental history where relevant.
- **Psychological** — prior mental-health treatment and diagnoses, trauma history (screen, **don't push for the narrative** — [[concept-trauma-stressor-disorders]]), coping style, prior episodes, attachment history ([[concept-attachment-styles]]), strengths.
- **Social** — family and relationships, work/school, housing and finances, culture/religion/identity, legal issues, supports and community. This is also where you catch the **protective factors** a strengths-based plan is built on.

The old DSM-IV multiaxial system *forced* this context onto every diagnosis; DSM-5 dropped the scaffold ([[concept-dsm-structure]]), so gathering context is now a **habit you supply**, not a form that prompts you.

### 3. Current functioning (and the risk screen)

- The **mental status exam** — a structured description of present state ([[concept-mental-status-exam]]).
- **Screening measures** where indicated — a PHQ-9, a GAD-7 ([[concept-screening-tools]]) — to baseline severity and enable [[concept-treatment-planning | measurement-based care]].
- **A risk screen — every intake, non-negotiable.** Item 9 of the PHQ-9 (thoughts of death/self-harm) or a direct question. Asking about suicide **plainly and non-anxiously** does not plant the idea; freezing is not an option (full treatment in [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | Unit 8]]). This is also where [[unit03-ethics-law | the limits of confidentiality]] get spoken — informed consent and the confidentiality speech usually open the intake ([[concept-informed-consent]], [[concept-confidentiality-limits]]).

## Opening and closing the intake

- **Open** with informed consent and the confidentiality-and-its-limits speech — the client can't give you an honest history without knowing where the walls are ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]]).
- **Close** by summarizing what you heard (a microskill doing assessment work — see [[concept-microskills-hierarchy]]), offering an initial impression *tentatively*, checking it against the client's sense of fit, and agreeing on next steps. Ending with the client's own goals seeds [[concept-treatment-planning]].

## The stance traps

- **Interrogation drift.** Rapid-fire closed questions fill your form and empty the room. Lead with open questions and reflections; let the history come *through* the relationship.
- **"I need all the data."** The completeness reflex serves the counselor's anxiety. You do not need the trauma narrative to screen, to assess safety, or to start — pushing for it before trust and stability exist is how helpers re-traumatize ([[concept-trauma-stressor-disorders]]). Screening is asking plainly and accepting the answer.
- **Premature formulation.** Diagnosing in the first ten minutes and then interviewing to confirm it is confirmation bias with a notepad. Hold the impression as a **hypothesis to revisit** ([[concept-dsm-structure]]).
