# Freedom, Responsibility & Authenticity (the Second Given)

In everyday speech "freedom" sounds like good news. In existentialism it is the **hard** given. If there is no pre-set human essence, then we are the **authors** of our lives and **responsible** for them, and there is no solid ground under our choices that makes them objectively right. [S1][S4] Yalom's formulation is that freedom means **groundlessness**: no one else is responsible for the shape of my life, and that is frightening. [S10]

## The core vocabulary

**Responsibility (authorship).** To be responsible, in Yalom's sense, is to be the *author* of one's situation, feelings, and life direction, including in how one *responds* to what one did not choose. [S10] Clinically this is the move from "*it* happened to me" or "*he* makes me feel" toward "*I* am choosing this, and I could choose otherwise."

**Willing and deciding.** Responsibility is recognized; **willing** acts on it. Yalom describes wishing → willing → deciding → acting. Many clients are stuck at **wishing** (fantasy without commitment) or **avoid deciding**, because every decision gives up the alternatives. Decisions are painful because they **exclude**, and they confront us with our own authorship. [S10] Kierkegaard's **despair of possibility** is the same failure: lost among options, committing to none. [S2]

**Bad faith (Sartre).** Self-deception about freedom, in either of two directions: [S1][S4]
- **Collapsing into facticity:** "That's just who I am." "I have no choice." "I'm a waiter." The client treats a *role or history* as a fixed essence.
- **Collapsing into transcendence:** pretending one's past, body, commitments, or circumstances don't constrain one at all. "None of that matters, I can just start over."

**Authenticity.** Owning one's existence, including its limits, rather than living by default ("what one does," Heidegger's *das Man*). [S1][S8] It does **not** mean discovering a buried "true self" and expressing it. That is the pop version, and it is closer to [[theory-person-centered]]'s actualizing tendency than to existentialism. Existential authenticity is a *way of choosing*, not a hidden content. [S1][S8]

## Guilt: two kinds, and why the difference matters

| | **Neurotic guilt** | **Existential guilt** |
|---|---|---|
| About | imagined or trivial transgressions, often against parental or internalized rules | failing one's **own potential**; the unlived life; the gap between who one is and who one could be |
| Function | punishes; keeps the person small | **signals**: points toward what one is not yet living |
| Clinical response | examine, reduce, often treat as symptom | **listen to it** rather than relieve it; it can guide change |

May distinguished normal from neurotic guilt in the same way he distinguished normal from neurotic anxiety. [S12][S13] Heidegger's existential guilt adds that we are always "in debt" to possibilities we did not take. [S8] The clinical mistake is to reassure away *existential* guilt ("you shouldn't feel guilty") when the guilt is accurate information. A client who says "I've wasted ten years in a job I hate" may need that feeling heard, not dissolved.

## Freedom is always situated — the critique

The weakest form of existential therapy treats freedom as unlimited and places all responsibility on the individual. That form is **bad philosophy** as well as bad therapy:

- **Existentialism itself corrected it.** Later Sartre recast freedom as **freedom-in-situation**, constrained by history, scarcity, and social structures. [S1][S4] **Beauvoir** argued that **oppression** doesn't remove freedom but blocks its **exercise** by withholding the conditions it needs (security, education, leisure), and that affirming my freedom requires affirming others'. [S5] Facticity is *half* of existence, not an excuse to be seen through. [S1]
- **The multicultural critique.** Existentialism's emphasis on individual choice and self-authorship reflects **Western individualist** values. For clients whose identity is **familial or communal**, "what do *you* want, apart from your family?" can be alienating, or can read as disrespect for the clients' values. [S31] Critics inside the existential tradition argue for **adaptation**, not abandonment. [S31] See [[concept-cultural-humility]] and [[concept-privilege-power]] (Unit 4).

**How to apply.** Hold both halves. "You have some choice here" and "your situation really does constrain you" are *both* true. The work is to find the real zone of freedom **inside** the real constraints, without inflating it into blame or shrinking it into fatalism. With a client facing discrimination, the existential question is not "why do you *choose* to suffer?" but "given what is being done to you, how do you want to meet it, and who do you want to be in it?" This is close to Frankl's **attitudinal** meaning ([[theory-logotherapy]]).
