# Reporting, Forensic Care & Advocacy

A survivor who tells you will, sooner or later, face a set of decisions about systems: hospital or not, police or not, campus report or not, and — if they are a child, or if a child is currently at risk — a decision that is **not theirs or yours to make**. This page is the practical map. Getting it wrong is not a minor administrative error: Campbell's work found that system contact left many survivors feeling guilty, depressed, anxious, and unwilling to seek further help, with this **secondary victimization positively correlated with PTSD symptoms.** [S19] **Where you send someone is part of your clinical response.**

## The medical forensic exam

Often called a "rape kit," which is a misleading name — the kit is one component, and the exam has **two purposes at once**: medical care and evidence collection. [S27] Knowing what it involves lets you describe it accurately to someone deciding whether to go, which is frequently the most useful thing you can do in the first hours.

**What it is.** A **Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE)** is a nurse with specialized forensic training who conducts the exam. [S28] Alongside medical care — injury treatment, **STI prophylaxis, emergency contraception**, and follow-up — the exam may collect physical evidence, document injuries, and record the patient's account. Many jurisdictions use a **SART** (Sexual Assault Response Team) model in which the nurse examiner, a community advocate, and law enforcement operate as a coordinated team, which exists specifically to reduce the secondary victimization in [S19]. [S28]

**The two facts that most change a survivor's decision:**

1. **Having the exam does not obligate anyone to report or prosecute.** In most jurisdictions a kit can be collected and stored while the person decides later, and anonymous or non-investigative options exist in many places. Survivors routinely believe the exam *is* reporting, decline it on that basis, and lose the option irreversibly.
2. **The exam is survivor-directed.** DOJ's national protocol is explicit that care is survivor-centered: **the patient may decline any part of the exam** — any step, any sample, the photographs — **and still receive medical care.** [S27] Saying this out loud restores exactly the control that [[concept-disclosure-response]] identifies as the active ingredient.

**Timing.** Evidence is generally collectable **up to about 120 hours (5 days)** after an assault. [S27] Treat this as a rule of thumb, not a deadline to quote: **windows, payment rules, and age-related requirements are set by jurisdiction and have widened in many states.** The right sentence is "there is a time window and it may be longer than you think — let's find out what it is here," followed by a call to the local rape crisis center or hotline. Medical care itself has no deadline.

**Practical note worth carrying:** encourage nothing, but if someone asks, evidence is better preserved if they have not showered, changed, or laundered clothes — and if they have, that does not make the exam pointless. Never let this become pressure. A person who wants a shower more than they want a kit is making a legitimate choice about their own body, which is the whole point.

## Reporting pathways — plural

"Reporting" is not one decision, and treating it as a yes/no question is itself a small coercion.

**Civilian / law enforcement.** Filing a police report initiates a criminal process. It is the survivor's choice for adults. Most sexual assaults are still not reported — BJS estimated **46% of rape/sexual assaults reported to police in 2023 (±8.2%)**, the highest share since 2010, meaning roughly half are not [S4] — and **non-reporting is a normal and frequently rational decision**, not evidence of a weak case or a false allegation. Pressure to report is specifically identified as retraumatizing. [S17]

**Military — the two-track system**, and the cleanest illustration that reporting is not binary. DoD's SAPR program offers: [S29]

| | **Restricted Reporting** | **Unrestricted Reporting** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Confidentiality** | Confidential — no investigation, no command notification | Not confidential |
| **What it triggers** | Access to medical care, advocacy, and counseling | Law-enforcement investigation and command notification |
| **Who you may tell** | **Only** a SARC, SAPR Victim Advocate, or healthcare provider | Anyone, including chain of command |

Why this matters even if you never work with service members: **who you tell determines what happens next**, and telling the wrong person forecloses the confidential option permanently. A service member who discloses to their commander cannot then file restricted. If a client is or was in the military, this is worth knowing before they start talking.

**Veterans — MST.** VA uses **Military Sexual Trauma (MST)** for sexual assault or harassment experienced during military service, and the access rules are unusually generous and unusually little-known: MST-related care is **free**, available to **any veteran or service member regardless of era, discharge status, or VA enrollment**, and **requires no prior report and no documentation** that it occurred. [S31] This is a concrete, checkable referral fact that changes lives, and most counselors do not know it.

**Campus — Title IX.** Handle with care, because the regulations are in flux. The **2024 Title IX Final Rule was vacated nationwide on January 9, 2025** by the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky, and the Department of Education reverted to enforcing the **2020 regulations**. [S30] **Do not memorize current requirements; they will change again.**

What *is* durable is the structure, and it is the part that matters clinically: on a campus, employees fall into categories — some are **confidential** (typically counseling center staff, health services, and designated advocates) and some are **mandated to report to the Title IX Coordinator** when they learn of sex discrimination or harassment. **Which one you are determines what you can honestly promise a student before they begin talking.** The professional obligation that survives every regulatory change: **know your own status, and say it up front** — before the disclosure, not after. A student who tells a "responsible employee" believing it was private, and then finds an investigation underway they did not choose, has experienced exactly the loss of control this module is organized around. That is a confidentiality-limits problem in the sense of Unit 3's [[concept-informed-consent]] and [[concept-confidentiality-limits]], and the fix is the same: front-load it.

## Mandated reporting: the hard cases

The general duty lives in Unit 3's [[concept-mandated-reporting]] — the standard is **reasonable suspicion, not proof**, and reporting is not optional once it is met. Three sexual-abuse-specific collisions:

**1. A child currently being abused.** Report. This is the clear case. You are not required to be sure, you are not required to investigate, and — see [[concept-child-sexual-abuse]] — you **must not** develop the account before reporting, because untrained questioning contaminates the forensic interview. [S13] Report on what you have, in the child's words.

**2. An adult client discloses childhood sexual abuse.** This is the case most counselors get wrong in both directions. **Generally, historical abuse of a now-adult client is not itself reportable** — the mandate is triggered by a child currently at risk. **But** it becomes reportable if there is reason to believe **the perpetrator currently has access to children** — still teaching, coaching, living with grandchildren, working in a youth setting. That converts a historical disclosure into a current-risk question. **State statutes vary substantially on this point**, and it is a consult-and-document situation, not a judgment call to make alone: check your state's statute, consult a supervisor, and document the reasoning. Some states impose broader duties than the general rule above.

The clinical problem is real and should not be minimized: an adult survivor who has taken years to tell anyone may experience a report as a second loss of control, and it can end the therapy. That does not relieve the duty where it applies. What it does require is **candor in advance** — the confidentiality limits conversation at intake is what prevents this from being an ambush — and, where a report must be made, **telling the client you are making it, and why**, rather than doing it behind them. See [[concept-confidentiality-limits]] and [[study-tarasoff]] for the analogous reasoning about third-party risk.

**3. Vulnerable adults.** Most states extend mandated reporting to sexual abuse of elders and adults with disabilities. Populations with disabilities are at substantially elevated risk and are among the least likely to be believed.

## Advocates — the referral you should know by name

A **rape crisis advocate** is not a therapist, and the difference is worth understanding rather than blurring. Advocates accompany survivors to the hospital and to police interviews, explain what each system will do next, help with practical needs, and — in many states, under specific privilege statutes — hold a **confidentiality privilege distinct from and sometimes broader than a counselor's**. Local rape crisis centers provide this free.

Two reasons this is the highest-value referral in the module. First, it is the direct counter to secondary victimization: an advocate's function is to make the systems less harmful to traverse, which is the problem Campbell documented. [S19] Second, it is **available immediately, at any hour, without an intake, an assessment, or a waitlist** — unlike you. The **National Sexual Assault Hotline (800-656-HOPE)** routes to local centers. [S16]

**Find your local center's number before you need it.** Looking it up while someone sits in your office is a worse experience than having it written down.
