# Treating Sexual Trauma: Phases, Protocols & the Memory Wars

Unit 8's [[concept-trauma-informed-care]] draws the line this page stands on: **trauma-*informed* care** is a universal stance every counselor adopts, and **trauma *treatment*** is a specific trained modality. That distinction is not repeated here. What this page adds is which treatments were built for *this* trauma, how strong the evidence is, what you do in the long stretch before a client is ready for any of them — and the one category of technique that has done documented, career-ending harm.

## Phase-based treatment: the organizing frame

Judith Herman's three stages of recovery are the standard map, and they are useful precisely because they tell you what *not* to do first: [S35]

1. **Safety and stabilization** — establishing safety in the body, in relationships, and in the person's life; building emotion-regulation capacity and resources.
2. **Remembrance and mourning** — processing the trauma: putting words and feeling to it, and grieving what it took.
3. **Reconnection and integration** — rebuilding a life and a self that the trauma is part of rather than the whole of.

**The stages are not linear.** Movement back and forth is expected and is not failure — a client who was doing Stage 2 work and needs to return to stabilization has not regressed, they have hit the normal shape of the work. [S35]

Two consequences worth internalizing. First, **the great majority of what a foundations-level counselor does is Stage 1** — safety, stabilization, alliance, practical support, and regulation skills. That is not a consolation prize; it is where most of the time goes even for trauma specialists, and it is what makes Stage 2 survivable. Second, **Stage 2 is the stage that requires training.** Going there without it is the classic beginner harm.

**STAIR** is phase-based treatment made explicit, and the best-worked example: **Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation**, developed by Cloitre for adult survivors of childhood abuse. **Eight sessions of skills work** in emotion regulation and interpersonal functioning come **before** any trauma-focused narrative work. [S34] The rationale is this module's thesis in miniature: chronic early interpersonal trauma disrupts affect regulation and relationships, so **capacity is built before the story is touched.**

## The protocols, and what the evidence says

The first-line trauma treatments named in the APA and VA/DoD guidelines are listed in [[concept-trauma-informed-care]]. Here is what is specifically known about them for sexual trauma.

| Treatment | What it does | Sexual-trauma specifics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CPT** (Cognitive Processing Therapy) | Cognitive; targets "stuck points" — distorted beliefs about the trauma, especially about **blame, safety, trust, power, esteem, and intimacy** | **Developed by Resick specifically for sexual assault survivors.** The blame-focused cognitive work maps directly onto the self-blame in [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]] [S32] |
| **PE** (Prolonged Exposure) | Behavioral; repeated imaginal and in-vivo exposure to trauma memories and avoided situations | Equally supported for this population [S32] |
| **TF-CBT** | Trauma-focused CBT for children and adolescents, with a caregiver component | **The standard for children**, including child sexual abuse |
| **EMDR** | Exposure plus bilateral stimulation | Works as a package; **whether the eye movements add anything beyond the exposure component remains debated** — the mechanism is contested even where the outcome is not (noted in [[concept-trauma-informed-care]]) |
| **STAIR** | Phase-based skills training, then narrative work | Built for adults with PTSD from **childhood** abuse [S34] |

**The landmark trial.** Resick and colleagues randomized female rape victims with chronic PTSD to CPT, PE, or a wait-list. Both active treatments produced large reductions in PTSD versus wait-list, and **CPT and PE did not differ significantly from each other.** [S32] **Durability:** the 5–10 year follow-up located 144 participants and reassessed 87.5% of them, finding treatment gains **maintained over the long term.** [S33]

Two things to take from this. **Clinically:** you can tell a survivor, accurately, that there are treatments that work for this and that the gains hold for years. That is a true sentence, it is not a platitude, and hope grounded in evidence is a legitimate intervention. **Methodologically:** when two treatments tie, the honest conclusion is *offer a genuine choice*, not *pick a winner*. Some survivors want to work on the beliefs (CPT); some want to stop avoiding (PE). For a population whose defining injury was the removal of choice, offering the choice is not a neutral procedural detail.

## Your job before the referral

The material in [[concept-trauma-informed-care]] says do not dig. The obvious question is what you do instead, and the answer is more substantial than it sounds:

- **Stage 1 work.** Safety, stabilization, regulation skills, sleep, practical problems, reducing isolation.
- **The alliance itself.** For someone harmed inside a relationship by someone they trusted, a reliable, boundaried, non-exploitative relationship is not preparation for the treatment — it is a substantial part of it. See [[concept-therapeutic-alliance]].
- **Psychoeducation.** Naming tonic immobility, normalizing delayed disclosure, explaining that self-blame is near-universal. This is frequently the highest-yield thing you will do, and it requires no trauma-treatment training. [S21]
- **Risk assessment.** Elevated suicide risk means [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]] and [[concept-safety-planning]] are part of the work, not an optional add-on. [S3]
- **Referral** — clinical (a trained trauma clinician) and practical ([[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]]).
- **Letting them not talk about it.** A client can do useful work on sleep, relationships, and functioning without ever narrating the assault. That is a legitimate course of therapy, not an avoidance to be overcome.

## The memory wars, and the rule that came out of them

This is the part of the material where a well-meaning counselor can destroy a family and end their own career, and it deserves to be learned precisely.

**What is disputed:** whether memories of real abuse can become inaccessible and later be recovered. Serious researchers hold opposing positions, and the dispute is live in the peer-reviewed literature. [S36] Do not claim it is settled in either direction.

**What is not disputed, by either camp:** **suggestive techniques can generate confident, detailed, entirely false memories.** [S36] The techniques concerned:

- **Hypnosis** for memory retrieval — it does not improve recall and it **increases** false recollection, while raising confidence in it.
- **Guided imagery** and visualization of "what might have happened."
- **Age regression.**
- **Repeated suggestive questioning**, including "you can tell me, it's safe here" applied to a memory that has not been reported.
- **Symptom-checklist reasoning** — concluding from a list of present-day symptoms that abuse "must have" occurred, and then working to retrieve it.

The historical record is not ambiguous about consequences: suggestive practice produced **malpractice liability and de-licensure** for the clinicians involved, and devastated families on the basis of events that did not occur. [S36]

**The practice rule, which follows regardless of who is right about repression:** **never work to recover a memory.** If a client spontaneously reports a memory they did not previously have, your job is to **stay neutral about its historical status while taking the distress entirely seriously** — those are compatible, and holding both is the skill. You do not confirm it, you do not challenge it, you do not help elaborate it, and you do not build a treatment plan around establishing whether it happened. Note the resonance with the "believe, don't investigate" rule in [[concept-disclosure-response]]: in both cases you are declining a forensic role you are not qualified for, and in both cases the client's distress is real either way.

**Connected trap:** the same reasoning applies to the fragmentation debate in [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]]. A counselor who believes trauma memories are *necessarily* fragmented may read a coherent account as fabricated; one who believes they are necessarily coherent may read gaps the same way. Both are adjudicating, and neither is qualified to.
