# The ACE Study (Felitti & Anda, 1998)

The study that made childhood trauma a public-health problem rather than a private misfortune, and the single most-cited piece of research behind the trauma-informed movement. It is genuinely important. It is also the most frequently misused study in the helping professions, and the misuse is instructive enough to be worth as much of your attention as the finding.

## The design

Felitti, Anda and colleagues surveyed **over 17,000 Kaiser Permanente HMO members** in Southern California, in two waves between **1995 and 1997**, linking retrospective self-report of childhood adversity to adult health status and behavior. [S7][S8] Published in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine* in 1998. [S7]

The **ten ACE categories**, in three groups: [S8]

| Abuse | Household challenges | Neglect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotional abuse | Mother treated violently | Emotional neglect |
| Physical abuse | Household substance abuse | Physical neglect |
| Sexual abuse | Household mental illness | |
| | Parental separation or divorce | |
| | Incarcerated household member | |

The **ACE score** is simply the count of categories endorsed, 0–10. Note what that construction does and does not do — it is an **unweighted count of categories**, so a single category endured daily for a decade scores 1, and five categories each occurring once score 5. This is the seed of nearly every problem below.

## The finding

**Adversity was common.** Nearly two-thirds reported at least one ACE; more than a fifth reported three or more. The distribution: **36.1% zero, 26% one, 15.9% two, 9.5% three, and 12.5% four or more.** [S8] In an insured, employed, largely middle-class Californian population — which is part of what made it striking.

**Adversity was graded.** The landmark result is the **dose-response relationship**: as ACE count rises, so does risk across an extraordinary range of adult outcomes — smoking, alcoholism, drug use, depression, suicide attempts, sexually transmitted infections, heart disease, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, skeletal fractures. [S7] People with four or more ACEs showed multiples of the risk seen at zero.

**Why it mattered.** Not any single association, but the *shape*: a monotonic gradient across many unrelated outcomes, in a large ordinary population, is hard to explain away and points at a common upstream cause. It reframed childhood adversity as a population-level determinant of physical health — not only mental health — and that reframing is what put trauma on public-health agendas. The dose-response curve is the finding; everything else is downstream.

## The critiques, and why they matter more than the usual caveats

The standard limitations apply — **retrospective self-report** of childhood decades later, **cross-sectional** design so causation is inferred rather than demonstrated, and a **non-representative cohort** (insured Kaiser members in one region, largely white and middle-class, which limits generalization in both directions). Those are real and ordinary.

The critiques that actually change practice are these:

**1. The score discards the information that matters clinically.** Frequency, severity, duration, and **developmental timing** of exposure are all invisible to a category count. [S11] Two people with a score of 4 may have had nothing in common. A person scoring 1 may have suffered chronic, intense abuse; a person scoring 4 may have had brief low-level exposure to four things. The score cannot tell them apart, which is fine for describing populations and useless for understanding an individual.

**2. It omits whole categories of adversity.** NCTSN's list of what the ACE questionnaire misses is not marginal: **traumatic bereavement, medical trauma, natural disasters, racial trauma, community violence** — among others. [S11] A child in a violent neighbourhood, or carrying [[concept-historical-trauma | racial trauma]], can score zero. The instrument was built around *household* adversity and inherits that boundary.

**3. It does not predict individuals.** This is the empirical finding that should settle the clinical question: ACE scores are associated with **mean group differences** in health outcomes but have **low accuracy in identifying which individuals** will go on to have poor health. [S10] A population-level risk gradient and an individual-level predictor are different objects, and the ACE score is only the first. — see [[concept-trauma-screening]].

**4. It is read deterministically, and that does harm.** Critics argue the framing **oversimplifies complex lives, creates deterministic narratives about children's futures, pathologizes poverty and structural disadvantage**, and embeds a deficit-based stance toward families. [S13] A high ACE score describes elevated risk in a population — it is not a prognosis, and children have been told otherwise. The gradient also correlates heavily with material deprivation, which raises the question of how much is "childhood trauma" versus poverty measured indirectly.

## What Anda himself said

The decisive point, and the one to remember: **the study's own principal investigator publicly objected to how his instrument was being used.** Robert Anda wrote that the ACE questionnaire was designed to **research — not screen** — the relationship between childhood adversity and later outcomes, and that the ACE score **"is neither a diagnostic tool nor is it predictive at the individual level."** [S9]

It is rare and clarifying for an author to disown a use of their own work this directly. When you meet an agency, a school district, or a state initiative treating the ACE score as a clinical screening instrument, the objection is not a contrarian reading of the literature — it is the position of the person who built it, corroborated by subsequent psychometric work [S10] and by a medical specialty society's formal position statement locating ACEs in **population-level** prevention. [S12]

## What to carry

- **Cite it for the population claim it supports:** childhood adversity is common, graded, and predicts a broad range of adult health outcomes at population scale. That is a major, well-replicated contribution.
- **Do not cite it as an individual risk calculator.** It does not classify individuals well [S10] and was not built to [S9].
- **Do not treat a score as a history.** If you want to know what happened to someone and what it meant, that is assessment ([[concept-intake-interview]]), conducted in a relationship, over time — not a ten-item count.
- **Notice the general lesson**, which is a [[unit12-research | Unit 12]] lesson: a robust population-level association can have poor individual predictive accuracy, and conflating the two is one of the most consequential errors in applied research. Compare [[concept-reliability-validity]] on why an instrument's validity is relative to a stated purpose — the ACE score is not an invalid instrument, it is a valid instrument being used for a purpose it was never validated for.
