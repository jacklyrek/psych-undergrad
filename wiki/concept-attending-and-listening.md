# Attending & Active Listening

Before you can reflect, question, or empathize, you have to **attend** — physically and mentally show the client that you are present and following. Attending is the foundation skill at the base of Ivey's [[concept-microskills-hierarchy | microskills hierarchy]]: clients talk more, and disclose more, when they feel attended to. [S5] This page collects the sub-skills that make up good attending and active listening: SOLER, the "three Vs + B," the use of silence, and observation.

## SOLER: attending as observable behavior
Gerard Egan, in *The Skilled Helper* (1975), turned the vague instruction "be present" into five coachable nonverbal behaviors — the acronym **SOLER**: [S14]

- **S — Squarely.** Face the client, adopting a posture that says "I'm with you." (Egan suggests a slight angle rather than dead-on can feel less confrontational.)
- **O — Open posture.** Uncrossed arms and legs; an open stance signals you're not defended or closed off.
- **L — Lean.** Lean slightly *toward* the client to convey engagement (leaning back reads as disengaged).
- **E — Eye contact.** Maintain eye contact **without staring** — steady, natural, not fixed.
- **R — Relax.** Be relaxed and natural; your calm helps the client settle. A tense helper makes a tense room.

SOLER is a *starting scaffold*, not a straitjacket — the point is the message it sends (presence, safety), not rigid compliance.

## Ivey's "three Vs + B"
Ivey's version of attending behavior has four channels you can watch and adjust in yourself: [S5]

- **Visual** — culturally-appropriate eye contact.
- **Vocal** — tone, pace, and volume (your *voice* carries as much warmth as your words).
- **Verbal tracking** — staying on *the client's* topic rather than steering to your own.
- **Body language** — attentive, congruent posture and expression.

Verbal tracking is the one beginners most often break: the urge to redirect to what *you* find interesting (often the "problem to fix") pulls the session off the client's thread.

## Active listening and minimal encouragers
Active listening is attending *plus* signaling, in small ways, that you're following — **minimal encouragers** ("mm-hm," "go on," a nod, repeating a key word), well-timed [[concept-microskills-hierarchy | paraphrases and reflections of feeling]], and the disciplined use of silence. The aim is to keep the client's own exploration going, not to insert yourself.

## The use of silence
Silence is a skill, not an accident — and beginners reliably rush to fill it. Held well, silence gives the client room to **access a feeling, reach for a word, or think a thought through** rather than being interrupted. Research distinguishes **productive** silence (space to feel, express, or reflect) from **neutral** and **obstructive** silence, and finds silence is most useful *after* a solid [[concept-therapeutic-alliance | alliance]] and when a client is processing intense emotion. [S17] The practical rule: when you feel the urge to speak into a client's pause, count — often the most useful thing you can do is nothing, and let the client break the silence.

## Observation skills
Attending isn't only *sending* presence; it's *receiving* data. Observation means noticing **how** the client communicates — especially **mismatches between words and nonverbals** (saying "I'm fine" while their eyes fill, or "it was nothing" with clenched fists). Those incongruences are often the most important thing in the room, and naming one gently ("you say it's fine, but I noticed your voice caught there") can open a session up. This is also how you catch [[concept-congruence | your own]] incongruence and the client's. [S5]

## Attending is not culturally universal (important)
SOLER and the "three Vs" encode **dominant US-white norms.** Direct, sustained eye contact reads as respectful attention in some cultures and as **rude, intrusive, or aggressive** in others; comfortable interpersonal distance and the meaning of touch and silence also vary. Applying SOLER rigidly can therefore *misfire* — reading a client's culturally-appropriate downcast gaze as evasiveness, for instance. Attending well means calibrating these behaviors to the person in front of you, which is exactly where Unit 4's material picks up. [S14] See [[unit04-multicultural]] and [[concept-cultural-humility]].

## Why it matters in the room
Attending is the least glamorous skill and the one everything else rests on: reflections land only if you actually heard, empathy is impossible without observation, and a client who doesn't feel attended to won't give you anything to work with. It's also the skill most directly opposed to the fix-it reflex — you can't attend and rehearse your solution at the same time.
