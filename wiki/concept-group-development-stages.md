# Stages of Group Development

A group is not a fixed thing you lead the same way every week — it is a **developing organism** with predictable phases, and the single most common beginner mistake is to treat every session as if the group were at the same maturity. Conflict in week three is not a sign the group is failing; it is usually a sign the group is **on schedule**. Knowing the stages lets you read what a group needs *right now* and, crucially, stops you from mislabeling normal developmental turbulence as a problem to be smoothed over. Two frameworks matter: **Tuckman's** generic sequence and **Corey's** counseling-specific stages. They describe the same arc.

## Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing (+ adjourning)

Bruce **Tuckman (1965)** reviewed the small-group literature and named a four-stage sequence that has proven remarkably durable; **Tuckman & Jensen (1977)** added a fifth [S7]:

1. **Forming** — orientation. Members are anxious, polite, and dependent on the leader. They test the water, look for acceptance, and want to know the rules and whether they're safe. Little real work happens; the task is **inclusion and safety**.
2. **Storming** — conflict. As comfort grows, differences surface: members jockey for position, challenge one another and often the **leader's authority**, and the group feels tense or unsafe. This is the hardest stage for a beginner to tolerate — and the most important *not* to suppress. Conflict, handled well, is how the group earns real trust.
3. **Norming** — cohesion. The group works through its conflicts and settles into shared norms and roles. Trust and belonging rise; members start to feel like an "us." Cohesiveness ([[theory-yalom-factors]]) consolidates here.
4. **Performing** — the working stage. The group functions as a productive unit: members take risks, give and receive honest feedback, and use one another. This is where the therapeutic factors do their heaviest lifting.
5. **Adjourning** (added 1977) — termination/mourning. The group ends; members process loss, consolidate gains, and say goodbye. Skipping this stage wastes the ending's therapeutic potential.

## Corey: the counseling-specific version

Counselor-education texts (Corey) reframe the same arc for therapy and counseling groups, adding a stage *before* the group even meets [S8][S9]:

- **Pre-group (formation).** Before session one: **screening, selection, preparation, and informed consent** ([[concept-group-ethics-and-formation]]). Yalom's research is emphatic that this stage — often skipped by beginners — strongly predicts who stays and who drops out. The group's fate is partly decided before it convenes.
- **Initial stage** — orientation and exploration (Tuckman's *forming*). Establishing trust, goals, and norms; addressing confidentiality *immediately*. Members are tentative and watching the leader.
- **Transition stage** — the counseling name for **storming**. Anxiety, defensiveness, resistance, and the **struggle for control** come to the surface; members may challenge the leader or each other. The leader's task is to **help the group work through conflict, not around it** — to model that the group can survive disagreement. Mishandled, this stage stalls the group; handled well, it produces the trust the working stage runs on.
- **Working stage** — cohesion and productivity (Tuckman's *norming*+*performing* fused). Trust is at its highest; members take real risks, address difficult material, and hold each other accountable in a way that feels supportive rather than judgmental.
- **Final stage** — consolidation and termination. Reviewing progress, saying what needs saying, and planning how to **transfer the learning** to life outside the group.

| Tuckman | Corey (counseling) | The group's core task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| — | Pre-group | screen, prepare, consent |
| Forming | Initial | trust, safety, norms, goals |
| **Storming** | **Transition** | **survive conflict; earn real trust** |
| Norming | Working (early) | cohesion consolidates |
| Performing | Working | risk-taking, feedback, the real work |
| Adjourning | Final | consolidate gains, grieve, transfer learning |

## The leader's job changes by stage

The reason stages matter practically: **what a good leader does is stage-dependent** ([[concept-group-leadership]]).

- **Initial/forming** — high **executive function** and structure: set norms, protect members, reduce anxiety, model inclusion. The group can't self-regulate yet.
- **Transition/storming** — tolerate and *contain* conflict without fleeing it. The instinct to rescue the group from its own tension (or to side with whoever challenges you) is the trap; the move is to make the conflict examinable and safe. **Resisting the urge to smooth it over is the stance skill of this stage.**
- **Working/performing** — step back. Let members do the therapeutic work on each other; the leader's over-involvement here *starves* the group. This is the group version of the whole curriculum's "resist the urge to fix": as the group matures, the leader deliberately **decenters**.
- **Final/adjourning** — surface the ending, help members grieve it, and consolidate what they'll take with them.

## What's contested (don't over-apply the ladder)

- **Stages are a heuristic, not a law.** Real groups don't march cleanly forward; they **recycle** — a new member, a crisis, or a rupture can throw a "working" group back into storming, and open-ended groups (members joining and leaving continuously) never sit still in one stage at all [S7][S8]. Use the model to *read* a group, not to grade it against a timetable.
- **Tuckman's evidence is mostly non-clinical.** The 1965 model was a literature review across many group types (including training and work groups), not a study of therapy groups. It transfers well as a lens; it is not a validated clinical mechanism.
- **"Storming = failure" is the costly misread.** The most damaging error the model *prevents* is a beginner reading normal transition-stage conflict as the group breaking down and reflexively suppressing it — which robs the group of the trust that only comes from surviving conflict together.
