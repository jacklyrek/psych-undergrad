# The Lifeworld (*Lebenswelt*)

**One line:** the **lifeworld** is the pre-scientific, taken-for-granted world of everyday experience, shared with others and shaped by culture and history. Every science, including psychology, starts from it and abstracts away from it. Husserl warned that science goes wrong when it forgets this and mistakes its abstractions for the whole of reality. [S1][S2]

## The idea

In his last work, *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology* (1936), Husserl argued that modern science had fallen into a **crisis of meaning**. [S2]

- Galileo's great move was to **mathematize nature**: to treat the real world as the world of measurable quantities. Colors, warmth, and meaning became "merely subjective."
- That abstraction is enormously powerful. But science then forgets that it **was** an abstraction, drawn out of a lived world of meaning in which scientists themselves live, perceive, argue, and care. [S2]
- The result: a science that can measure everything about human beings except what their lives *mean* to them. [S2]

Husserl's corrective was not anti-science. It was to remember that **the lifeworld is the ground science grows from**, and that questions about meaning and value have their home there. [S2]

The SEP's definition of the lifeworld in the broader phenomenological tradition: the everyday **social and cultural** context of experience, including language, habits, and collective practices. [S1]

## Why it matters for counseling

1. **Evidence and lived experience are different kinds of knowledge, not rivals.** A trial showing that a treatment reduces PHQ-9 scores by X points is lifeworld-abstracted knowledge: indispensable, but it does not tell you what depression is like for *this* client or what recovery would *mean* to them. Evidence-based practice explicitly includes client values and preferences for this reason ([[concept-evidence-based-practice]]). The lifeworld concept tells you *why* that third leg can't be collapsed into the first. (Interpretive bridge; not a claim from the EBP literature itself.)
2. **Culture lives in the lifeworld.** What counts as obvious, polite, shameful, or normal is lifeworld-structured and mostly invisible *to the person inside it*. That includes you. This is the phenomenological root of the cultural-humility stance: your taken-for-granted is not the client's ([[concept-cultural-humility]], [[unit04-multicultural]]).
3. **Some disorders disturb the lifeworld itself.** Blankenburg's patient Anne Rau said what she had lost was *"natural self-evidence"*: the taken-for-granted, shared common sense that lets everyone else just *get on with* ordinary life. Phenomenological psychiatry reads parts of schizophrenia as a disturbance of exactly this lifeworld anchoring. [S28] See [[theory-phenomenological-psychopathology]].

## Keep apart
| Concept | Whose? | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Lifeworld** (Husserl) | shared, intersubjective | the taken-for-granted background of meaning that science abstracts from |
| **Phenomenal field** (Rogers) | individual | everything *this* person experiences, the only reality that matters for understanding them ([[person-carl-rogers]]) [S37] |
| **Life space** (Lewin) | individual, at a moment | person plus psychological environment, the field that determines behavior now ([[concept-lewin-field-theory]]) [S10] |
| **Umwelt / Mitwelt / Eigenwelt** (Binswanger) | individual, structured | three regions of one's world: surroundings, others, self ([[theory-daseinsanalysis]]) [S15] |
