# Child Sexual Abuse: Grooming, Disclosure & the CSAAS Problem

Child sexual abuse is not adult sexual assault with a younger victim. The consent question is settled in advance by law, the perpetrator is almost always someone the child depends on, the abuse is usually a **process** rather than an event, and the disclosure — if it ever comes — behaves in ways that look, to an untrained adult, exactly like lying. This page is about that last part, because the gap between how disclosure actually works and how people expect it to work is where children lose their chance to be believed.

## Definition

CDC defines child sexual abuse as the involvement of a person **under 18** in sexual activity that they do not fully comprehend, cannot consent to, or are developmentally unprepared for. [S3] The clause counselors most often miss: **the definition does not require physical contact.** Exposure, being made to watch sexual acts or pornography, being photographed, and online sexual solicitation are child sexual abuse. A child who says "he never touched me" has not ruled anything out.

**Prevalence:** CDC's current estimate is **at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys** in the United States — figures the agency itself says "likely underestimate the true impact." [S3] You will also see **1 in 13 boys**, from 2013–2014 self-report data; the estimates for boys are especially unstable because they are especially sensitive to definition and to non-disclosure. [S3][S20] **About 90% of child sexual abuse is perpetrated by someone known and trusted by the child or the child's family.** [S3] That figure is the key to everything below.

## Grooming: abuse as a process

Grooming is the sequence by which access is obtained and silence is secured. The five-stage model from the research group that built the Sexual Grooming Scale: [S6]

1. **Victim selection** — targeting a child who is accessible and, frequently, isolated, needy, or poorly supervised.
2. **Gaining access and isolating** — engineering time alone, often through a role that legitimizes it (coach, tutor, relative, youth leader, family friend).
3. **Developing trust — with the child *and* with the protective adults.** This stage is the one people miss. The parents are groomed too: the perpetrator becomes the helpful, generous, reliable adult whom the family is grateful for, which is exactly what makes the later disclosure unbelievable and what makes the family's first instinct to defend him.
4. **Desensitization to touch and sexual content** — an escalating gradient from ordinary affection to sexualized contact, and from ordinary conversation to sexual material, each step small enough that no step is where the child could have objected.
5. **Post-abuse maintenance** — securing continued compliance and silence, through affection, gifts, shared-secret framing, blame ("you wanted it"), or threat (what will happen to the family, to the child, to the perpetrator).

**Empirically, grooming is near-universal:** in a survey of adult CSA survivors, **99% endorsed at least one grooming behavior**, averaging around **14** behaviors each. [S6]

Two clinical consequences. First, **the gradient is why "why didn't you stop it?" is an incoherent question** — there was no discrete moment presenting itself as the moment to refuse. Second, **grooming manufactures complicity**, which is the engine of the shame in [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]]: a child who accepted the gifts, kept the secret, or felt affection for the person often experiences themselves as a participant. They were not. They were engineered.

## How children actually disclose

The research finding that matters most: **disclosure is a process, not an event.** [S11] Systematic review of CSA disclosure research (33 studies, 2000–2016) finds that children commonly delay, disclose partially, test the water with an indirect or nonverbal hint first, and retreat if that hint lands badly. Barriers operate at every ecological level — individual (shame, self-blame, not having words), family (loyalty, dependence, fear of what telling would do to everyone), community (nobody to tell, no privacy), and cultural/societal (taboos around sex, honor and family reputation, disbelief of children). [S11][S39]

What this means in practice: **a delayed, fragmentary, inconsistent disclosure is the normal presentation, not the suspicious one.** The child who tells immediately, completely, and consistently is the exception. Adults who expect the exception read the norm as fabrication.

## CSAAS: the half that survived and the half that did not

Roland Summit's 1983 **Child Sexual Abuse Accommodation Syndrome** described five categories: **secrecy; helplessness; entrapment and accommodation; delayed, conflicted and unconvincing disclosure; and retraction.** [S7] It was enormously influential and was written for a good reason — to explain to disbelieving adults why abused children behave in ways that look like lying. Learn what it claims. Then learn, carefully, what happened to it.

**The half that survived.** **Secrecy and delayed, partial disclosure are well-supported** by independent empirical work. [S10][S11] When the New Jersey Supreme Court in *State v. J.L.G.* (2018) excluded CSAAS testimony generally, it **carved out delayed disclosure** as the one component with genuine scientific support. [S9]

**The half that did not.** **Routine recantation is not supported.** London, Bruck, Ceci and Shuman's review of the disclosure literature found that **most children who disclose in a competent interview do not later recant.** [S10] Recantation happens, and pressure from a non-believing family raises it, but it is not the expected course, and treating it as expected was the most damaging part of the model.

**The structural problem, which is worse than either half.** CSAAS is a **clinical description, not a validated diagnostic instrument**, and as applied it is **not falsifiable**: if a child discloses, that is consistent with abuse; if a child does not disclose, that is secrecy and accommodation; if a child recants, that is retraction. A framework in which every possible behavior confirms the hypothesis **cannot discriminate abused from non-abused children**, and therefore cannot be evidence that abuse occurred. [S8] This is the reason appellate courts have increasingly excluded it, and Summit himself objected to its use as proof. [S7][S9] State law varies; the direction of the case law is the finding, not a nationwide rule.

**The lesson to carry:** hold the **disclosure dynamics** (delay, partial telling, retreat after a bad reaction) and discard the **detector** (there is no behavioral syndrome that establishes abuse). The same logic disposes of the "warning signs checklist": no behavioral indicator or set of indicators is specific enough to diagnose CSA, and the plausible signs — sleep problems, withdrawal, regression, sexualized behavior, school decline — overlap with a dozen ordinary childhood stressors. Sexualized behavior well outside developmental norms is the closest to a specific indicator, and it is still not proof.

## You are not the forensic interviewer

This is the operational rule that follows from everything above, and it is the single most important thing on this page.

When a child discloses to you, **stop questioning and report.** A **forensic interview** at a Children's Advocacy Center is a single recorded conversation by a trained, neutral interviewer using a standardized protocol, structured specifically to gather the child's account **without shaping it**. [S13] The reason it must be protected: by the time a child reaches the CAC, they may have already recounted the allegation to as many as **six** untrained adults, each of whom likely asked leading or suggestive questions a trained interviewer is trained to avoid — and a defense can then argue, sometimes correctly, that the account was contaminated before anyone qualified heard it. [S13]

So your role at the moment of disclosure is narrow and non-negotiable: **receive it, do not develop it.** Let the child use their own words, do not supply names or details or ask "did he touch you *here*?", do not press for more, do not ask the child to repeat it for someone else, and write down what they said **in their words** as soon as you can. Then make the report — see [[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]] and [[concept-mandated-reporting]]. The instinct to gather enough information to be *sure* before reporting is both wrong about the standard (reporting requires **reasonable suspicion**, not proof) and actively destructive of the evidence.

Two more rules from NCTSN's caregiver guidance that apply to counselors equally: **never promise secrecy** — not before, not after, and especially not in response to "don't tell anyone" — and **stay calm**, because a child reads adult shock or anger as directed at *them* and will shut down. [S12]
