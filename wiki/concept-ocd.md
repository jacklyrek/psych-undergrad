# Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

DSM-5 moved OCD out of the anxiety chapter and gave it a family of its own — **obsessive-compulsive and related disorders** — on the grounds of shared phenomenology and neurobiology: repetitive, driven behavior locked to distressing internal experience. [S12] The move matters clinically: OCD is not "being very anxious," and treating it like generic anxiety misses its engine.

## The engine: obsession → anxiety → compulsion → relief → repeat

- **Obsessions** are "recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges, or images" that are **intrusive and unwanted** — contamination, harming someone, blasphemy, symmetry, doubt ("did I lock it?"). The person usually tries to suppress or neutralize them. [S12]
- **Compulsions** are "repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rules that must be applied rigidly" — washing, checking, ordering, counting, praying, mentally reviewing. Crucially, compulsions can be *invisible* (mental rituals). [S12]

The compulsion buys short-term relief and thereby *feeds* the obsession — the same avoidance-maintains-anxiety loop as [[concept-anxiety-disorders]], wound tighter. Diagnosis requires obsessions and/or compulsions that are **time-consuming (≥1 hour/day) or cause marked distress/impairment**, not better explained by a substance, medical condition, or another disorder. [S12]

**Ego-dystonic is the key word:** most people with OCD know the fear is irrational — only 2–4% lack insight — and are tormented *because* the thoughts feel alien to who they are. [S12] (DSM-5 grades this with insight specifiers: good/fair, poor, absent/delusional.) A gentle clinical implication: the client with intrusive harm-thoughts is horrified by them; the thought is not a plan, and treating it as a risk signal by reflex compounds their shame. (Genuine risk assessment still applies — [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | Unit 8]] — but *content of obsession ≠ intent*.)

## The relatives

Same chapter, same repetitive-behavior signature [S12]:

- **Body dysmorphic disorder** — obsessive preoccupation with a perceived appearance flaw, with mirror-checking/grooming/reassurance rituals. Distinguish from [[concept-eating-disorders]]: in BDD the concern is a *feature*; in anorexia it's weight/shape driving intake restriction.
- **Hoarding disorder** — persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value.
- **Trichotillomania** (hair-pulling) and **excoriation** (skin-picking) — body-focused repetitive behaviors.

## The discriminations that matter

- **OCD vs. GAD worry:** GAD worries are exaggerated versions of *real-life* concerns (money, health, work) experienced as plausible; obsessions are intrusive, often bizarre or repugnant, recognized as senseless, and answered with rituals. Worry drifts topic to topic; obsessions repeat with signature content. [S12][S7]
- **OCD vs. OCPD:** the classic confusable, and they are *different chapters entirely*. OCPD ([[concept-personality-disorders]], Cluster C) is "a chronic pattern of perfectionism and rigid control" that is **ego-syntonic** — the person finds their standards correct; it's everyone else who has a problem. No true obsessions or compulsions. OCD is episodic-symptomatic, ego-dystonic, and distressing to its owner. [S12][S11]
- **OCD vs. psychotic delusion:** the OCD client at the "absent insight" pole can approach delusional conviction, but the belief stays tethered to the obsessional theme and the ritual structure remains; see [[concept-psychotic-disorders]] for the delusion proper. [S12]
- **Obsession vs. trauma intrusion:** see [[concept-trauma-stressor-disorders]] — replays of a real event vs. feared possibilities.

## In the room

Reassurance is compulsion fuel. "Are you *sure* I didn't hit someone driving here?" answered with "I'm sure" relieves for minutes and strengthens the loop — family members are usually deep in this pattern by the time treatment starts. The evidence-based treatment (exposure and response prevention, a CBT specialty — Unit 9) works by breaking exactly that circuit, which is why a supportive-but-reassuring counselor can accidentally be part of the disorder. Warmth, yes; certificates of certainty, no.
