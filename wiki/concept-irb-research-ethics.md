# Research Ethics & the IRB — the Belmont Report

Every rule protecting research participants today was written in response to research that harmed people. That history is not incidental — it's the reason the ethics have the shape they do, and knowing it is what keeps "get IRB approval" from feeling like bureaucratic box-checking. This page is the research-world analog of Unit 3: the same duty of care you owe a **client** — honesty, consent, confidentiality, non-exploitation — owed instead to the person who agrees to be **studied**. For a counselor, it matters in two concrete ways: you may one day conduct or assist with research (a thesis, a program evaluation meant for publication, an agency study), and you constantly **read** research whose credibility depends on whether it was done ethically.

## The abuses that built the rules

The modern framework is a direct reaction to specific scandals [S19]:

- **Nazi medical experiments** in WWII concentration camps → the **Nuremberg Code (1947)**, whose first principle is that **"the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential."**
- The **Declaration of Helsinki (1964, WMA, revised since)** — the medical profession's own standards for research on human subjects, extending Nuremberg.
- The **Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972)** — the U.S. Public Health Service tracked hundreds of Black men with syphilis, **deceived them** about their condition, and **withheld penicillin** even after it became the standard cure, so as to study the untreated disease. Its exposure was the American scandal that forced reform: the **National Research Act (1974)**, the commission that wrote the Belmont Report, and the **IRB / Common Rule** system.

The pattern in every case: **vulnerable or captive people, no real consent, harm accepted in the name of knowledge.** The principles below are the point-by-point answer to that pattern.

## The Belmont Report: three principles, three applications

The **Belmont Report (1979)** is the foundational U.S. statement of research ethics. It names **three principles**, and — this is the part worth memorizing, because it's the clean discrimination the unit quizzes — each maps to a concrete **application** [S18]:

| Principle | What it means | Its application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Respect for persons** | People are **autonomous agents**; those with **diminished autonomy** (children, prisoners, cognitively impaired) get extra protection | **Informed consent** — voluntary, informed, with the right to decline or withdraw |
| **Beneficence** | **Maximize benefits, minimize harms** ("do no harm" + actively protect) | **Risk/benefit assessment** — the study's risks must be justified by its benefits |
| **Justice** | The **burdens and benefits** of research must be distributed **fairly** | **Fair subject selection** — don't load the risks onto the vulnerable/convenient, or reserve the benefits for the privileged |

Justice is the principle Tuskegee most flagrantly violated (poor Black men bore the entire burden for knowledge that would benefit others), and it's the one most easily forgotten, so it's worth anchoring: *who bears the risk, and who gets the benefit?*

## The IRB and the Common Rule

The **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** is the enforcement mechanism. Under the **Common Rule (45 CFR 46)**, research involving human subjects must be **reviewed and approved by an IRB before it begins** — a panel that checks the study against the Belmont principles: adequate informed consent, a defensible risk/benefit ratio, protections for vulnerable populations, and privacy/confidentiality safeguards [S18][S19]. Review is **prospective** (before data collection, not after — you can't retroactively make an unethical study ethical), and studies posing more than minimal risk get fuller scrutiny. The IRB is to a research participant what [[concept-informed-consent | informed consent]] and [[concept-confidentiality-limits | confidentiality protections]] are to a client — the structural guarantee that the power imbalance won't be exploited.

## ACA Section G: the counselor's research ethics

The **ACA Code of Ethics, Section G (Research and Publication)** is the counseling profession's own version, and it will read as familiar because it mirrors the clinical-ethics sections you studied in Unit 3 [S20]:

- **Informed consent to research** — voluntary participation; the purpose, procedures, risks, and any experimental elements disclosed; the right to decline or withdraw without penalty.
- **Confidentiality of research data** — the same protection research participants get that clients get.
- **Avoiding harm** and honest **reporting of results** — no fabrication, don't bury unfavorable findings, correct errors, and give proper **publication credit** (research-integrity duties that connect straight to the [[concept-evidence-based-practice | publication-bias]] problem — burying null results is both an ethics violation *and* a distortion of the evidence base).
- **Boundaries with participants** — a specific flag: when participants are also **students, supervisees, or clients**, they must be free to decline **without cost to that relationship** — the research version of Unit 3's [[concept-dual-relationships | dual-relationship]] rule (the power differential makes "voluntary" consent suspect).

## The stance: consent is the through-line

The single idea that unifies this page with the whole ethics thread of the curriculum: **the person's informed, voluntary, revocable agreement is the floor, not a formality.** Every abuse above is, at bottom, a consent failure. Every protection above — Belmont's respect for persons, the IRB's review, ACA's Section G — is a way of guaranteeing that a participant, like a client, is a person who agreed, understanding what they agreed to and free to stop. That's the same conviction under Unit 3's [[concept-informed-consent | informed consent]], now extended to the research relationship — which is a fitting place for the final unit of the spine to close: the methods that let us know things, bounded by the ethics that keep the knowing from costing people their dignity.
