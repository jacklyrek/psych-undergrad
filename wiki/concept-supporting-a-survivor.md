# Supporting a Survivor You Love

The deliberate sibling of the PFA elective's [[concept-supporting-the-bereaved]], and the half of this module that is not about being a counselor. Someone will tell you — a partner, a friend, an adult child, a parent disclosing something from fifty years ago — and you will not be in a professional role, will not have a referral list open, and will not get a second first response.

This is not a diluted version of the clinical material. **Informal support is where most disclosures actually land**, and Ullman's finding applies with full force to it: the reactions a survivor receives predict their PTSD symptoms, and positive reactions predict the **perceived control** that tracks recovery. [S14][S15] You are not a lesser substitute for a therapist. You are the variable the research was measuring.

## The same core, minus the paperwork

The three sentences from [[concept-disclosure-response]] are the same here, and they are enough: [S17]

- **"I believe you."**
- **"It's not your fault."**
- **"Thank you for telling me."**

Then: **"What do you need?"** — and accept "I don't know" as an answer rather than filling it.

The harmful responses are the same too, and one is far more likely from someone who loves the person: **"I'll kill him."** Rage on a survivor's behalf feels like loyalty and functions as a burden. It is the **egocentric response** in the Unsupportive Acknowledgment category [S15] — the survivor now has to manage your feelings, reassure you, and possibly protect the person who harmed them from you. It also tells them, clearly, that telling you more will cost them. Feel it; do not perform it at them. Take it somewhere else.

The other one to watch for is **taking over**: calling the police, booking the appointment, driving them to the hospital, telling the family — before asking. This is classified as a **negative** social reaction, not a neutral one. [S15] The thing that was done to them was the removal of their control over their own body and life; the loving, competent, decisive response reproduces that structure in miniature. **Ask first, every time, even when the answer seems obvious.**

## The things people get wrong that a counselor would not

**"Why didn't you tell me sooner?"** — usually hurt, heard as accusation. Delayed disclosure is the norm ([[concept-child-sexual-abuse]]), and for men the delay is commonly two decades or more. [S20] They are telling you now. That is the event.

**Pressing for details.** From a partner this often has a specific and ugly shape — wanting to know exactly what happened, who, how far — that is genuinely about the supporter's own need to process. It does not help, it is not owed, and asking for it makes the next disclosure less likely. If they volunteer, receive it. Do not ask.

**Treating them as fragile or as changed.** Stigmatizing and treating someone differently is in the **Turning Against** category — the more PTSD-predictive of the two. [S15] Suddenly handling them with care, stopping ordinary teasing, or looking at them differently communicates *you are damaged now*. Keep the relationship intact. They have lost enough of their life already.

**Pressure to report.** Specifically identified as retraumatizing. [S17] Their decision, on their timeline, including never. You can make sure they know the options exist ([[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]]) — once, without a follow-up campaign.

**Disappearing after the crisis.** The support that matters most is the least dramatic: still there in month six, when everyone else has moved on and the survivor has stopped bringing it up because they can tell people are tired of it. Same lesson as [[concept-supporting-the-bereaved]] — **keep showing up after the casseroles stop.**

## Intimacy, for partners

If your partner discloses, this becomes real, and silence about it is worse than clumsiness.

Changes in sexual interest and response are common after sexual trauma — loss of interest, avoidance, sometimes the opposite, and flashbacks or dissociation during sex. **Triggers may be unknown until they are encountered**, including by the survivor, so "just tell me what to avoid" can be an unanswerable question. [S38]

The workable stance: **they set the pace, with a standing, genuinely-meant option to stop, change, or start again at any point** — and "stop" must never cost them anything, or it is not a real option. [S38] Talk about it outside of sex, when nobody is exposed and nothing is in progress. Expect non-linearity: a good week does not mean it is resolved, and a bad one does not mean it is back to the start.

Two more things. **A flashback during sex is not about you** — it is a threat-response system doing what [[concept-neurobiology-of-trauma]] describes, and reacting to it as rejection turns their trauma into an injury they have caused you. And **you are allowed to have needs**; you are not allowed to make meeting them the condition of the relationship's stability. If that gap is unmanageable, that is a real problem worth taking to a therapist of your own — which is the next section.

## You are a secondary survivor

**Secondary survivor** names the partner, friend, or family member of someone who was assaulted. The distress is real and valid — and it is **not the survivor's job to manage.** [S37]

Where it goes instead: your own friends, your own therapist, a support line. Many rape crisis centers serve loved ones as well as survivors, and the **National Sexual Assault Hotline (800-656-HOPE)** is available to supporters, not only to survivors. [S16] If this has activated something from your own history, that is common and is worth its own help rather than being carried silently — the same counsel Unit 8 gives counselors in [[concept-counselor-self-care]] and [[concept-vicarious-trauma]], applied to you.

## The boundary: support is not therapy

The failure mode for a supportive, capable person — and especially for someone studying counseling — is drifting into being their therapist. It does not work, for structural reasons that have nothing to do with competence.

**What you can do:** listen, believe, be steady, stay present over months, help with concrete practical things when asked, and know the referral numbers.

**What you cannot do:** be their trauma clinician. You cannot be neutral about them, you have your own stake in their recovery, you will be tempted to dig because you want them better, and a relationship that becomes primarily therapeutic loses the thing that made it valuable — an ordinary relationship in which they are a whole person rather than a patient. This is the informal version of [[concept-dual-relationships]], and the reasoning is the same. **If you are a counselor or in training, this is also an ethical line, not just a practical one:** you do not treat people you love, and "I'm just using what I know" is how that line gets crossed. [S20]

**What helps most is the least specialized thing you have:** you were there, you believed them, and you were still there later.
