# Piaget's Cognitive-Development Stages

**The idea:** children are not miniature adults who simply know less — they *think differently*, in qualitatively distinct ways that reorganize as they grow. Jean Piaget described cognition as something the child **actively constructs** by acting on the world, not something poured in by teaching. For a counselor, the payoff is calibration: **what kind of reasoning can the person in front of me actually do?** — which governs everything from how you explain a treatment to a child to why an adolescent can suddenly agonize over abstract hypotheticals.

## The engine: schemas, assimilation, accommodation, equilibration [S2]

- **Schema** — a mental structure, a unit of knowledge ("dog": four legs, furry, barks).
- **Assimilation** — taking in new information by **fitting it into an existing schema** (a child sees a cat and calls it "dog").
- **Accommodation** — **revising the schema** when it doesn't fit ("that's a *cat* — different thing").
- **Equilibration** — the drive to resolve the tension between the two; the motor that pushes development from stage to stage.

Assimilation vs. accommodation is a classic confusable pair: **assimilation bends the world to fit the mind; accommodation bends the mind to fit the world.**

## The four stages [S2]

| Stage | Age (approx.) | Signature achievement | The limit it's still under |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sensorimotor** | 0–2 | knows the world through **sensory & motor action**; gains **object permanence** (~6 mo) — things exist when out of sight | thought is tied to action; little symbolic representation early on |
| **Pre-operational** | 2–7 | **symbols & language** explode; pretend play | **egocentrism** (can't take others' viewpoint), no **conservation**, centration, no reversibility |
| **Concrete operational** | 7–11 | **logical operations** on concrete things: **conservation**, reversibility, classification, inductive logic | can't yet reason systematically about the abstract or hypothetical |
| **Formal operational** | 12+ | **abstract & hypothetical-deductive** reasoning — theories, "what if," ideals like justice and love | (Piaget's ceiling; many adults use it inconsistently) |

### Two landmark demonstrations

- **Object permanence** (sensorimotor): before ~6 months, out of sight is out of mind — hide a toy and the infant doesn't search. Its arrival is the stage's hallmark.
- **Conservation** (the pre-op → concrete boundary): pour the same water from a short wide glass into a tall thin one and ask a 4-year-old which has more. The pre-operational child says the tall one — they **center** on height and can't **reverse** the operation. The concrete-operational child knows the amount is unchanged. Conservation is *the* tell that a child has crossed into concrete operations.
- **Egocentrism** (pre-op): not selfishness — the literal inability to grasp that another person sees a different view. (Piaget's "three mountains" task.)

## Using it clinically

- **Match your language to the stage.** A pre-operational child cannot process an abstract question like "how does that make you feel about your family?"; they work in the concrete and symbolic — hence **play therapy**, drawing, and stories rather than insight talk. A concrete-operational child can reason about real situations but not hypotheticals; an adolescent in formal operations can suddenly do the abstract, future-oriented, idealistic (and anxious) reasoning that fuels a lot of teenage distress.
- **Don't assume "formal operations" is universal in adults.** Stress, limited education, or the domain can all pull an adult's reasoning back toward the concrete. Meet the actual reasoning, not the chronological age.
- **Normal vs. concern.** Egocentrism at 4 is on-schedule; the same at 12 is a flag. Knowing the sequence is, again, how you tell typical from clinical ([[unit05-development]]).

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **He underestimated infants and children.** Later methods show **object permanence** and **theory of mind** (grasping others' minds) arrive **earlier** than Piaget claimed — theory of mind by ~4–5, well before his egocentrism timeline says it should. [S2]
- **He overestimated adolescents (and adults).** **Formal operational** reasoning is used inconsistently and is far from universal even in adults. [S2]
- **He skipped the social and cultural.** Piaget's child is a lone scientist experimenting on the world. **Vygotsky's** sociocultural critique names the gap: cognition is **scaffolded by social interaction and language** — the **zone of proximal development** (what a child can do *with help*) is where learning happens, and culture shapes what develops. [S2]
- **Stages are a better guide to *sequence* than to *ages*.** The order is robust; the ages are approximate and "poorly generalizable," with abilities overlapping stage boundaries. [S2]
