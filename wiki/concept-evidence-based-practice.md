# Evidence-Based Practice, the Evidence Hierarchy & the Replication Crisis

"Evidence-based" is one of the most-used and most-misused phrases in the field. It is invoked to sell trainings, to win insurance approval, and sometimes to shut down a conversation ("but is it *evidence-based*?"). This page does three things: says what evidence-based practice **actually means** (it's an act of integration, not a trump card), gives you the **hierarchy** for ranking what counts as strong evidence, and — crucially — hands you the **humility** to read that evidence critically, because the field has learned the hard way that a lot of published findings do not hold up.

## What EBP actually is: three legs, integrated

The APA's official definition, which [[unit09-modalities | Unit 9]] already leaned on, is worth holding verbatim: evidence-based practice in psychology is **"the integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture, and preferences"** [S11]. Three co-equal legs:

1. **Best available research evidence**,
2. **Clinical expertise** (the counselor's trained judgment), and
3. **Client characteristics, culture, and values/preferences.**

The load-bearing word is **integration**. EBP is **not** "research evidence overrides everything else"; a treatment with the best RCT support is not evidence-based practice for a client who won't do it, can't access it, or whose culture it ignores. This is the definition that keeps "evidence-based" from becoming a cudgel — and it's the same three-legged stool wherever you meet the phrase. **Don't confuse EBP with an EST** (below): an *empirically supported treatment* is a specific therapy shown to work in trials (one input to leg 1); *evidence-based practice* is the whole integrative process.

## The evidence hierarchy: ranking "best available research"

Not all evidence is equal, and the **evidence pyramid** ranks study designs by how resistant they are to bias [S13], strongest at the top:

1. **Systematic reviews & meta-analyses** — rigorous syntheses of *all* the good studies on a question (a meta-analysis pools their effect sizes).
2. **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)** — the strongest single-study design for a treatment claim ([[concept-experimental-vs-correlational]]).
3. **Cohort studies** — follow groups over time (observational, no randomization).
4. **Case-control studies** — compare people with/without an outcome, looking back.
5. **Cross-sectional studies / case series.**
6. **Case reports & expert opinion** — weakest; a single anecdote or authority.

As you climb, the evidence gets **less susceptible to bias** but also **rarer**. Two caveats keep the pyramid honest: it ranks **design, not execution** — a **poor** meta-analysis is not automatically better than a **well-run** RCT, and quality-appraisal systems like **GRADE** exist precisely to judge quality *within* a level; and the top designs can still have **poor external validity** ([[concept-validity-threats]]), so "highest on the pyramid" is the starting question, not the final word.

**Empirically supported treatments (ESTs)** apply this logic to name specific therapies. Chambless and Hollon's criteria are the standard: **well-established** = efficacy shown in **≥2 good between-group experiments by independent research teams** (guarding against a single lab's bias); **probably efficacious** = a lower tier with fewer or non-independent studies [S12]. Note what the label means and doesn't: "empirically supported" is a **floor** ("this has been *shown* to work for this problem"), **not** a ranking that makes unlisted approaches worthless — and the common-factors / dodo-bird finding (Unit 9) shows technique differences are often small once the alliance is accounted for.

## The reason to stay humble: the replication crisis

Here is the fact that should recalibrate how you read any single exciting study. In 2015, the **Open Science Collaboration** tried to replicate **100** psychology studies from top journals. Although ~97% of the originals reported significant results, only about **36%** of the replications reached significance, and effect sizes **shrank by about half** [S14]. This is the empirical core of the **replication crisis**, and its causes are now well understood:

- **Publication bias (the file-drawer problem)** — journals prefer positive, novel results, so null findings and failed replications go unpublished, making the visible literature look stronger than the real one.
- **p-hacking / researcher degrees of freedom** — the many small, defensible-looking analytic choices (when to stop collecting data, which covariates to include, which outcomes to report) can be flexed — usually unconsciously — until p dips below .05. Simmons and colleagues showed this can push the false-positive rate far above the nominal 5% [S14].
- **HARKing** — presenting a post-hoc finding as if it had been the hypothesis all along.
- **Allegiance bias** — the developer's own trials tend to report the **biggest** effects (the SFBT-effect-size caution in Unit 9; the enthusiasm behind new group and neuro claims in Units 10–11).

The field's response is the **open-science** movement: **preregistration** and **registered reports** (committing to hypotheses and analyses *before* seeing the data, and getting publication guaranteed regardless of outcome), larger samples, open data, and — the culture change that matters most — **valuing replication and null results**.

The takeaway is not cynicism ("psychology is fake"). It's **calibration**: a single, splashy, un-replicated study earns provisional interest, not belief; a replicated effect with a preregistered design and a meaningful effect size earns real confidence. Being a discerning consumer of the field's competing claims — the syllabus objective — *is* this calibrated skepticism.

## Where this lands clinically

When a workshop, a rep, or a headline pitches you an intervention, this page is the checklist: *What's the evidence — an RCT, a meta-analysis, or a testimonial? Who ran the studies — independent teams or the developer? How big is the effect, and has it replicated? And does it fit **this** client's values and access* ([[concept-effect-size]], the three legs)? That is not academic caution; it's how you avoid wasting a client's time and money on the confidently-marketed-but-unsupported, which the field produces in abundance.
