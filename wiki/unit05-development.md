# Unit 5 — Human Development Across the Lifespan

The syllabus gives this unit its one-line justification: **you can't tell what's wrong if you don't
know what's normal for the age.** A 3-year-old who doesn't grasp that others see the world
differently is on schedule; a 30-year-old who can't is not. A toddler with separation anxiety is
developing well; the same protest in a specific context tells you something else. Development is the
baseline against which "clinical concern" is even a meaningful phrase — so this unit builds the
baseline, and then teaches the one framework that will show up in your consulting room more than any
other: **attachment.**

This is a Tier 2 clinical-foundations unit. It maps to CACREP's **Human Growth & Development** core
area, and it hands forward directly to [[unit06-psychopathology]] (you diagnose against a
developmental norm) and back to [[unit01-helping-skills]] (attachment is the theory of how your
client will relate to *you*).

## The four frameworks, and why attachment leads

| Framework | Who | What it explains | The one-line version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[theory-attachment]] | Bowlby & Ainsworth | how early caregiving builds a template for all later closeness | *the single most clinically useful developmental idea* |
| [[theory-eriksons-stages]] | Erik Erikson | the psychosocial task the person is wrestling with at each life phase | "which crisis is this client stuck in?" |
| [[theory-piaget]] | Jean Piaget | how thinking itself is reorganized from infancy to adolescence | "what kind of reasoning can this person actually do?" |
| [[concept-biopsychosocial-model]] | George Engel | the frame that keeps all three (plus biology and context) in one picture | "the person suffers as a whole, not as isolated organs" |

The syllabus and course-map both flag **attachment** as *the single most clinically useful
developmental idea*, and this unit takes that seriously: attachment gets a full theory page
([[theory-attachment]]), a dedicated **four-styles** page built for discrimination
([[concept-attachment-styles]]), and the people who built it ([[person-bowlby-ainsworth]]). The
reason is [[unit01-helping-skills]]: **attachment patterns show up constantly in how clients relate
to you and to others.** The client who needs constant reassurance and merges with you, the client
who stays cool and keeps the alliance at arm's length, the client whose closeness and fear fire at
once — those are attachment patterns walking into the room, and Bowlby explicitly called the
**therapist an attachment figure**. [S3]

## The thread that runs through everything: risk, not destiny

The hardest thing to hold correctly in this unit — and the place the "fix-it" reflex does the most
damage — is that **developmental frameworks describe probabilities, not verdicts.** Insecure
attachment is a **risk factor** for later problems *only if* development continues in the context of
other risks; many insecurely attached children turn out fine, and later relationships (including
therapy) can revise the template. [S3][S7] Erikson's "crises" are not pass/fail exams — you can
carry an unresolved one forward and still rework it later. [S1] A client is never their attachment
"type," their stuck Erikson stage, or their Piaget ceiling. Two technical names for why:

- **Equifinality** — many different developmental paths can lead to the *same* outcome (depression
  has many roads in).
- **Multifinality** — the *same* starting point (say, an insecure infancy) can lead to many
  different outcomes.

Both are just formal ways of saying: **a developmental history is a hypothesis about a client, not a
diagnosis of one.** Use it to understand, not to file them.

## Distinguishing typical development from clinical concern

This is a stated objective, and it has a concrete clinical shape. Clinicians who work with children
practice **developmental surveillance** — a longitudinal habit of eliciting concerns, taking a
milestone history, observing, and applying judgment — using **age-dependent milestone norms** to
calibrate *how concerned to be* when something is missing. [S11] The same logic scales to any age:
know the normal range first, treat deviations as questions rather than answers, and remember that
**most variation is normal variation.** Over-pathologizing ordinary developmental turbulence
(adolescent identity churn, a toddler's defiance, an older adult's life review) is the mirror-image
error to missing a real delay — and both are failures of knowing the baseline.

## The practice rep (from the syllabus)

**Sketch your own attachment history and your Erikson stages.** Where do you land on the four
attachment styles ([[concept-attachment-styles]]) — and can you see it in how you do closeness now?
Which Erikson crisis are you actually working on ([[theory-eriksons-stages]])? Self-application is
what makes these frameworks stick, and it previews the countertransference work of the whole
program: your own attachment pattern will shape how you sit with a clinging client or a distant one.

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **"Sensitive parenting → secure baby" is not a clean law.** The sensitivity–security correlation
  is only modest (r ≈ .24), and the **"transmission gap"** means a parent's own state of mind
  predicts the baby's attachment beyond what observed sensitivity explains. Temperament is a live
  rival account. [S3][S9]
- **The Strange Situation may be an "imposed etic."** Secure is the most common style in every
  culture studied, but the procedure was built on US norms and its behaviors can *mean* different
  things elsewhere. [S6]
- **Erikson and Piaget are heuristics, not measured facts.** The stage *ages* are approximate and
  culture-bound; Piaget under-credited infants, over-credited adolescents, and skipped culture
  (Vygotsky's critique). [S1][S2]
- **The biopsychosocial model is both indispensable and vague.** It's the field's default
  case-formulation frame *and* is criticized as a "slogan" that licenses everything and specifies
  nothing. Hold it as a checklist you must make specific to this client. [S4]
