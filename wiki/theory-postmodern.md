# Postmodern Theory

**Stuck because:** the person is trapped inside a **problem-saturated story** about themselves —
built from language, culture, and relationship — that hides their resources and exceptions. There is
**no single objective truth** to uncover; reality is *constructed.* **Better by:** collaboratively
**re-authoring** that story and building on the times the problem *didn't* win. [S13][S14]

This is the newest family and the most radical about the counselor's role. The therapist **gives up
the expert chair**: they don't diagnose the client's "real" problem or know the truth better than the
client does. The stance is **non-expert, collaborative, curious.** [S13]

## The shared foundation: social constructionism
There is no single objective reality waiting to be diagnosed; **meaning is constructed through language
and relationship**, and emerges in dialogue. [S13] The clinically useful version of this isn't a
denial of facts — it's a commitment to treating the **client's meaning** as primary and the counselor's
interpretations as just one more story, not the truth. Two main approaches grew from it.

## Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) — de Shazer & Insoo Kim Berg
**Future-oriented, anti-deterministic, resource-focused.** SFBT assumes clients can change quickly and
spends almost no time on the problem's origins — it builds the **solution** instead. [S14] Signature
techniques [S13]:
- **The miracle question** — *"Suppose tonight while you sleep a miracle happens and the problem is
  solved. You don't know it happened. What's the first thing you'd notice tomorrow that tells you?"*
  Elicits a concrete picture of the desired future.
- **Exception questions** — *"When is the problem even a little less present? What's different then?"*
  Finds the resources already in play.
- **Scaling questions** — *"On a 0–10 scale, where are you today? What would move you up one point?"*
  Makes progress concrete and incremental.

## Narrative Therapy — Michael White & David Epston
Identity is made of **stories**; distress comes from living inside a **dominant, problem-saturated
story.** [S13] Core moves:
- **"The person is not the problem; the problem is the problem."** — the defining premise.
- **Externalizing** — language that separates the person from the problem ("When did *the Anxiety*
  first start pushing you around?"), so it can be examined and resisted rather than *being* the person.
- **Dominant story vs. alternative story** — the problem-saturated account vs. the overlooked account
  of competence and value.
- **Unique outcomes (sparkling moments)** — exceptions where the problem *didn't* dominate; the seeds
  of the alternative story.
- **Re-authoring** — thickening that alternative story into a new, preferred narrative of identity. [S13]

> Memory hook: **SFBT** asks *"what does the solution look like, and when does it already happen?"*
> **Narrative** asks *"whose story is running your life, and what story would you rather live?"* Both
> are present/future-focused and both make the client the author.

## How change happens
Through **conversation that shifts meaning** — not insight into the past, not restructuring "distorted"
thoughts against a fixed reality, but co-constructing a more livable story and amplifying what already
works. The counselor's curiosity and not-knowing stance are *the method*, not just bedside manner. [S13]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)
- **"No single truth"** is philosophically contested and can sit awkwardly with **evidence-based
  practice** and real diagnostic/risk realities (you still assess suicide risk objectively — Unit 8).
  Most clinicians treat constructionism as a stance toward the client's *meaning*, not a denial of
  clinical fact. [S13]
- **Brevity vs. depth.** Critics ask whether solution-focused brevity can shortchange clients with
  complex trauma or entrenched problems. [S13]
- **Evidence base** is growing but thinner and harder to standardize than CBT's. [S14]
