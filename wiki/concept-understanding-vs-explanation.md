# Understanding vs. Explanation

**One line:** Jaspers distinguished two legitimate but different ways of making sense of a patient. **Understanding** (*Verstehen*) grasps how mental events *meaningfully* follow from one another, from the inside. **Explanation** (*Erklären*) identifies the *causes* of mental events, from the outside, the way natural science explains anything. Good psychiatry needs both, and trouble starts when one is mistaken for the other. [S22][S23]

## The distinction

| | **Understanding** (*Verstehen*) | **Explanation** (*Erklären*) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | *Why, meaningfully?* How does this follow from that, for this person? | *What causes this?* |
| Stance | inside: empathic re-living of meaningful connections | outside: observation, regularities, mechanisms |
| Connection type | **meaningful** (motives, reasons, feelings) | **causal** (laws, biology, mechanism) |
| Example | "He was humiliated at work, so his rage at home makes sense." | "His irritability follows a thyroid disorder." / "The delirium is from alcohol withdrawal." |
| Can be... | convincing, but not proven by statistics | tested, generalized, predicted |
| Roots | Dilthey's human sciences (*Geisteswissenschaften*) | the natural sciences (*Naturwissenschaften*) |

Jaspers took the understanding/explanation contrast from the philosopher **Wilhelm Dilthey**, who used it to separate the human sciences from the natural sciences. [S22]

## Three levels in Jaspers' method
Keep these apart; students often collapse the first two: [S22]
1. **Static understanding (phenomenology):** *what* is the patient experiencing? Precise description of an experience in its own right, before any connection-making. [S22]
2. **Genetic understanding:** *how* does this experience **meaningfully** arise from that one? [S22]
3. **Causal explanation:** what **causes** it? [S22][S23]

## Where understanding runs out

For Jaspers, some phenomena **cannot be understood** genetically. The classic case is the **primary delusion**, which cannot be meaningfully derived from anything prior in the person's experience. There, he said, the clinician must switch to explanation and look for a disease **process**. [S22][S24] That un-understandability was, for Jaspers, a mark of psychosis.

> **Critics' point:** "un-understandable" may reflect the *clinician's* failure of imagination rather than something about the patient, and has been used to put psychotic people beyond empathy. Modern phenomenological psychiatry keeps Jaspers's careful description but rejects the idea that psychotic experience is simply closed to understanding ([[theory-phenomenological-psychopathology]]). [S24][S31]

## Why this matters for a counselor

1. **Case conceptualization uses both, and should label which is which.** "Her panic follows her father's death" is an *understanding* claim. "Her panic is worsened by caffeine and sleep deprivation" is an *explanation* claim. Both belong in the Five Ps; they are tested differently ([[concept-case-conceptualization]]).
2. **Don't psychologize the medical.** A meaningful story can be told about almost anything, including symptoms with a physical cause (thyroid, delirium, a tumor, substance withdrawal). The existence of an understandable narrative **does not rule out a cause that needs a physician** ([[concept-scope-of-practice]], [[concept-biopsychosocial-model]]).
3. **Don't medicalize the meaningful.** Equally, a biological contribution does not make the person's reasons and experience irrelevant. The biopsychosocial model is, in a sense, Jaspers's point made institutional. [S23]
4. **Empathy has a method.** Jaspers's "understanding" is a disciplined, checkable use of empathy, close to what Rogers called entering the internal frame of reference ([[concept-empathy]], [[concept-phenomenology-of-empathy]]).

## Keep apart
- **Understanding ≠ agreeing.** Understanding how a client's violent rage follows from humiliation does not excuse it. It makes it intelligible.
- **Explanation ≠ reduction.** Giving a causal account doesn't make the experience "not real" or unimportant. [S23]
