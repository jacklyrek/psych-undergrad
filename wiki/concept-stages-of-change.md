# Stages of Change: The Transtheoretical Model

The most useful answer to "how do you break an addiction?" is: **not all at once, and not from a standing start.** Prochaska & DiClemente's **Transtheoretical Model (TTM)** — the "stages of change" — describes change as a **process a person moves through**, and gives the helper the single most important diagnostic question they can ask: *not* "how do I get them to quit?" but **"where are they right now?"** Matching your move to the stage is the whole game; pushing action on someone who is still precontemplative is the classic way to create "resistance." [S6]

## The five stages (plus the loop)

1. **Precontemplation** — **not considering change** in the foreseeable future (next ~6 months). Often unaware or minimizing ("I can stop anytime," "it's not a problem"). May be there because they've tried and failed before. *Others* usually see the problem first.
2. **Contemplation** — **aware and ambivalent.** Sees pros *and* cons of changing and is stuck between them ("I know I should, but…"). Can sit here for a long time. This is the home of **ambivalence**, the target of [[concept-treatment-and-recovery]]'s Motivational Interviewing.
3. **Preparation** — **intending to act soon** (within ~a month) and taking first steps: telling someone, researching options, cutting back, making a plan.
4. **Action** — **actively changing** the behavior — the visible, effortful phase (quitting, entering treatment). Where outsiders think "change" begins, but it rests on the three stages before it.
5. **Maintenance** — **sustaining the change** and working to prevent relapse; the new pattern is becoming the default but still takes work. This is where [[concept-relapse-prevention]] lives.

**Relapse / recycling** is treated as an **expected event**, not a stage failure: most people cycle back to an earlier stage and go around again. [S6]

## It's a spiral, not a staircase

Prochaska & DiClemente originally drew discrete stages, but the field now models change as a **spiral**: people loop through the stages **several times** before change sticks, each pass usually starting from a bit further along rather than back at zero. [S6] The clinical consequence is a posture, not just a fact: a return to use is a **normal turn of the spiral** to be learned from, not proof the person "can't change" — which is the same reframing [[concept-relapse-prevention]] makes at the level of a single slip.

## Decisional balance: the pros and cons tip

Movement across the early stages tracks a shifting **decisional balance** — the person's felt weight of the pros vs. cons of changing. In precontemplation the cons of change dominate (using still "works" for them); progress looks like the **pros of change** gaining weight until they tip the scale. [S6] You rarely tip it *for* someone by adding arguments (that triggers the **righting reflex** backlash — see [[concept-treatment-and-recovery]]); you help them **voice their own** pros of change.

## Matching the intervention to the stage

The model's practical core: **different stages need different help.** [S6]

| Stage | What the person needs | What backfires |
|---|---|---|
| **Precontemplation** | Information, concern, a relationship — *raise doubt*, don't demand action | Ultimatums, lectures ("just quit") |
| **Contemplation** | Help **tipping the balance** — explore ambivalence, evoke change talk | Pushing a plan before they're ready |
| **Preparation** | Concrete options, a plan, first-step support | Vagueness; withholding practical help |
| **Action** | Skills, structure, active treatment, reinforcement | Assuming they're "cured"; disappearing |
| **Maintenance** | Relapse-prevention skills, cue management, support network | Treating it as finished |

Delivering the right help at the *wrong* stage is a leading cause of stalled change — action-oriented advice to a precontemplator reliably produces defensiveness, not motion.

## Why this matters in the room
The single most common helper error in addiction work is **stage mismatch**: pressing action on someone who hasn't yet resolved *whether* to change. It feels like helping; it produces argument. TTM reframes the goal from "get them to quit today" to "help them take **one step** along the spiral," which is both more effective and more humane — and it means a precontemplative client who leaves merely *doubting* their use has moved, even though nothing visible changed.
