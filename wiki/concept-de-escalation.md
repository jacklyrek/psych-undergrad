# Crisis Intervention & De-escalation Models

A **crisis** is not defined by the event. It's defined by the person's **perception** of an event or situation as an **intolerable difficulty that overwhelms their current resources and coping** [S9]. Two people lose the same job; one problem-solves and one falls apart — the difference is in the appraisal and the resources, not the event. This matters because it tells you the intervention target: crisis work restores enough coping and control to get the person through the acute period, not to solve the underlying problem. This page covers the two things that show up in practicum: a **general crisis-intervention model** (the structure for helping someone in acute crisis) and **verbal de-escalation** (the specific skills for an agitated, escalating person).

## The through-line: safety is not a step, it's the thread

Across every model below, **client safety is continuous, not sequential** [S9]. It's tempting to read a step model as "do step 1, then step 2," but in crisis work safety is re-checked at every moment — because it can change in seconds. Hold that as the frame for everything that follows.

## James & Gilliland's six-step model (the anchor)

The unit's anchor text organizes crisis intervention as three **listening** steps and three **acting** steps [S9]:

**Listening**
1. **Define the problem** — from the *client's* point of view, using core [[concept-microskills-hierarchy | microskills]] (open questions, reflection, empathy). You cannot help with a crisis you've defined for them.
2. **Ensure client safety** — the continuous first priority; assess and reduce danger to self and others (this is where [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]] and [[concept-means-reduction]] live).
3. **Provide support** — be present, non-judgmental, and unconditionally accepting; the person needs to know someone is *with* them.

**Acting**
4. **Examine alternatives** — mobilize situational supports, coping mechanisms, and constructive thinking the person may not see in the tunnel of crisis.
5. **Make plans** — concrete, collaborative, doable next steps that restore a sense of **control** (this is where the [[concept-safety-planning | safety plan]] gets built).
6. **Obtain commitment** — a clear, realistic agreement to act on the plan.

The acting steps are *collaborative* — the counselor is more directive than in ordinary therapy (a crisis is not the time for pure non-directiveness) but still works *with* the person to restore their own agency, not to take over.

## Roberts' seven-stage model (the same logic, finer grain)

Roberts' **Seven-Stage Crisis Intervention Model** covers the same territory with more granularity [S10]: (1) **assess** lethality and biopsychosocial risk; (2) **establish rapport**; (3) **identify the major problems** (including the "last straw" precipitant); (4) **deal with feelings** (validate, actively listen); (5) **generate alternatives**; (6) **develop an action plan**; (7) **follow-up**. Note the shared spine with James & Gilliland — **assess/safety first, rapport, alternatives, plan, follow-up** — which is the pattern to actually remember. The models differ in how finely they slice the same sequence, not in what they tell you to do.

## Verbal de-escalation: Project BETA's ten domains

When the person is **agitated and escalating** — not (only) in despair but keyed up, angry, or on the edge of losing control — you need the specific skills of **verbal de-escalation**. The **Project BETA** consensus (American Association for Emergency Psychiatry) frames the goal in three moves: verbally **engage** the person → build a **collaborative** relationship → talk them **down** out of the agitation. Its **ten domains** [S11]:

1. **Respect personal space** — keep at least **two arms' lengths**, and keep an exit clear for *both* of you.
2. **Do not be provocative** — calm posture, **hands visible**, no staring down, no challenge; your body says "not a threat."
3. **Establish verbal contact** — **only one person** talks to the patient (multiple voices escalate and confuse).
4. **Be concise** — short sentences, simple words; an agitated brain processes language poorly. Repeat as needed.
5. **Identify wants and feelings** — "What would help right now?" Often the agitation has a concrete, meetable need behind it.
6. **Listen closely** — active listening; "Miller's law" (assume what they say is true *from their perspective*); reflect it back ("Tell me if I've got this right…").
7. **Agree or agree to disagree** — find something true to agree with ("You're right that the wait has been ridiculous"); you don't have to win.
8. **Set clear limits** — matter-of-factly and respectfully, without threats ("It's okay to be angry; it's not okay to hurt anyone here").
9. **Offer choices and optimism** — give real alternatives to violence and honest hope; choices restore control.
10. **Debrief** — afterward, with the patient *and* staff, to repair the relationship and learn from what happened.

## Restraint is a last resort — and here's the reasoning

Coercion, physical restraint, and forced medication are the **last** resort, not a shortcut [S11]. Beyond the injury risk, restraint tends to **reinforce the idea that violence is how conflict gets resolved**, is associated with longer stays and worse outcomes, and ruptures the very relationship that makes future help possible. Successful verbal de-escalation preserves the person's dignity and returns control to them, which is both more humane and more effective. This is the same logic as everywhere else in the unit: the counselor's job is to restore the person's agency, not to override it.

## The stance traps

- **Getting bigger when they get bigger.** Matching agitation with your own raised voice or squared shoulders escalates. The skill is to get *calmer and smaller* as they get louder.
- **Reasoning with an escalated brain.** Long explanations and logic fail during high arousal; be concise, meet the need, de-escalate first, *then* problem-solve.
- **Skipping safety because the model looks sequential.** Safety is continuous; re-check it constantly.
- **Winning the argument.** "Agree or agree to disagree" — being right is not the goal; getting to calm is.
