# Unit 3 — Ethics, Law & Professional Identity

This is the one unit where a mistake can end a career or badly harm a client. The syllabus puts it
fourth week for a reason: you will face a confidentiality-limit decision early in practicum, and
the contours need to be in your head *before* you're in the chair. The unit has three jobs:

1. **Know the ACA Code** — its structure, its principles, and the standards that come up most.
2. **Know confidentiality and its limits cold** — including the duty to warn/protect and mandated
   reporting.
3. **Know who you are professionally** — scope of practice, and how counseling differs from
   psychology, social work, and psychiatry.

**CACREP area:** Professional Counseling Orientation & Ethical Practice.

## The ACA Code of Ethics: structure

The 2014 ACA Code of Ethics (the current edition) contains **nine sections**, each opening with an
aspirational introduction followed by enforceable standards: [S1][S9]

| §   | Title                                             | One-line gist                                   |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| A   | The Counseling Relationship                       | welfare, consent, boundaries, termination       |
| B   | Confidentiality and Privacy                       | the promise and its exceptions                  |
| C   | Professional Responsibility                       | competence, advertising, public responsibility  |
| D   | Relationships With Other Professionals            | colleagues, teams, consultation                 |
| E   | Evaluation, Assessment, and Interpretation        | testing and diagnosis done properly             |
| F   | Supervision, Training, and Teaching               | duties of supervisors & educators               |
| G   | Research and Publication                          | research ethics with clients/participants       |
| H   | Distance Counseling, Technology, and Social Media | new in 2014 — telehealth & the digital boundary |
| I   | Resolving Ethical Issues/                         | what to do about violations, ethics vs. law     |

Memory scaffold: A and B are where clinical life happens (the relationship and the secret); C–E are
your professional conduct; F–G teaching and research; H technology; I meta-rules.

## The foundation: five values, six principles

The Code grounds its standards in **five professional values** (lifespan human development;
diversity/multiculturalism; social justice; integrity of the counselor–client relationship;
competent, ethical practice) and **six ethical principles**: [S1][S10]

- **Autonomy** — fostering the client's right to control the direction of their own life.
- **Nonmaleficence** — avoiding actions that cause harm ("first, do no harm").
- **Beneficence** — working for the good of the individual and society.
- **Justice** — treating individuals equitably; fostering fairness.
- **Fidelity** — honoring commitments; keeping the trust the relationship depends on.
- **Veracity** — dealing truthfully.

These six are the vocabulary of every ethics discussion you'll have in a program. Real dilemmas are
almost always **principle vs. principle**: breaking confidentiality to stop a suicide is
nonmaleficence/beneficence overriding autonomy and fidelity. Notice the trap here: the
principles are not a decision procedure you can execute. The Code itself says **"no specific
ethical decision-making model is always most effective"** — you're expected to use *a* credible,
defensible process (identify the problem → consult the Code and the law → consult colleagues →
weigh principles → document your reasoning), not to find the one right algorithm. [S1]

## Legal duty vs. ethical duty (keep these apart)

Two different systems regulate you, and they can disagree: [S1]

|              | Ethical duty                                                       | Legal duty                                                     |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source       | professional code (ACA), enforced by association & licensing board | statute, regulation, case law — enforced by courts & the state |
| Breach means | ethics complaint, sanction, loss of license                        | criminal/civil liability, damages                              |
| Example      | don't abandon a client (A.12)                                      | report suspected child abuse (state statute)                   |

The Code is explicit that breaching it "does not necessarily constitute legal liability" — and the
reverse also holds: something can be legal and still unethical (dating a client the day after
termination is legal in many states; the Code prohibits it for five years). When ethics and law
**conflict**, Standard I.1.c says: make your commitment to the Code known, try to resolve the
conflict, and if it can't be resolved, you **may** follow the law — acting in the client's best
interest. [S1] The classic live example is a court order to disclose records: ethically you seek
consent or seek to narrow the disclosure (B.2.d), legally you may ultimately have to comply. [S1]

A related three-way distinction — **privacy vs. confidentiality vs. privilege** — is unpacked in
[[concept-confidentiality-limits]]; privilege is the *legal* shield ([[study-tarasoff]]'s cousin
case, *Jaffee v. Redmond*, created the federal version). [S2]

## The clinical core: consent, confidentiality, and their limits

The working sequence to internalize:

1. **[[concept-informed-consent]]** — the client's decision to enter counseling must be informed,
   documented, and *ongoing* (A.2). The confidentiality limits are disclosed here, up front.
2. **[[concept-confidentiality-limits]]** — the promise (B.1) and its exceptions (B.2): serious and
   foreseeable harm, legal requirements, contagious life-threatening disease, court order — with
   **minimal disclosure** always the rule.
3. **[[concept-duty-to-warn]]** — when the exception involves danger to an identifiable other:
   Tarasoff's duty to warn (1974) → duty to **protect** (1976), and the state-by-state variation.
   The case itself: [[study-tarasoff]].
4. **[[concept-mandated-reporting]]** — the statutory, non-discretionary exception: suspected abuse
   or neglect of children (all 50 states) and, in most states, vulnerable adults.

This chain is the **`confidentiality-exceptions`** cluster — the unit's main interleaving target,
because the exceptions are confusable in exactly the way that matters: *who* triggers them, *what
discretion* you have, and *who* you tell. Unit 8 (crisis work) will build directly on it. [S3][S5]

## Boundaries and the professional self

- **[[concept-dual-relationships]]** — the 2014 Code's two-track approach: a short list of
  *prohibited* relationships (sexual/romantic with current clients — absolute; former clients —
  5 years; friends/family where objectivity fails) and a longer list of *managed* boundary
  questions (attending a client's wedding, the small-town overlap), with documentation required.
  Includes the crossing-vs-violation distinction. [S1][S11]
- **[[concept-scope-of-practice]]** — practice only within your competence (C.2), grow it properly
  (education → training → supervised experience), monitor your effectiveness, and stop when
  impaired. [S1]
- **[[concept-helping-professions-compared]]** — counselor vs. psychologist vs. clinical social
  worker vs. psychiatrist: training, license, stance, and **who prescribes**. [S7][S8]

## The practice rep (from the syllabus)

Write, in plain language, the confidentiality speech you'd give a new client in the first five
minutes — including the exceptions. A worked model lives in [[concept-confidentiality-limits]];
write yours before reading it, then compare. You will say a version of this for real.

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **The law is 50 different laws.** Duty to warn/protect, reporting triggers, and privilege all
  vary by state — mandatory in some, permissive in others, silent in a few. Learn the *pattern*,
  then learn your state's statute when you know where you'll practice. [S3][S5][S6]
- **Tarasoff assumes prediction that the science doesn't support.** Validated tools for predicting
  dangerousness are lacking; clinical judgment plus consultation and documentation is the actual
  standard. [S3]
- **How much to warn clients up front** is debated — exhaustive warnings chill disclosure and
  aren't retained; the consensus is a clear plain-language summary at intake, revisited when live
  (consent as process). [S4]
