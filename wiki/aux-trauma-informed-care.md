## Start here: the thing nobody tells you first

Trauma-informed care is taught, almost universally, as settled best practice. It is written into policy across U.S. health care, education, child welfare, and criminal justice. Your program will present it as a professional obligation, and it is one.

**And the rigorous outcome evidence for it is thin.** In 2025 the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality — the federal agency for health-services evidence synthesis — screened **1,326 publications**, found **12 eligible studies**, and concluded there was **"insufficient evidence on the effectiveness of current TIC approaches"** for *every* outcome category examined: reducing repeat trauma exposure, improving healthcare processes and policies, improving behavioral and psychosocial wellness, improving physical health, or changing patient harm. [S24] The review also found **"little consistency"** in what organizations even mean by the term, and recorded that its expert Key Informants **"acknowledged debate around what TIC contributes beyond what many generally recognize as 'good care.'"** [S24]

Three things to hold at once, because the temptation is to collapse into one of them:

1. **"Insufficient evidence" is not "it doesn't work."** It means the studies needed to settle the question have not been done well enough or often enough. Twelve studies, all at high risk of bias, cannot establish a negative any more than a positive. [S24]
2. **The framework is still defensible** — on low risk of harm, ethical coherence, and consistency with what *is* independently established about trauma's prevalence and effects. [S25] Most of what TIC asks for (explain before you act, offer choice, don't ambush people with questions, don't use coercion where avoidable) is defensible on its own terms whether or not a trial has measured it.
3. **This is a live example of implementation outpacing evidence** [S25] — and noticing that is itself a competency, the one [[unit12-research]] exists to build. A counselor who can say "this is the standard of care, here is what supports it, and here is what has not been demonstrated" is doing the job better than one who recites the six principles as though they were findings.

The rest of this module is built to let you make that distinction across every part of the topic.

## The map

1. **[[concept-retraumatization]]** — the harm the whole framework exists to prevent, and the one part with real empirical support. What actually re-traumatizes people (coercion, powerlessness, unpredictability, not being believed), the restraint and seclusion evidence, and the counseling-room version — which is quieter and much easier to commit. [S2][S4][S5][S6]
2. **[[study-ace]]** — the study underneath everything. Felitti & Anda's 17,000-person Kaiser cohort, the **graded dose-response** finding that made trauma a public-health issue, the ten categories, and the substantial critiques. [S7][S8][S13]
3. **[[concept-trauma-screening]]** — the decisions a clinician actually makes. Universal screening vs. assessment, **why the ACE score must not be used as an individual screening tool** (and that its own author says so), what a validated screen looks like by contrast (PC-PTSD-5, LEC-5), and the rule that governs all of it: **don't screen for what you can't respond to.** [S9][S10][S11][S12][S14][S15]
4. **[[concept-tic-implementation]]** — why TIC is an organizational-change framework rather than a technique. SAMHSA's **ten implementation domains**, the trauma-**aware → sensitive → responsive → informed/specific** continuum, and what a single counselor can and cannot do inside a system that is not trauma-informed. [S1][S3][S16][S17][S18]
5. **[[concept-historical-trauma]]** — SAMHSA's sixth principle, filled in. Brave Heart's **historical trauma** and historical unresolved grief, Carter's **race-based traumatic stress** (published in counseling psychology's own flagship journal), and the **contested** status of epigenetic transmission. [S19][S20][S21][S22][S23]
6. **[[concept-tic-evidence]]** — the appraisal in full: what AHRQ found and what it means, the definitional problem that makes TIC hard to study, the "is it just good care?" objection, and how to hold a practice standard whose evidence base is incomplete. [S24][S25][S26]

## The stance, in one paragraph

Trauma-informed care asks you to change your **defaults**, not to acquire a technique. The default assumption becomes *this person may be a trauma survivor and I will not know*, so the safe, predictable, choice-offering way of working is applied to everyone rather than switched on for disclosed survivors — which is what "universal" means in this framework and why it does not require anyone to tell you anything. [S1] The hard part is not learning the six principles; it is noticing the ordinary, well-intentioned, institutionally normal things that remove a person's control — the intake form that demands a trauma history before any relationship exists, the "I just need to ask a few questions," the appointment changed without notice, the decision made for someone's own good. Those are the re-enactments. [S2] And the honest posture, given the evidence: do these things because they are respectful and low-risk and consistent with what we know about trauma — **not** because a trial has shown they change outcomes, because it has not. [S24][S25]

## What's genuinely contested / easy to over-learn

- **The evidence base is thin, and saying so is not cynicism.** AHRQ 2025: **insufficient evidence for every outcome examined**, 12 eligible studies, high risk of bias throughout, and no consistent operationalization across the literature. [S24] Do not repeat "trauma-informed care is evidence-based" without qualification — the defensible claim is that it is **evidence-*informed*, low-risk, and ethically coherent**, which is a different and honest sentence. (Compare the [[aux-psychological-first-aid]] elective, where the same distinction had to be drawn about PFA.)
- **The ACE score is a research instrument, not a screening tool — per its own author.** Robert Anda wrote that the questionnaire was designed to **research, not screen**, and that the score **"is neither a diagnostic tool nor is it predictive at the individual level."** [S9] Empirically, ACE scores predict **group** differences but classify **individuals** poorly. [S10] Universal individual ACE screening is therefore not a matter of preference; it is a measurement error with a policy behind it. — see [[concept-trauma-screening]].
- **A dose-response curve is not a life sentence.** The ACE study's power is epidemiological — risk gradients across a population. Applied to one child it becomes determinism, and critics argue the framing **pathologizes poverty and structural disadvantage** and embeds deficit narratives. [S13] The score also ignores frequency, severity, duration, and developmental timing, and omits whole adversity categories including **racial trauma, community violence, traumatic bereavement, and medical trauma**. [S11] — see [[study-ace]].
- **"Trauma-informed" has become a word people apply to anything.** The AHRQ review could not find consistent content behind the label, and its informants openly questioned what TIC adds beyond good care. [S24][S26] Treat the term as a claim to be checked, not a credential — including when an employer, a training vendor, or a state initiative uses it.
- **Intergenerational trauma: the effects are less disputed than the mechanism.** Yehuda's own paper says "**putative** role of epigenetic mechanisms." [S22] Whether *epigenetic* transmission is established in humans remains under debate, and separating **biological** from **social** transmission (parenting, narrative, ongoing structural conditions) is genuinely hard — with social transmission usually the more parsimonious account. Popular claims that ancestral trauma memory is scientifically validated outrun the evidence. [S22] — see [[concept-historical-trauma]].
- **Historical trauma is contested inside Indigenous mental health scholarship itself**, not only from outside — over whether the construct is empirically tractable and whether framing collective suffering as inherited pathology competes with structural and political explanations of health inequity. [S23] Teach it with that debate attached.
- **The terminology is not standardized.** Trauma-aware, trauma-sensitive, trauma-responsive, trauma-informed, trauma-specific — the continuum is useful, but different sources order and define these differently. [S17] Don't treat any one version as canonical, and define your terms when you use them.
