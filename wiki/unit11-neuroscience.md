# Unit 11 — Neuroscience & Biological Bases of Behavior

You are not going to be a neuroscientist, and you will never prescribe a medication. So why does a counselor need the brain? Because **clients ask.** "What's actually wrong with my brain?" "Is this a chemical imbalance?" "Should I go on medication — and if I do, will I be on it forever?" "My psychiatrist put me on an SSRI; what does that even do?" A counselor who can answer these **responsibly** — accurately, without overclaiming, and knowing exactly where their scope ends and a prescriber's begins — is doing real clinical work. A counselor who can't either goes silent or, worse, improvises medical advice they aren't licensed to give. The syllabus objective is deliberately modest and deliberately practical: build a **working mental model** of the brain and body in mental health. Enough to explain, to coordinate, and to refer. Not enough to play doctor.

This is a Tier 3 "rounding out" unit that maps onto the **biological bases** strand of CACREP's **Human Growth & Development** core area, and onto its psychopharmacology requirement — counselors are trained in the "classifications, indications, and contraindications of commonly prescribed psychopharmacological medications **for appropriate medical referrals and consultation**" [S16]. Note the last clause: the competency is for *referral and consultation*, never prescribing. It depends most on Units 6 and 8.

## The stance shift for this unit

The "resist the urge to fix" thread takes a specific and slightly paradoxical shape here. This is the unit most likely to tempt you toward the fix reflex — because biology *feels* like the level where the real machinery lives, the "actual" cause under the messy human story. The discipline is to hold the biology as **one lens among three, not the master lens.**

- **Biology is a leg of the stool, not the floor it stands on.** The [[concept-biopsychosocial-model | biopsychosocial model]] is not "biology, plus some soft stuff." Genes, neurotransmitters, meaning, relationships, and social conditions are *co-causes*, and the biological one is not automatically the most fundamental. Collapsing a person into their neurochemistry ("it's just a chemical imbalance") is **neuro-reductionism**, and it's usually both wrong (see below) and clinically deflating — it tells a client their problem is a broken part, not a life to be understood and changed.
- **You don't prescribe — you translate, coordinate, and refer.** The whole medication skill set for a counselor is: recognize the classes, know roughly what they do and their common side effects, **educate** the client enough to have an informed conversation with their prescriber, **consult** with that prescriber, and **refer** [S6][S16]. What you never do is start, stop, change, or second-guess a medication — including telling a client who feels better that they can quit. That's practicing medicine without a license and, with some drugs, dangerous (see [[concept-psychotropic-classes]]).
- **Neuroscience destigmatizes both directions.** Used well, biology *reduces* shame: naming the freeze response as an [[concept-neurobiology-of-trauma | automatic brainstem reflex]] frees a survivor from "why didn't I fight back," and knowing that **therapy itself changes the brain** ([[concept-neuroplasticity-therapy]]) dissolves the false hierarchy that puts "real" (medical) treatment above "just talking." Talking *is* biological.

## What's contested — hold these carefully

This is an area where the popular story runs well ahead of the settled science, exactly as Unit 8 warned. Read the *direction* of the findings as solid and the confident narratives as suspect.

- **The "chemical imbalance" theory of depression is not supported.** A 2022 umbrella review found **no consistent evidence** that depression is caused by low serotonin — across metabolites, receptors, the transporter, tryptophan-depletion, and gene studies [S7]. Antidepressants **still help many people** (Cipriani's network meta-analysis: all 21 beat placebo, though by **modest** margins [S6]) — but *not*, it seems, by correcting a known deficiency; the leading account is now **downstream neuroplasticity** [S8]. Hold the tension: *"chemical imbalance" is a myth; medication can still be genuinely useful.* (The review is itself contested [S7] — teach it both-sides.)
- **The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia is real but partial.** The modern version is *elevated presynaptic striatal dopamine* driving **aberrant salience**, not "too much dopamine, everywhere" — and glutamate, GABA, and others are also involved [S10]. Antipsychotics block D2 and dampen positive symptoms; they don't "cure a dopamine excess."
- **Neuroplasticity is real but overhyped.** The brain genuinely rewires with experience [S11], and therapy leverages that [S12][S13]. But "neuroplasticity" has become a marketing word for a great deal of unfounded "rewire your brain" content — and the link from a specific therapy move to a specific brain change is largely **inferential**; neural correlates are not mechanism [S14].
- **Polyvagal theory, "amygdala hijack," and left-brain/right-brain** are clinically popular metaphors whose strong neurophysiological claims are **contested or oversimplified** [S17]. Use them as bridges to clients, not as established fact (carried over from Unit 8).
