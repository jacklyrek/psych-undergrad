# Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (the toolkit)

The *worldview* behind Solution-Focused Brief Therapy — social constructionism, the re-authored story, the non-expert stance — is Unit 2's [[theory-postmodern]]. This page is the **toolkit**: the specific, unusually concrete questions a solution-focused counselor asks. SFBT is worth a counselor's attention for two reasons. First, it's **brief** (often ~5 sessions) and portable — useful in settings with session limits. Second, it's the cleanest antidote to the fix-it reflex's cousin, the **problem-saturated interview**: SFBT deliberately spends almost no time on *why* the problem exists and nearly all of it on **what the client wants instead and when that's already happening**. Developed by **Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg** at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, it was **empirically derived** — built from watching real sessions and noting which counselor moves led to progress, rather than deduced from a theory [S12].

## The founding assumptions

The techniques only make sense on top of a few premises [S12]:

- **You don't need to understand a problem to solve it.** Cause and solution are decoupled; excavating origins is optional and often wasteful.
- **The client is the expert** on their own life and already has resources and exceptions to build on.
- **Change is constant, and small change generalizes.** A tiny shift in one corner ripples outward.
- **Focus on solutions, not problems.** What you attend to grows; attend to what's working.
- The rule of thumb: **"If it works, do more of it. If it doesn't work, do something different."**

## The core toolkit

**The miracle question** — the signature move. A classic wording: *"Suppose tonight while you sleep a miracle happens and the problem that brought you here is solved. But because you were asleep, you don't know it happened. When you wake up tomorrow, what would be the first small sign that tells you things are different? What else? Who else would notice?"* [S12]. It bypasses problem analysis and elicits a **concrete, vivid, behavioral picture** of the desired future — which becomes the goal, described in the client's own detail. Experimental work finds the miracle question **reduces negative affect** more than problem-focused questions [S14].

**Exception questions** — *"Tell me about a time recently when the problem could have happened but didn't — or was a little less bad. What was different then? What were you doing?"* Exceptions are the seeds of solutions already present in the client's life. Of all SFBT techniques, the **strengths-oriented ones (exceptions, scaling) have the most empirical support** [S12][S14].

**Scaling questions** — *"On a scale of 0 to 10, where 10 is the day after the miracle and 0 is the worst it's been, where are you today?"* Then the two follow-ups that do the work: *"What makes you a [4] and not a [2]?"* (surfaces existing strengths) and *"What would a [5] look like — what's one small thing that would tell you you'd moved up half a point?"* (defines the next tiny step). Scaling turns a vague felt-sense into something observable and produces **concrete action steps** [S14].

**Coping questions** — for clients too stuck for the miracle question: *"Things sound really hard. How have you managed to keep going? How do you get through a day like that?"* It reframes bare survival as active coping and finds resources hidden inside the despair.

**Compliments and the formula-first-session task** — genuine recognition of what the client is doing right, and the classic homework: *"Between now and next time, notice what happens in your life that you'd like to keep happening."* — which primes the client to look for exceptions [S12].

## The evidence (and a caution)

SFBT has a substantial and growing base — a 2024 meta-analysis of community-based RCTs found a **large effect (g ≈ 1.17) on psychosocial functioning** [S13], and proponent tallies report positive outcomes in the large majority of ~250 studies worldwide [S12]. **But** — flag this clearly — some of the largest reported effect sizes (e.g., couples *g* ≈ 3.02, group *g* ≈ 1.64) come from **proponent sources** and are almost certainly inflated by **allegiance and publication bias**; the peer-reviewed estimates are more modest, and SFBT has *not* been shown to beat established treatments so much as to **match them in fewer sessions** [S12][S13]. Treat "began as evidence-based" as a real strength and the giant numbers with skepticism.

## The stance traps

- **Toxic solution-forcing.** Rushing a grieving or acutely distressed client to "what would be different?" can feel dismissive of real pain. Solution focus does not mean ignoring suffering — you still hear the person (Unit 1); you just don't *dwell* in problem analysis. (In crisis, the Unit 8 stance takes priority.)
- **Skipping the alliance.** The questions are so structured they can be run mechanically, which kills them. They work inside a warm, collaborative relationship, not as a questionnaire.
- **Confusing "brief" with "shallow."** Brevity is a design goal met by focus, not a promise that every client is a quick fix; some presentations need depth SFBT doesn't provide.
