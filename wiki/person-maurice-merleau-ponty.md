# Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961)

**Why he matters:** Merleau-Ponty put the **body** at the center of phenomenology. His *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) argued that perception, and with it all understanding, is the achievement of a **lived body** engaged with the world, not of a detached mind. He is the phenomenologist whom psychology and cognitive science have absorbed most readily. [S4][S5][S48]

## Life and work, briefly
- French philosopher, a contemporary and co-editor with Sartre (the two later fell out). Taught at Lyon and the Sorbonne, where he gave courses on **child psychology** (1949–52), and from 1952 held a chair at the Collège de France. [S4]
- Unusually for a phenomenologist, he **read and used empirical science**: Gestalt psychology, neurology (the Schneider case from Gelb and Goldstein), and developmental psychology. [S4]
- Key ideas: the **lived body** (*corps propre*), **body schema** vs. body image, **motor intentionality** ("I can"), **habit** and the habit-body, the **phantom limb**, the **primacy of perception**, and in his late, unfinished work the **flesh** (*la chair*): a single sensible fabric shared by the perceiver and the perceived. [S4]
- Died suddenly at 53. *The Visible and the Invisible* was published posthumously. [S4]

## How he differs
- **vs. Husserl:** the subject is a body, not a transcendental consciousness; and a complete reduction is **impossible**. [S1][S48]
- **vs. Heidegger:** takes the body seriously, which Heidegger largely does not. [S1]
- **vs. Gibson:** similar conclusions (perception is of possibilities for action), reached by philosophical description rather than experiment ([[concept-affordances]]).

## Legacy in clinical and cognitive science
Embodied and enactive cognition ([[concept-4e-cognition]]); Fuchs's phenomenological psychiatry of corporealization and disembodiment ([[concept-phenomenology-of-depression]]); body-oriented understandings of trauma. [S5][S32]
