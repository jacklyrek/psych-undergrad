# TIC as Organizational Change: The Ten Domains

The most consequential misunderstanding about trauma-informed care is that it is something a clinician does. It is mostly something an **organization** is, and the part you personally control is the smaller part. SAMHSA is explicit: implementing a trauma-informed approach **requires change at every level of an organization**. [S1]

This matters practically for two reasons. You will be inducted into somebody's TIC initiative and should know what a real one looks like versus a training day and a poster. And you will at some point work in a system that is *not* trauma-informed, and need to know what is still available to you.

## The ten implementation domains

SAMHSA's framework pairs its six principles with **ten implementation domains** — the places an organization has to actually change: [S1][S16]

1. **Governance and leadership**
2. **Policy**
3. **Physical environment**
4. **Engagement and involvement** (of clients and staff, in the change process itself)
5. **Cross-sector collaboration**
6. **Screening, assessment, and treatment services**
7. **Training and workforce development**
8. **Progress monitoring and quality assurance**
9. **Financing**
10. **Evaluation**

**Count how many an individual counselor controls.** Realistically: part of domain 6, and your own participation in 7. **Eight of the ten are structural** — budgets, policies, buildings, governance, data systems. That is the argument of this page in one observation. When a clinician says "I practice in a trauma-informed way," they are describing a stance they hold inside a system whose policies may contradict it daily.

Two domains are worth pausing on because they are the ones organizations skip:

- **Physical environment** (3) is not decoration. Lighting, sightlines, whether a person can see the exit, whether the waiting room is exposed, whether someone can be overheard at a check-in desk, whether the space is predictable. These map directly onto [[concept-retraumatization]] — an environment that cannot be anticipated or controlled is the thing being avoided.
- **Engagement and involvement** (4) means clients and frontline staff participate in designing the change. A trauma-informed approach imposed on staff from above, without their voice, **re-enacts the powerlessness it claims to oppose** — which is a real and frequently-noted irony, and one reason initiatives fail.

**Financing (9) and evaluation (10)** are the ones that separate a genuine initiative from a training day: if nobody paid for it and nobody is measuring it, it is a value statement.

SAMHSA's own 2023 *Practical Guide* exists because the 2014 framework proved hard to operationalize [S3] — worth knowing as evidence that the implementation problem is recognized by the framework's authors, not just its critics.

## The continuum

Organizations sit somewhere on a progression, commonly rendered as: [S17][S18]

| Stage | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Trauma-aware** | Basic understanding that trauma is widespread and affects people |
| **Trauma-sensitive** | That understanding begins to shape language, attitudes, and interactions |
| **Trauma-responsive** | Practices actively change; **universal screening** with trauma-specific assessment and treatment offered to those who need it; ongoing adjustment |
| **Trauma-informed** | The approach is embedded across policy, environment, workforce, and governance — the full domain set |
| **Trauma-specific** | **Clinical treatments** for trauma symptoms — PE, CPT, TF-CBT, EMDR — delivered within that frame |

Two cautions, both important.

**The terminology is not standardized.** [S17] Different sources order and define these differently — some place "trauma-responsive" above "trauma-informed," others below; some treat "aware" and "sensitive" as one stage. Do not treat any version as canonical; define your terms when you use them. This inconsistency is not trivial — it is part of why the evidence base is hard to synthesize ([[concept-tic-evidence]]). [S24]

**Trauma-informed and trauma-specific are different objects, not adjacent stages.** Trauma-informed/responsive describe **organizational philosophy and practice**; trauma-specific denotes **clinical treatments** delivered inside them. [S17][S18] This is the organizational-scale version of the informed-vs-treatment distinction in [[concept-trauma-informed-care]], and it fails the same way: an agency that trains everyone in trauma awareness has not thereby acquired anyone who can deliver CPT.

## Workforce: the domain that includes you

Training and workforce development (7) is where trauma-informed care turns back on the organization's own staff, and it covers more than teaching people the six principles. [S2] It includes hiring and supervision practices, whether staff have enough control over their own work, whether the organization responds to workplace incidents in a trauma-informed way, and — centrally — whether it takes **vicarious trauma** seriously as an occupational reality rather than an individual weakness.

This is Unit 8's [[concept-vicarious-trauma]] and [[concept-counselor-self-care]] reframed as an **organizational obligation** instead of a personal one. The distinction is not cosmetic. An agency that tells staff to practice self-care while assigning unmanageable trauma caseloads with no supervision has located a structural problem in the individual — which is, again, the framework's own critique applied to the framework's own institutions. If you assess a prospective employer's TIC credentials on one thing, make it this: **how are they treating the people doing the work?**

## What you can do in a system that isn't trauma-informed

The realistic case, and the one worth planning for. You control less than the framework implies, but not nothing:

- **Your own defaults** — explain before you act, offer choice wherever choice exists, keep your commitments, don't ambush, don't press. None of that requires permission ([[concept-retraumatization]]).
- **Your own room** — predictability, seating, where the door is, how sessions start and end.
- **How you hand people to other parts of the system** — preparing someone for what an intake, a hospital, or a court process will be like restores some predictability even when you cannot change the process. This is the counselor's version of what a rape crisis advocate does against secondary victimization ([[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]]).
- **Naming it accurately in supervision** — a policy that routinely re-traumatizes clients is a clinical problem and can be raised as one.
- **Honest limits.** You cannot single-handedly make an organization trauma-informed, and burning out trying is a well-documented path. Know which domain a problem lives in before deciding whether it is yours to solve.
