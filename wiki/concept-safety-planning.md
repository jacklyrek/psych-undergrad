# Collaborative Safety Planning

Once [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | risk is assessed]], the question becomes *what do we build together so this person survives the next crisis?* The answer is a **safety plan**, and the answer is emphatically **not** a "no-suicide contract." The **Safety Planning Intervention (SPI)**, developed by Barbara Stanley and Gregory Brown, is a brief, **collaborative, clinician-assisted** written plan: a prioritized list of coping strategies and supports the person can move through when a suicidal crisis begins to build [S6]. It is one of the most evidence-supported brief interventions in suicide prevention — in a study of emergency-department patients, the SPI plus structured follow-up calls was associated with **~45% fewer suicidal behaviors** over six months versus usual care [S7], and it is endorsed by the Joint Commission, the VA/DoD guideline, and the Zero Suicide framework.

## The six steps, in order

The order is the point. The plan moves from **what the person can do entirely alone** toward **external and professional help**, so the earliest steps are always available even when no one else is [S6]:

1. **Warning signs.** The personal thoughts, images, moods, and behaviors that signal a crisis is starting — in the client's own words ("when I start replaying the breakup and stop answering texts"). Naming these is what tells the person *when* to open the plan.
2. **Internal coping strategies.** Things the client can do **on their own, without contacting anyone**, to ride out and distract from the urge — walk, cold water, music, exercise, a specific show. These come first because they build the sense that *I can get through a wave myself.*
3. **Social contacts and settings for distraction.** People and places that take the mind off the crisis — a sibling to text about nothing, a coffee shop, a gym — **without** disclosing the suicidal thoughts. The job here is distraction and connection, not disclosure.
4. **People to ask for help.** Family or friends the client *will actually tell* they're in crisis. This is the first step that involves disclosing the crisis to someone.
5. **Professionals and agencies to contact in a crisis.** The counselor, the local emergency department, and the **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** (call or text 988) — with names and numbers written down, because a person in crisis will not go looking for them.
6. **Making the environment safe.** Reducing access to lethal means — the [[concept-means-reduction | means-restriction]] step. For highly lethal means (especially firearms), this is done *with responsible others*, not left to the person alone.

## Why it is built *with* the client, not handed to them

A safety plan photocopied and handed over is nearly worthless. The intervention *is* the collaborative conversation: eliciting the person's own warning signs, testing whether each coping strategy is realistic ("would you actually call your brother, or would you not want to worry him?"), and troubleshooting the barriers. A strategy the client didn't help choose is a strategy they won't use at 2 a.m. The plan is also a **living document** — revisited and revised as circumstances and risk change, not a one-time form [S6]. And it dovetails with the ideation-to-action logic: steps 3–4 raise **connectedness** and step 6 lowers **practical capability**, which are exactly the two things you can change tonight ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]]).

## Safety plan vs. no-suicide contract — the discrimination that matters

Beginners (and older training materials) reach for the **"no-suicide contract"** — a client's verbal or written *promise* not to harm themselves. **Drop it.** The two look superficially similar and are opposites in what they do [S7]:

| | No-suicide contract | Safety plan (SPI) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What it asks** | a **promise** ("I won't kill myself") | a **plan** ("here's what I'll *do* when the urge comes") |
| **Who it serves** | the clinician's anxiety / liability | the client's survival |
| **Evidence** | **none** that it reduces suicide or attempts | RCT/cohort support for reduced suicidal behavior |
| **Failure mode** | **false reassurance**; defensive, not therapeutic; gives the client nothing to do | requires ongoing collaboration and revision |

A contract extracts a promise and offers nothing in return; when the crisis comes, the person has a broken promise and still no plan. Worse, it can lull the clinician into thinking the work is done. A **commitment to safety** is fine as a stance — but the deliverable is the *plan*, the concrete list of what the client will do and whom they will reach.

## The stance traps

- **Handing over a form.** If you filled it out and gave it to them, you skipped the intervention. The value is in the co-construction.
- **Skipping means reduction to avoid an awkward conversation.** Step 6 is the step most likely to save a life and the one most often rushed or dropped ([[concept-means-reduction]]).
- **Confusing a promise with a plan.** "They contracted for safety" is not a safety plan. Ask: does the client know what to *do*, and whom to *call*, when the warning signs start?
- **One and done.** Risk changes; the plan has to change with it.
