# Confidentiality and Its Limits

**The idea:** counseling works because clients can say anything. The Supreme Court put it plainly:
effective psychotherapy "depends upon an atmosphere of confidence and trust," and "the mere
possibility of disclosure may impede development of the confidential relationship necessary for
successful treatment." [S2] Confidentiality is therefore the profession's central promise — and
because it is not absolute, you must know its limits **cold** and tell clients about them up front.

## Three words that aren't synonyms

| Term                         | What it is                                                                            | Who enforces it                                            |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Privacy**                  | the client's underlying *right* to control access to themselves and their information | constitutional/common-law right                            |
| **Confidentiality**          | the professional's *ethical duty* not to disclose what the client reveals (B.1.c)     | ethics code, licensing board                               |
| **Privileged communication** | the *legal* protection against forced disclosure **in court**                         | evidence law; belongs to the **client**, not the counselor |

Privilege is the narrowest: it only matters in legal proceedings, it is created by statute or case
law, and the client (not you) holds it — the client can waive it; you can't. *Jaffee v. Redmond*
(1996) established the psychotherapist–patient privilege in **federal** courts, explicitly
including licensed clinical social workers; states have their own privilege statutes, which vary. [S2]

## The promise (ACA Code, B.1)

- **B.1.b:** request private information only when it benefits the counseling. [S1]
- **B.1.c:** "Counselors disclose information only with appropriate consent or with sound legal or
  ethical justification." [S1]
- **B.1.d:** at initiation **and throughout**, inform clients of the limitations — and flag
  foreseeable situations where confidentiality must be breached. [S1]

## The exceptions (B.2 — the part to know cold)

1. **Serious and foreseeable harm** (B.2.a) — disclosure "required to protect clients or identified
   others from serious and foreseeable harm." Covers **danger to self** (suicide — see Unit 8) and
   **danger to others** ([[concept-duty-to-warn]]). Note the 2014 wording: *serious and foreseeable*, not "imminent" — a deliberate change to a more workable standard. When in doubt, **consult**. [S1]
2. **Legal requirements** (B.2.a) — chiefly **mandated reporting** of suspected abuse/neglect of
   children and vulnerable adults ([[concept-mandated-reporting]]). [S1][S5]
3. **Contagious, life-threatening diseases** (B.2.c) — *may* (not must) disclose to an identifiable
   third party at serious, foreseeable risk of contracting the disease, after assessing whether the
   client intends to inform them; state laws govern. [S1]
4. **Court-ordered disclosure** (B.2.d) — first seek the client's written informed consent, or take
   steps to **prohibit or narrow** the disclosure as much as possible. [S1]
5. **End-of-life decisions** (B.2.b) — counselors working with terminally ill clients considering
   hastening death have the *option* to maintain confidentiality, within applicable law and after
   consultation. [S1]

Plus the quieter, everyday "exceptions" clients should know about: supervision and interdisciplinary
treatment teams (B.3.b), subordinates/office staff (B.3.a), third-party payers (B.3.d — insurers
require a diagnosis), and consultation. These aren't breaches; they're the ordinary plumbing of
care — but consent should cover them. [S1]

**The overriding rule: minimal disclosure (B.2.e).** When you must disclose, clients are (where
possible) told beforehand and involved in the decision, and **"only essential information is
revealed."** You breach the *seal*, never the whole vault — you tell the necessary person the
necessary fact, not the client's history. [S1]

## The confidentiality speech (the syllabus practice rep — worked model)

Write yours first, then compare:

MINE:
	Let's go over confidentiality. The main idea is that I can't tell anyone anything you say, unless there are legal or ethical concerns. Those concerns are narrow, but pretty understandable: if you present a clear threat to your own or someone else's life, then I have to report that. If you are neglecting or abusing children or special needs adults, I need to report that. If I am asked to testify in court, I will ask you for permission and then say whatever you have allowed me to say with the above exceptions in mind. Any time that I would disclose something, I always keep the details as narrow as possible. Only giving out information that is absolutely necessary. This is all so that you can feel confident being completely honest with me, which is the only way this is gonna work.

> "One more thing before we start, and it's important. What you say in this room stays in this
> room. I don't tell your family, your employer, or anyone else that you're even here. There are a
> few exceptions, and I'd rather you hear them now than be surprised later. If I believe you're in
> real danger of seriously hurting yourself, or someone else is in danger from you, I'm required to
> act to keep people safe — and I'd involve you in that as much as I possibly could. If you tell me
> a child or a vulnerable adult is being abused or neglected, the law requires me to report that.
> And in rare cases a judge can order me to release records — if that ever happened I'd fight to
> keep it as narrow as possible. Also, I meet with a supervisor to make sure you're getting good
> care, and if you use insurance, they require a diagnosis. That's the whole list. Everything else
> is between us. What questions do you have?"

Notice what makes it work: plain words, no legalese, the *reason* for each exception, the promise
to involve the client, and an invitation to ask. Compare the failure mode — mumbling through
"there-are-limits-to-confidentiality-per-state-law" at speed because the topic is awkward. Clients
hear the discomfort, not the content. Saying it calmly is a skill; rep it out loud. [S1][S4]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **How much to say up front.** Exhaustive warnings would be "time prohibitive and unnecessarily
  frightening," and clients don't retain them anyway — hence: a clear summary at intake, revisited
  when a limit becomes live. Some forensic writers (Darby & Weinstock's "dialectical principlism")
  even argue full re-warning mid-crisis can be ethically withheld when safety demands honest risk
  assessment; that's a debated position, not settled doctrine. [S4]
- **Does warning clients chill disclosure?** The worry is real but the evidence is mixed; the field
  has settled on candor because discovering an unstated exception *after* a breach is far worse for
  trust. [S4]
- **Privilege varies by state and by profession** — who is covered (LPCs? interns?) and what
  exceptions apply (the "dangerous patient" carve-out) differ by jurisdiction. [S2][S3]
