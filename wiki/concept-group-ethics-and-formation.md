# Group Ethics & Formation

A large share of a group's outcome is decided **before the first session** — in who is selected, how they are prepared, and what norms are set. This is also where group work departs most sharply from individual ethics (Unit 3), because a group has a feature a dyad does not: **other clients in the room**. That one fact reshapes confidentiality, consent, and the counselor's protective duty. The **ASGW Best Practice Guidelines** organize the whole job into three phases — **planning, performing, and processing** — and the two that beginners underweight are the bookends: planning (formation) and processing (reflection) [S10].

## Screening and selection (the pre-group task)

Both the **ACA Code (A.8)** and **ASGW** require the counselor to **screen prospective members** [S10][S11]. The standard: select members whose **needs and goals are compatible with the group's purpose**, who **will not impede the group process**, and **whose well-being will not be jeopardized** by the group experience [S10]. Screening is a two-way fit judgment — it protects the *candidate* (a fragile or acutely-in-crisis person may be harmed by, or unable to use, a particular group) and it protects the *group* (a member whose needs are radically mismatched can derail it, and the wrong mix produces the "difficult member" dynamics of [[concept-difficult-group-members]]). The intensity of screening **scales with the depth of the group** ([[concept-group-types]]): light for a psychoeducational class, careful and clinical for a reconstructive psychotherapy group.

Composition is part of selection: **homogeneous** groups (members share a problem — e.g., a bereavement group) cohere faster and normalize a specific struggle; **heterogeneous** groups offer a richer social microcosm and more varied feedback. Neither is universally right — the choice follows the group's aim.

## Preparation: the pre-group meeting

Yalom's research is emphatic and often-skipped: **pre-group preparation improves outcomes and reduces dropout** [S2]. Before the group starts, meet with members to demystify the process — explain how group works, what to expect (including that early sessions can feel awkward and that conflict is normal — [[concept-group-development-stages]]), correct misconceptions ("I'll just be talked at" / "I'll be forced to bare my soul"), set realistic expectations, and establish norms. Prepared members drop out less and engage sooner. Formation is not paperwork; it is the first therapeutic act.

## Informed consent in groups

Group informed consent ([[concept-informed-consent]]) carries everything individual consent does — purpose, risks and benefits, the leader's qualifications, voluntary participation and the right to leave — **plus group-specific elements**: the norms and expectations, the risks *unique to groups* (exposure to others' distress, the possibility of conflict or of feeling pressured), and, above all, the **limits of confidentiality**. A special case is the **involuntary or mandated member** (court-ordered groups are common in addiction and domestic-violence work): they must still be told what is and isn't required, what will be reported to the referring body, and what genuine choices remain — consent is meaningful even when attendance isn't optional.

## The confidentiality limit that is unique to groups

This is the single most tested point in group ethics, because it inverts an individual-therapy assumption. In individual therapy the counselor controls confidentiality (within its legal limits — Unit 3, [[concept-confidentiality-limits]]). In a group, **the other members are not bound by the counselor's ethics code** — and the counselor **cannot guarantee** that they will keep what they hear private.

The **ACA Code B.4.a** states it precisely [S11]:

> "In group work, counselors clearly explain the importance and parameters of confidentiality for the specific group being entered."

Parse what that does and doesn't promise. The counselor's duty is to **explain** confidentiality's importance, **set it as an explicit group norm**, describe the **parameters** (what confidentiality means here, why it matters, the consequences of breaching it), and often have members agree to it — but the counselor can only promise *their own* confidentiality, not the members'. Honesty about this limit is itself part of consent: members decide what to disclose knowing the room is not sealed. A further legal wrinkle: **privilege** (the legal protection that can keep therapy communications out of court) **generally does not apply to group treatment** — the presence of third parties can waive it — unless a specific state statute preserves it [S10]. So the honest framing to members is: *"I will keep what's said here confidential and I'll ask the same of everyone, and we'll treat it as a firm expectation — but I can't guarantee others will, and the law may not protect it the way it protects one-on-one therapy."*

## The protective duty

Because a group can harm — the encounter-group **casualty** finding ([[concept-group-leadership]]) is the evidence — ACA (A.8.b) charges the counselor to **take reasonable precautions to protect members from physical, emotional, or psychological trauma** [S11]. In practice: screen out foreseeable bad fits, set and enforce safety norms, intervene when a member is being scapegoated or attacked ([[concept-difficult-group-members]]), and keep the group's intensity within tolerable bounds (the moderate-not-maximal stimulation finding). Protection is not softness; it is the floor that makes the risk-taking of real group work safe enough to attempt.

## Processing (the underweighted back end)

The third ASGW phase, **processing**, closes the loop: the leader reflects on each session (ideally with a co-leader or supervisor), tracks each member's trajectory, and attends to endings — helping members consolidate gains and **transfer learning** out of the group ([[concept-group-development-stages]] final stage). Skipping processing is how groups drift and how casualties go unnoticed.
