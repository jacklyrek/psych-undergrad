# The Biopsychosocial Model

**The idea:** a person's health — mental and physical — emerges from **biological, psychological, and social** factors interacting, and "the person suffers as a whole and not as isolated organs." George Engel proposed it in a 1977 *Science* paper, *The Need for a New Medical Model*, as a corrective to the reigning **biomedical model**. [S5] For a counselor it's less a theory than the **default case-formulation frame**: when you sit with a client, you deliberately look across all three domains instead of collapsing the problem into any one.

## What Engel was reacting against

Engel argued the biomedical model rested on two flawed philosophical commitments and therefore left "no room within its framework for the social, psychological, and behavioral dimensions of illness": [S4][S5]

- **Reductionism** (and physicalism) — reducing complex illness to a single physical/chemical primary cause. Depression = a serotonin problem, full stop.
- **Mind–body dualism** — treating mind and body as separate, with only the material body as causally real.

Against this he set a model grounded in **general systems theory**: biology, psychology, and the social world are **nested, interacting levels** that reciprocally influence one another and health. Change one level and the others move. This is the same systems logic behind [[theory-family-systems]] — the problem lives in the interactions, not in one isolated part.

## The three domains, for a clinical formulation

A biopsychosocial formulation of *this client's* problem asks across all three:

- **Bio** — genetics, temperament, medical conditions, medication, sleep, substance use, the brain and nervous system (the eventual subject of Unit 11, neuroscience).
- **Psycho** — thoughts, beliefs, coping style, emotion regulation, personality, **attachment history** ([[theory-attachment]]), the internal working models and Erikson-stage tasks this unit supplies.
- **Social** — family, relationships, work, culture, socioeconomic conditions, discrimination, community and access to care (the terrain of [[unit04-multicultural]]).

A useful extension many clinicians add is **spiritual/existential** (meaning, faith, purpose) — sometimes written "bio-psycho-social-spiritual." Done well, the model is what keeps you from the counselor's version of reductionism: reading a client's distress as *only* their thinking, or *only* their chemistry, or *only* their circumstances.

## Why it's a Unit 5 anchor

Human development is inherently biopsychosocial — a maturing body, a developing mind, and a shaping social world moving together — so this model is the **integrating frame** that holds attachment, Erikson, and Piaget in one picture rather than three competing lists. It's also the on-ramp to [[unit07-assessment | Unit 7]] (assessment): the [[concept-intake-interview | intake]]'s **biopsychosocial history** is this model turned into an interview structure.

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

The biopsychosocial model is simultaneously **indispensable and heavily criticized** — hold both. [S4]

- **Vague / unfalsifiable.** Nassir Ghaemi: it "gives mental health professionals permission to do everything but no specific guidance" — ideology dressed as theory.
- **A "slogan," not a model.** Kenneth Kendler: it was "never … a scientific model or even a philosophically coherent model … It was a slogan" — while still useful as a *teaching* device.
- **"Equal in all cases" is wrong.** Asserting bio, psycho, and social matter equally *everywhere* contradicts conditions where biology alone essentially explains the outcome.

**The revival / how to use it well:** don't treat it as an explanation by itself. Treat it as an organizing **checklist you must make specific to this client** — noting that domains carry different weight for different problems and at different stages (social factors may dominate a problem's origin while biology dominates acute treatment). Its real job is to force you to theorize the *interactions* between domains, not to claim they all weigh the same. [S4]
