# Suicide Risk Assessment

The single most important reframe in this whole page: **you are assessing risk in order to *manage* it, not to *predict* who will die.** Individual suicide is not reliably predictable — no instrument or risk-factor list forecasts who acts and when — and treating assessment as prophecy leads both to false confidence and to defensive practice [S1][S5]. This is the same **prediction critique** the [[study-tarasoff]] reading raised about dangerousness prediction: the tools stratify *populations* and point you toward the right *intervention and disposition*, and that is enough to do the job [S3]. The goal is a clinical decision — how closely to watch, what to remove, where the person spends tonight — grounded in a direct, humane conversation.

## First: ask directly, and don't flinch

The beginner's fear is that naming suicide will "put the idea in someone's head." The evidence is clear that it does not: reviews across community, adolescent, adult, and at-risk samples find **no increase in ideation from being asked** — and some find that talking about it **reduces** distress [S4]. The real danger is *not* asking. So ask plainly and non-anxiously:

> *"Are you thinking about killing yourself?"* — or *"Sometimes when people feel this bad, they think about ending their life. Are you having thoughts like that?"*

Euphemisms ("hurting yourself," "doing something silly") blur the very thing you need to know. Say the word, then be quiet and let the answer land.

## Ideation vs. behavior — and the ideation ladder

The **Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** organizes the assessment around a distinction beginners collapse: **suicidal ideation is not suicidal behavior**, and each is graded separately [S1].

**Ideation runs on a five-point severity ladder** of increasing seriousness:
1. **Wish to be dead** ("I wish I wouldn't wake up") — passive.
2. **Nonspecific active suicidal thoughts** ("I've thought about killing myself") — no method.
3. **Active ideation with methods** considered — but no plan or intent.
4. **Active ideation with some intent** to act.
5. **Active ideation with a specific plan *and* intent.**

The jump from **passive** ("I wish I were dead") to **active** ("I've thought about how") is a meaningful clinical line — but passive ideation is not "safe," it's the start of the conversation. Beyond ideation you assess **intensity** (frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, reasons for dying/living) and, separately, **behavior**: actual attempts, **interrupted** attempts (stopped by an outside circumstance), **aborted/self-interrupted** attempts (stopped by the person themselves), and **preparatory acts** (acquiring means, giving possessions away, writing a note, researching methods) [S1]. Preparatory behavior with no "attempt" yet is a serious sign, not a reassurance.

The practical drill (from the syllabus practice rep) is to have a plain sequence you can run without notes: **ideation → plan → intent → means/access → preparation → history of past attempts.** A past attempt is the **single strongest long-term risk factor** [S3].

## The CASE Approach: how to elicit intent

Shea's **Chronological Assessment of Suicide Events (CASE Approach)** is a way of *organizing* the interview so you miss less and uncover more real intent. It walks four **chronological regions** in order [S2]:

1. **Presenting** ideation/events — the crisis that brought them in today.
2. **Recent** ideation/events — the past ~8 weeks.
3. **Past** ideation/events — lifetime history.
4. **Immediate** ideation — how they feel about safety *right now*, and what happens next.

Its central insight is the gap between **stated intent** (what the client tells you) and **reflected intent** (how much thinking, planning, and action they describe — which often reveals more than they'll directly admit) and **withheld intent** (what they're hiding). A person who says "not really" but describes researching methods, giving things away, and writing letters is showing you reflected intent that outweighs the stated denial. Techniques like asking for the **behavioral incident** (facts and sequence, not opinions — "what did you do next?"), **gentle assumption** ("what other ways have you thought about?"), and **normalization** lower the barrier to honest disclosure [S2].

## Risk factors vs. warning signs vs. protective factors

This is the confusable trio at the heart of the cluster — and they answer different questions.

| | What it is | Time frame | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Risk factor** | a standing characteristic that raises *baseline, longer-term* likelihood | **chronic / distal** | prior attempt, psychiatric disorder, substance use, trauma history, access to firearms, chronic pain, male sex, isolation |
| **Warning sign** | an observable indicator that risk is elevated *right now* | **acute / proximal** | talking/writing about death, giving things away, a sudden calm after despair, saying goodbye, seeking means |
| **Protective factor** | a characteristic that *lowers* likelihood | buffering | reasons for living, children/responsibility, social connectedness, engaged treatment, coping skills, beliefs discouraging suicide |

**Warning signs are about the near term; risk factors are about the background.** A person can carry many chronic risk factors for years at a stable baseline, and it's the *acute* change — the warning signs, the precipitant — that signals danger in the coming hours to days [S3]. The VA/American Association of Suicidology mnemonic for warning signs is **IS PATH WARM**: **I**deation, **S**ubstance abuse, **P**urposelessness, **A**nxiety/agitation, **T**rapped, **H**opelessness, **W**ithdrawal, **A**nger, **R**ecklessness, **M**ood change [S3]. Protective factors matter but do not cancel acute risk — a devoted parent in an acute crisis is still in an acute crisis.

## Ideation-to-action: why thinking about it and acting on it are different problems

The most useful modern frame is the **ideation-to-action framework**: the causes of suicidal *ideation* are **distinct** from the causes of the *progression* from ideation to an *attempt* [S5]. This reorganizes the whole assessment.

- **Why people come to think about suicide.** In Joiner's **Interpersonal Theory**, ideation grows from **thwarted belongingness** ("I'm alone") plus **perceived burdensomeness** ("they'd be better off without me"). In Klonsky & May's **Three-Step Theory (3ST)**, ideation = **psychological pain + hopelessness**, and **connectedness** is what keeps moderate ideation from escalating.
- **Why some act on it.** Both theories converge on **capability**: the move from wanting to doing requires overcoming the powerful, wired-in fear of death and pain. **Acquired capability** builds through exposure to painful and provocative experiences — prior self-injury, abuse, combat, others' suicides. Practical capability includes **access to means**.

The clinical payoff: **a client with intense ideation but low capability is in a different situation than one who has rehearsed, has access, and has habituated to the fear** — and it tells you where to intervene. You can't quickly change someone's pain history, but you *can* raise connectedness (safety planning, follow-up) and lower practical capability (means reduction) tonight. This is why the two things you *do* after assessing risk are [[concept-safety-planning]] and [[concept-means-reduction]].

## From assessment to disposition

Risk is stratified to guide the decision, not to generate a number [S3]:

- **Low** (ideation without plan/intent, few acute factors, protective factors intact): outpatient care, a collaborative **safety plan**, means-safety conversation, follow-up.
- **Moderate** (active ideation, some intent/planning, mounting warning signs): intensified outpatient or crisis services, increased contact/monitoring, **remove means**, involve supports.
- **High** (persistent ideation with plan and intent, preparatory behavior, acute crisis): **do not leave the person alone**, immediate means removal, and hospitalization or emergency evaluation.

And this is where Unit 3 becomes non-optional: **imminent danger to self is a limit of confidentiality** ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]]) — you can and must act, including breaking confidentiality to protect life, and you should have told the client this was a limit during informed consent.

## The stance traps

- **Freezing / deflecting.** The hardest part is staying in the conversation. Changing the subject or rushing to "it'll be okay" abandons the person at the exact moment they risked telling you.
- **False reassurance and premature problem-solving.** "You have so much to live for" argues with the pain instead of understanding it. Sit in it first; the connection *is* an intervention.
- **Treating the screen as the assessment.** A PHQ-9 item-9 endorsement or a C-SSRS score is a *trip-wire and a structure*, not a substitute for the clinical conversation ([[concept-screening-tools]]).
- **Assessing to predict.** If you catch yourself trying to decide "will they or won't they," reset to "how do I manage this risk and keep them safe." Prediction is the wrong job.
