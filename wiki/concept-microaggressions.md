# Microaggressions (and the Debate About Them)

**The idea:** Sue et al. (2007), in *American Psychologist*, defined racial microaggressions as
"brief and commonplace daily verbal, behavioral, or environmental indignities, whether intentional
or **unintentional**, that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative racial slights and
insults." Three features do the conceptual work: they are *small* (each instance deniable), they
are *cumulative* (the cost is the drip, not the drop), and they are usually delivered by people
who believe themselves unprejudiced — which is what connects this page to
[[concept-privilege-power]]'s invisibility problem. [S6]

## The taxonomy (the testable core)

| Type | Definition | Intent | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Microassault** | explicit, old-fashioned derogation — name-calling, deliberate exclusion, displaying hostile symbols | usually **conscious/deliberate** | using a slur; refusing service |
| **Microinsult** | subtle rudeness or insensitivity that **demeans** identity | often unconscious | "You're so articulate." (to a Black professional — the compliment contains the low expectation) |
| **Microinvalidation** | communications that **negate or nullify** the person's experiential reality | often unconscious | "I don't see color." · "Anyone can succeed here if they work hard." · "Where are you *really* from?" |

Discrimination hooks: microassault = closest to overt racism (the *intent* is there);
microinsult = *demeans* (an insult wearing a compliment's clothes); microinvalidation = *erases*
(tells the person their reality didn't happen). The third is the counselor's occupational hazard —
"I treat everyone the same" and premature reassurance ("I'm sure they didn't mean anything by it")
are both microinvalidations available in-session, with a warm voice. [S6]

## In the room: rupture and repair

Sue et al.'s paper is subtitled *Implications for Clinical Practice* because the clinical claim is
the sharp one: microaggressions occur **inside therapy**, and each one is an alliance rupture
([[concept-therapeutic-alliance]]) with a client who may already be deciding whether counseling is
safe — one plausible contributor to the minority dropout gap ([[unit04-multicultural]]).
[S6][S15]

The skill is repair without self-recentering ([[concept-cultural-humility]]'s other-oriented
stance). A client says, "When you said my standards for my kids were 'maybe a little intense,' it
felt like you were writing off how our family does education":

> **Counselor A (defensive/invalidating):** "Oh — that's not what I meant at all! I'm honestly the
> least judgmental person about parenting styles. I just meant intense in a good way!" (Denies,
> recenters the counselor's innocence, and — by explaining the client's reaction away — commits a
> microinvalidation *while apologizing for one*.)

> **Counselor B (repair):** "Thank you for telling me — I can see that landed as a judgment on
> your family's values, and I'm sorry. I think I was measuring against my own defaults without
> noticing. Can you tell me more about what those standards mean in your family?" (Owns it briefly,
> names the mechanism, returns the floor.)

Ruptures repaired this way can *strengthen* the alliance — the client learns difference is
discussable here, which is broaching's promise kept under pressure ([[concept-privilege-power]]).
[S6][S10]

## The critique (know this side too)

Lilienfeld (2017), *Perspectives on Psychological Science*: the microaggression research program
makes **strong claims on inadequate evidence**. The load-bearing objections: definitions are
**elastic** (almost anything can qualify post hoc); classification rests on **subjective report
alone** (no observer-independent criteria); the "**aggression**" label imputes motives the data
can't show; members of the same minority group *disagree* about whether a given remark is
offensive; and the causal claim (microaggressions → mental-health harm) rests on correlational,
self-report designs. His proposed fix: the more neutral term **"perceived racial slight"** and a
moratorium on strong claims pending better research. Notably, **Sue's rejoinder conceded many of
these points** while defending the value of documenting lived experience; Williams (2019) answered
Lilienfeld point-by-point, and the argument continues in print. [S12]

How to hold both: the *phenomenon* (subtle, cumulative, deniable slights that wear on people) is
well-attested experience and a real clinical concern; the *research program's* strong causal and
taxonomic claims outrun current evidence. In session, the epistemology debate barely matters —
if your client experienced your remark as a slight, the alliance took the hit regardless of what
the construct is named, and repair is the same move either way. In a paper, cite both sides.
[S6][S12]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- Everything in the section above — this is the unit's flagship live controversy. A program will
  expect the taxonomy; a good program will also expect Lilienfeld. [S6][S12]
- **Base rates and thresholds** — how common, how harmful, and where slight ends and
  microaggression begins are all unsettled empirically. [S12]
