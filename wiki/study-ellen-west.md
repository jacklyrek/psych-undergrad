# The Case of Ellen West

> **Content note:** this case involves an eating disorder and a completed suicide. It is included because it is the most famous case in phenomenological psychiatry and a standard ethics teaching case, not for its drama. Kept clinical.

**One line:** Binswanger's case study of "Ellen West" (a pseudonym) is the showpiece of existential analysis, a detailed reconstruction of one woman's world-design. It is also a cautionary tale. Later archival work suggests she was **misdiagnosed** and that her clinicians **discharged her knowing she would probably kill herself**. Binswanger then wrote that death up as the fulfillment of her existence. [S15][S19][S20]

## The facts (as far as they're established)

- **Who:** a Jewish woman (1888–1921) with a long history of what Binswanger called melancholia and of disordered eating: dread of becoming fat, severe restriction, then compulsive bingeing, with purging and heavy laxative use. [S15][S20]
- **Treatment history:** two psychoanalyses before Binswanger. She was admitted to Binswanger's **Bellevue sanatorium in Kreuzlingen** from January to March 1921. [S20]
- **Diagnosis:** Binswanger diagnosed **schizophrenia** (a progressive "schizophrenia simplex"). He consulted **Eugen Bleuler**, who concurred, and **Alfred Hoche**. Hoche is notorious as co-author of a 1920 tract arguing for the killing of "life unworthy of life." [S20]
- **Decision:** the consultants agreed that no treatment promised cure. Therapy was suspended, and **despite her explicit suicidal intentions she was discharged** home with her husband's consent. [S20]
- **Death:** three days later, after a day on which she ate freely, walked, read poetry, and wrote letters, and was reported to be calm and even happy, she took a lethal dose of poison and died. [S15][S20]
- **Publication:** Binswanger published the case in 1944–45. The English translation appeared in May, Angel and Ellenberger's *Existence* (1958), which made it the best-known existential-analytic case in the English-speaking world. [S15]

## What Binswanger argued

Binswanger read the case through his three worlds ([[theory-daseinsanalysis]]). [S15]
- Ellen's existence was split between an **ethereal world** of lightness, spirit, and thinness, which she longed for, and a **tomb-world** of heaviness, earth, body, and appetite, which she felt imprisoned in.
- Eating became a daily confrontation with that split. Her world-design constricted until death appeared as the only way out of the tomb-world. Death is a pervasive theme in the diaries, poems, and letters Binswanger used as material. [S15]
- Most controversially, he interpreted her suicide as an act in which her existence **finally became authentically her own**. He described her calm last day as festive. [S15]

## The critiques (this is why the case is still taught)

1. **Misdiagnosis.** Read today, the documented presentation fits **anorexia nervosa, binge-eating/purging type** (with depression) far better than schizophrenia. Hirschmüller's archival edition, based on the hospital files, Binswanger's private notes, and family papers, argues exactly this. Its reporting suggests Binswanger defended the "psychotic" diagnosis partly because an incurable diagnosis made the discharge defensible. [S20] Anorexia nervosa was a known diagnosis at the time. The modern eating-disorder criteria are on [[concept-eating-disorders]].
2. **A foreseeable suicide.** The treating team discharged a patient with an announced suicidal plan. The archival reporting suggests the discharge was effectively *arranged* around that outcome, and the involvement of Hoche, a public advocate of "euthanasia" for the "incurable," makes this especially troubling. [S20] Under any modern standard of care the decision would be indefensible ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]], [[concept-safety-planning]]).
3. **Romanticizing death.** Framing a suicide as the patient's authentic self-realization treats a death that might have been prevented as a philosophical fulfillment. This is the ethical center of the case. [S19][S20]
4. **Rogers: "Ellen West, and loneliness."** Carl Rogers argued that she was treated as an **object** of diagnosis and analysis by a series of experts, and that nobody had entered her experience in a real relationship. She died, on his reading, of loneliness as much as illness. He believed a relationship offering the core conditions might have changed the outcome. [S21] ([[person-carl-rogers]], [[concept-core-conditions]].)
5. **Whose story?** Akavia's historical study shows how the published case *constructs* its subject. The archive gives a more varied picture of Ellen West than Binswanger's tragic, fated figure. [S19]

## Lessons for a counselor

- **Describing someone's world is not the same as meeting them in it.** A brilliant phenomenological *analysis* can coexist with a failure of *relationship*. Rogers' critique is the phenomenological stance turned back on its own practitioners ([[concept-phenomenological-stance]]).
- **Diagnostic labels carry prognoses, and prognoses shape decisions.** An "incurable psychosis" label licensed therapeutic nihilism. Diagnosis is a hypothesis, and pessimistic ones deserve extra scrutiny ([[concept-categorical-vs-dimensional]]).
- **Understanding a wish to die is not endorsing it.** You can take seriously what death *means* to a client while still acting fully on safety. Existential and phenomenological exploration never replaces risk assessment ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]]).
- **Beware theory-driven meaning-making about a client's death.** It serves the clinician's narrative, not the client.
