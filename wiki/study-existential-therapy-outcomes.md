# Does Existential Therapy Work? The Outcome Evidence

For a long time the honest answer was "we don't know." Existential therapists often argued their work *couldn't* be tested by randomized trials. That has changed partly: there is now a meta-analysis and several well-run RCTs. The evidence is **real but narrow**. It concentrates on **structured, meaning-focused programs for people with serious illness** and says little about open-ended existential psychotherapy. [S26]

## The meta-analysis: Vos, Craig & Cooper (2015)

The only meta-analysis of existential therapies as a family, published in *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*. [S26]
- **21 eligible RCTs → 15 with unique data; N = 1,792** adults.

| Type | k | Meaning in life | Psychopathology | Self-efficacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Meaning therapies** | 6 | **d = 0.65** post, **0.57** follow-up | **d = 0.47** | **d = 0.48** |
| **Supportive-expressive** | 5 | — | d = 0.20 post, 0.18 follow-up | n.s. |
| **Experiential-existential** | 2 | n.s. | n.s. | — |
| **Cognitive-existential** | 1 | n.s. | n.s. | — |

**Authors' conclusion:** "despite the small number and low quality of studies, some existential therapies appear beneficial for certain populations," with particular support for **structured interventions** that include **psychoeducation, exercises, and direct, positive discussion of meaning, with physically ill patients**. [S26]

**Read it critically:**
- The analysis used a **fixed-effect** model, which assumes one true effect across quite different therapies and populations. A random-effects model would usually give wider, more cautious intervals. [S26]
- "No significant effect" for experiential-existential therapy rests on **two studies**. That is **absence of evidence**, not evidence of no effect.
- The authors are **existential therapy researchers**. That doesn't invalidate the work, but it is the kind of allegiance worth noting ([[concept-evidence-based-practice]]).

## The key trials

**Meaning-Centered Group Psychotherapy (MCGP): Breitbart et al., 2015.** [S27]
- Built on Frankl's sources of meaning: 8 weekly sessions, groups of 6–8, covering meaning, identity before and after the cancer diagnosis, historical, attitudinal, creative, and experiential meaning, and hope. [S32]
- **N = 253** with advanced cancer, randomized to MCGP vs. **supportive group psychotherapy**, an **active** control that shares the nonspecific ingredients of group support.
- Completers (≥3 sessions): MCGP did better on **spiritual well-being, quality of life, depression, hopelessness, desire for hastened death,** and physical symptom distress. **Anxiety did not differ.**
- **Intent-to-treat** (everyone randomized): advantages held only for **quality of life, depression, and hopelessness**.
- *Why it matters:* beating an active group-support control suggests the **meaning content** adds something beyond "being in a supportive group."

**CALM (Managing Cancer And Living Meaningfully): Rodin et al., 2018.** [S28]
- Brief individual therapy (3–6 sessions + boosters), integrating existential, relational, and attachment perspectives.
- **N = 305**, CALM + usual care vs. usual care alone (unblinded).
- Depressive symptoms lower at **3 months (d = 0.23)** and **6 months (d = 0.29)**; better **end-of-life preparation** at 6 months; **no adverse effects**.
- *Caveat:* **small** effects against a **passive** comparator.

**Dignity therapy: Chochinov et al., 2011.** [S29]
- A brief intervention in which dying patients record what matters most about their lives, producing an edited "generativity document" for their families.
- **N = 441**, three arms (dignity therapy / client-centred care / standard palliative care).
- **No significant difference in distress**, whether depression, desire for death, or suicidality.
- **But** patients rated it more **helpful**, better for their **sense of dignity** and quality of life, and valuable **for their families**.
- *Why it matters:* it separates **"patients value it"** from **"it reduces symptoms."** Both are legitimate outcomes, but they are different claims.

## What this means for practice

1. **For the seriously ill, structured meaning-centered work has decent RCT support.** It is a reasonable, evidence-informed option in palliative and oncology settings. [S26][S27][S28]
2. **For general outpatient work, existential therapy is untested, not disproven.** Present it honestly: it is a coherent, clinically influential framework with **thin** direct outcome evidence. The broader findings that the **therapeutic relationship** and other common factors matter a great deal ([[theory-common-factors]], [[concept-therapeutic-alliance]]) are compatible with it but are not evidence *for* it specifically.
3. **Match the tool to the target.** For **fear of dying** as a symptom, exposure-based CBT has stronger support [S21] ([[concept-death-anxiety]]). For **loss of meaning** in serious illness, meaning-centered approaches have the best support [S26][S27]. For **insomnia**, Frankl's paradoxical intention works, now as part of CBT for insomnia [S30] ([[theory-logotherapy]]).
4. **Existential themes travel well into other modalities.** ACT's values work ([[concept-act]]) and Schneider's existential-integrative approach [S14] both embed existential concerns inside better-tested structures.
