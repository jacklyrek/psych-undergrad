# What Sexual Trauma Does: Tonic Immobility, Self-Blame & Sequelae

The diagnostic criteria for PTSD live in Unit 6's [[concept-trauma-stressor-disorders]] and the threat-response neurobiology lives in Unit 8's [[concept-neurobiology-of-trauma]]; neither is restated here. This page carries what is **specific to sexual trauma** — the handful of findings that change how you listen, starting with the one that answers the question survivors are asked most often and that damages them most.

## Tonic immobility: the answer to "why didn't you fight?"

**Most people do not fight.** Under inescapable threat, humans — like many other animals — can enter **tonic immobility**: an involuntary, temporary state of motor inhibition. The body locks. Not a decision, not compliance, not consent.

The key study is a prospective cohort at a Stockholm emergency clinic: of **298 women** presenting after sexual assault, **70% reported significant tonic immobility** and **48% reported extreme tonic immobility** during the assault. At six-month follow-up (n=189), **38.1% had developed PTSD** and **22.2% severe depression** — and tonic immobility **predicted both.** [S21] Peer-reviewed review work corroborates TI as a common, involuntary, evolutionarily conserved defensive response rather than an isolated finding. [S22]

Three things follow, and they are among the highest-yield facts in this module.

1. **"Why didn't you fight back?" has an empirical answer, and it is "you almost certainly couldn't."** Most survivors did not resist because resistance was not available to them. This is the single most useful piece of information you can give a survivor who is interrogating their own behavior — not as reassurance, but as fact.
2. **Absence of injury or resistance is not evidence that an assault did not occur.** Juries, families, police, and survivors themselves reason from "she didn't fight" to "so it wasn't really rape." The physiology says otherwise.
3. **Immobility itself becomes a source of shame.** Survivors interpret their own freezing as consent, weakness, or complicity — *"I didn't even try to stop it, so what does that say about me?"* — and that interpretation is a prime driver of the self-blame below. Naming tonic immobility to a survivor is often the intervention that loosens it.

The mechanism connects straight to [[concept-neurobiology-of-trauma]]: this is the far end of the defensive cascade past fight and flight, when neither is possible.

## Self-blame, and the contested distinction

Self-blame after sexual violence is close to universal, and it is not irrational: believing you caused it preserves the belief that you can prevent it happening again.

Janoff-Bulman's influential 1979 distinction: [S24]

- **Behavioral self-blame** — attributing to something you **did** ("I shouldn't have walked home alone," "I shouldn't have had that last drink"). Attributes to a **modifiable** source and preserves a sense of future control.
- **Characterological self-blame** — attributing to something you **are** ("I'm the kind of person this happens to," "there's something about me"). Attributes to a **non-modifiable** source and carries deservingness.

Janoff-Bulman proposed behavioral self-blame as **adaptive** — a control-maintenance strategy — and that claim propagated into a great deal of clinical teaching.

**It has not held up.** Subsequent research in survivor samples found **both** forms of self-blame associated with **greater** post-rape depression and distress. [S25] So: keep the taxonomy, because it tells you what *kind* of blame you are hearing and the two need different responses — behavioral blame is often addressed by information (see tonic immobility above), characterological blame is an identity wound and is slower work. Drop the claim that behavioral self-blame is good for people. This is a good example of an appealing clinical distinction outliving its evidence, and worth flagging whenever you meet it in a textbook.

## Shame, and why it differs from guilt

Guilt is about an act; shame is about the self. Sexual violence produces an unusual amount of shame relative to other traumas, for reasons specific to it: the body was involved, sexuality is already culturally loaded with secrecy, the perpetrator was usually someone trusted ([[concept-sexual-violence-scope]]), and — in child sexual abuse — **grooming manufactures a felt complicity** the child never actually had ([[concept-child-sexual-abuse]]). [S6]

The hardest version, and one you should be ready for rather than surprised by: **physiological arousal during the assault.** It happens, it is a reflex and not consent, and survivors who experienced it often carry the deepest shame and are the least likely to mention it. This is a particularly common and particularly silencing confusion for male survivors. [S20] You do not raise it uninvited. You do make it clear, by not flinching at anything else, that you are someone it could be said to.

## PTSD and the rest of the sequelae

Sexual assault is among the traumas most likely to produce PTSD. In the Stockholm cohort, **38.1% met PTSD criteria at six months** and **22.2% had severe depression.** [S21] Beyond PTSD, documented long-term consequences of child sexual abuse include depression and PTSD symptoms, substance and opioid misuse, risky sexual behaviors, **increased suicide risk**, sexually transmitted infections, and chronic physical conditions including heart disease, obesity, and cancer. [S3]

**Revictimization** is the pattern that most surprises people and is most easily misread as blame: women with childhood sexual abuse histories face a **two- to thirteen-fold increased risk** of experiencing sexual violence in adulthood. [S3] Say this carefully. It is a statement about what early abuse does to a person's ability to detect danger, assert boundaries, and be protected — not a statement that anyone invites what happens to them. But **not** knowing it leads counselors to treat a second assault as implausible, which is its own harm.

Note also the two-way traffic with the [[aux-addiction]] elective: substance use is a common consequence of sexual trauma and a common context for it. Treating either in isolation misses the other.

## "Rape trauma syndrome" is history, not a diagnosis

Burgess and Holmstrom described **rape trauma syndrome** in 1974, in two phases: an **acute phase** of disorganization, and a **long-term reorganization phase** beginning roughly two to three weeks out. [S23] It was important work — it gave the field its first structured account of post-rape reactions.

It has been substantially **absorbed by PTSD**, which entered DSM-III in 1980; the symptoms Burgess and Holmstrom catalogued (nightmares, hypervigilance, avoidance, intrusive memories) are core PTSD features. Survivors today are assessed and treated under the PTSD framework, not diagnosed with RTS. RTS persists mainly as a **courtroom term**, and courts describe it accordingly — New York's evidence guide calls it **"a therapeutic concept"** with limited evidentiary use. [S23] Know it as history and as a term you will meet in legal contexts; do not use it as a diagnosis.

The clinically useful residue of the two-phase model is the observation that acute presentation varies enormously — some survivors present visibly distressed, others flat, composed, or apparently fine. **A calm survivor is not a survivor who is unaffected**, and misreading composure as "she seems fine" is a documented route to under-serving people.

## Contested: are trauma memories fragmented?

You will be taught, probably confidently, that traumatic memories are stored differently — fragmented, sensory, non-narrative. **The evidence is genuinely disputed.** [S26]

- **Brewin's position:** trauma memories in PTSD are disorganized and lack narrative coherence, and recovery involves integrating intrusive sensory fragments into a coherent narrative. Brewin and Field's 2024 meta-analysis supports incoherence.
- **Rubin and colleagues' position:** testing 60 trauma-exposed adults across 28 coherence measures, they found trauma memories **as coherent as** very positive and very important memories, with no deficit in participants with PTSD.
- **Reviews from 2006 to 2016 call the evidence inconclusive**, in part because "coherence" has been operationalized inconsistently across studies, with automated indices whose validity in this context is unestablished.

**The clinical consequence is what to carry, and it is independent of who wins.** An account that comes out non-chronologically, with gaps, or differently on a second telling is **neither proof that trauma occurred nor proof of fabrication** — and you are not equipped to adjudicate which. Treating inconsistency as a lie is the error that harms survivors; treating it as diagnostic of trauma is the error that produces false confidence and, historically, false accusations ([[concept-sexual-trauma-treatment]]). Both errors come from believing the debate is settled.
