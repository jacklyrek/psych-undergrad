# The DSM-5-TR: Structure, History, and How to Use It

The *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* is the common language of American mental-health care: a catalog of disorders, each defined by explicit criteria. The syllabus's advice is exact — **you don't read it cover to cover; you learn its structure and use it as a reference.** This page is that structure.

## A short history that explains the present

- **DSM-I (1952):** 106 diagnoses in 130 pages, organized on a scheme the U.S. Army built in WWII — psychoanalytic in flavor, thin on criteria. [S1]
- **DSM-III (1980):** the revolution. Explicit, symptom-count diagnostic criteria replaced theory-laden descriptions so two clinicians could (in principle) reach the same diagnosis. Size jumped to 265 diagnoses / 886 pages. [S1]
- **DSM-IV (1994) → DSM-5 (2013):** DSM-5 carries **237 disorders** (fewer than DSM-IV's 297 — consolidation, e.g., the autism merger), dropped the Roman numeral, and made the structural changes below. [S1]
- **DSM-5-TR (2022):** a *text revision* that went beyond text: **prolonged grief disorder** added; free-standing **symptom codes for suicidal behavior and nonsuicidal self-injury** (recordable with or without any diagnosis — a preview of [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | Unit 8]]); unspecified mood disorder; criteria or specifier tweaks to 70+ disorders; terminology modernized ("experienced gender"), with the whole text reviewed by an **Ethnoracial Equity and Inclusion Work Group**. [S2]

Two removals mark the field's self-corrections: homosexuality was deleted as a diagnosis in 1973 — the standing proof that what counts as "disorder" is partly a social decision — and the bereavement exclusion was deleted in 2013 (the debate lives on [[concept-mood-disorders]]). [S1]

## How the manual is organized

**By lifespan, in one list.** DSM-5 scrapped the old "disorders of infancy, childhood and adolescence" chapter and ordered the manual developmentally: chapters that typically emerge early ([[concept-neurodevelopmental-disorders]]) come first; neurocognitive disorders of later life come last. [S4] Chapters are grouped so that neighbors are plausible differentials — the schizophrenia spectrum sits beside bipolar, which sits beside depressive disorders; the anxiety chapter is followed by [[concept-ocd | obsessive-compulsive and related disorders]] and then [[concept-trauma-stressor-disorders | trauma- and stressor-related disorders]], both of which DSM-5 *moved out* of the anxiety chapter into families of their own. [S12][S9]

**Each disorder entry contains** the diagnostic criteria plus supporting text: diagnostic features, prevalence, development and course, risk factors, culture- and gender-related features, and differential diagnosis — the entry is a small literature review, not just a checklist. [S1]

**When criteria don't quite fit:** DSM-IV's "not otherwise specified" became two options — **other specified disorder** (you name the reason the presentation misses full criteria) and **unspecified disorder** (you don't). Clinically honest vagueness beats a forced fit.

## What DSM-5 removed: the multiaxial system

DSM-IV diagnoses came in five axes: **I** clinical disorders; **II** personality disorders and intellectual disability; **III** relevant medical conditions; **IV** psychosocial/environmental stressors; **V** the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF, 0–100). DSM-5 dropped the whole apparatus for **nonaxial documentation** — all disorders and relevant conditions in one list, ordered by clinical priority. The GAF died of "conceptual lack of clarity … and questionable psychometric properties," replaced (optionally) by the WHO's **WHODAS 2.0** disability measure; Axis IV's job passed to expanded **V/Z codes** for psychosocial and environmental problems (housing, abuse, relational distress). [S3][S4]

Why counselors still care: the axes *forced* every diagnosis to sit inside a picture of the person's body, stressors, and functioning. The counseling literature's warning is that without the structural prompt, context becomes optional — and the profession's whole identity is "strength-based and developmentally, culturally and contextually sensitive" work. [S3] The fix is a habit, not a form: document stressors, functioning, and strengths anyway (the [[concept-biopsychosocial-model]] is the natural frame).

## DSM and ICD — who's who

The DSM is an American Psychiatric Association product; the **ICD** (WHO's International Classification of Diseases) is the world's. Worldwide, "the ICD is more frequently used for clinical diagnosis, whereas the DSM is more valued for research" [S1] — and in U.S. practice the two are welded together: the codes you write on claims *are* ICD codes, listed beside each DSM diagnosis. That's also the honest answer to "why diagnose at all?" in many settings: **reimbursement generally requires a covered diagnosis**, a structural pressure counselors must notice (it pushes toward diagnosing, and toward "covered" diagnoses) and manage ethically — diagnosis should record clinical reality, not billing convenience. [S3]

## Culture in the manual

DSM-5 retired "culture-bound syndrome" for three sharper ideas [S23]:

- **Cultural syndromes** — recognized symptom clusters in a cultural group (e.g., *ataque de nervios*; *susto*).
- **Cultural idioms of distress** — shared ways of *talking about* suffering ("nerves," heat in the chest) that don't map one-to-one onto criteria.
- **Cultural explanations** — causal models (e.g., *susto* as soul loss after a fright).

The **Outline for Cultural Formulation** (five domains: identity; explanations of illness; psychosocial environment; the clinician–client relationship; overall assessment) and the 16-question **Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI)** operationalize this. The stakes are misdiagnosis in both directions: "behavior that is aberrant in one culture can be standard in another" — apparent paranoia can be a rational stance for a migrant from state surveillance, spiritual experiences are not hallucinations by default, and [[concept-acculturation | acculturative stress]] can look like disorder. [S23] This is [[unit04-multicultural]] doing diagnostic work.

## How to actually use it (the counselor's algorithm)

1. Start from the presentation, not the manual — what does the client say is wrong, in their words?
2. Generate 2–3 candidate categories; read the **differential diagnosis** text, not just criteria.
3. Check the gates: duration, impairment, substance/medical rule-outs, culture (would this be unremarkable in the client's context?).
4. Prefer honest uncertainty (other specified/unspecified, provisional) over premature precision.
5. Write the diagnosis down as a *hypothesis to revisit* — Unit 7 turns it into a [[concept-case-conceptualization | case conceptualization]]; it is not the end of thinking.
