# Types of Group Work (ASGW)

"Group" is not one thing. A corporate task force, a divorce-recovery skills class, an outpatient interpersonal-growth group, and an inpatient group for chronic psychiatric conditions are all "groups," but they differ in **purpose, depth, membership, and the leader's role**. The **Association for Specialists in Group Work (ASGW)** — the ACA division that sets group standards — organizes the field into **four specializations**, distinguished primarily by **focus and depth of aim** [S10][S12]. Knowing which type you're running (and which you're *trained* to run — a scope-of-practice question, [[concept-scope-of-practice]]) determines everything downstream: who you admit, how you lead, and what "success" means.

## The four types

### 1. Task / work groups
**Aim:** accomplish an identified **work goal external to the members** — get something *done*.
**Examples:** committees, task forces, planning groups, teams, community organizations, study circles.
**Focus:** the task and the group's *process in service of the task* (a skilled task-group leader still attends to dynamics — a committee tears itself apart on process just like a therapy group — but the *point* is the product, not the members' personal growth).
**Depth:** shallowest with respect to members' inner lives; members are there for the work.

### 2. Psychoeducational (guidance) groups
**Aim:** **prevent** and **educate** — impart information and build specific skills, usually around a known theme.
**Examples:** a stress-management class, a parenting-skills group, a divorce-adjustment group, an anger-management or social-skills group, a psychoeducational group about managing depression.
**Members:** typically **relatively well-functioning** people who want knowledge or skills for a life role or stressor.
**Focus:** structured, often curriculum-based, leader as *teacher/facilitator*. Prevention and growth, not remediation of deep pathology. (This is the ASGW type that most resembles the classroom — and it borrows heavily from Unit 9's structured skills, e.g. a [[concept-dbt-skills]] skills group.)

### 3. Counseling groups
**Aim:** **interpersonal problem-solving** and **personal/interpersonal growth** — address the "**problems of living**" (ASGW's phrase).
**Members:** people dealing with **conscious, situational, developmental** concerns — not severe or entrenched dysfunction.
**Focus:** the **here-and-now** ([[concept-group-leadership]]), member-to-member interaction, growth and prevention/remediation of *common* life difficulties. **Not aimed at major personality change.** This is the bread-and-butter group of a counseling practicum.

### 4. Psychotherapy groups
**Aim:** **remediation and reconstruction** — treat deeper, more **severe or enduring** psychological dysfunction, including personality-level change.
**Members:** people with significant, often long-standing psychological problems (and, historically, more acute or serious diagnoses).
**Focus:** may work with **unconscious** material, developmental history, and repetitive dysfunctional patterns, not only conscious problems of living. Requires the most advanced training. (Yalom's *Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* is fundamentally about *this* type — though its therapeutic factors and here-and-now method inform all four.)

## The distinction that gets tested: counseling vs. psychotherapy groups

CACREP exams and supervisors press on the **counseling-vs-psychotherapy** hinge because the line is real but easily blurred [S12]:

| | **Counseling group** | **Psychotherapy group** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aim** | growth, prevention, problem-solving | remediation, reconstruction |
| **Depth** | conscious, situational "problems of living" | deeper, enduring dysfunction; may work with the unconscious |
| **Members** | relatively well-functioning, developmental concerns | more severe / entrenched problems |
| **Personality change?** | **not** a goal | **can be** a goal |
| **Setting (typical)** | schools, colleges, community agencies | clinical / inpatient / intensive outpatient |

The two share a great deal of method — both run on cohesion, the here-and-now, and the therapeutic factors — so in practice they sit on a **continuum of depth** rather than in sealed boxes. The reason the distinction still matters is **competence and scope** ([[concept-scope-of-practice]]): running a reconstructive psychotherapy group requires training a psychoeducational-group leader may not have, and the deeper the aim, the more careful the **screening** must be ([[concept-group-ethics-and-formation]]).

## Why the type drives everything else

Picking the type is not a labeling exercise; it sets the parameters:

- **Screening** tightens as depth increases — a stress-management class needs light screening; a psychotherapy group needs careful selection so members won't be harmed or won't derail the group ([[concept-group-ethics-and-formation]]).
- **Leadership** shifts from *teacher* (psychoeducational) toward *process facilitator* (counseling/psychotherapy) — more here-and-now, less curriculum ([[concept-group-leadership]]).
- **What "difficult member" means** changes: a monopolist derails a psychotherapy group's process ([[concept-difficult-group-members]]) but may barely matter in a one-off task meeting.
- **The therapeutic factors** ([[theory-yalom-factors]]) are strongest in counseling and psychotherapy groups, where cohesion and interpersonal learning have time to develop; a single task meeting rarely touches them.
