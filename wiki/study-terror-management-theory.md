# Terror Management Theory & the Mortality-Salience Replication Failure

**Terror management theory (TMT)** was the most ambitious attempt to turn the existential claim about death into testable, experimental psychology. Its story, a large literature followed by a failed high-powered replication, is worth knowing both for what it says about death anxiety and as a textbook case of evidence appraisal ([[unit12-research]]).

## The theory

Drawing on Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973), TMT proposes that humans, uniquely aware of their mortality, would be paralyzed by terror if not for two psychological buffers: [S17]
1. **Cultural worldviews**: shared belief systems that make the world meaningful and offer literal or symbolic immortality (religion, nation, legacy).
2. **Self-esteem**: the sense of being a valuable participant in that meaningful world.

**The mortality-salience (MS) hypothesis** follows. If worldviews and self-esteem protect against death anxiety, then **reminding people of their death** should increase their investment in them: more favorable views of people who share one's worldview, harsher views of those who threaten it, and more self-esteem striving. [S17]

The **classic paradigm**: participants write briefly about their own death (vs. a control topic such as dental pain), complete a delay task, then rate essays by a pro-US and an anti-US author. The predicted effect is that the MS group favors the pro-US author more. [S18]

## The evidence, in three stages

| Year | Study | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **2010** | **Burke, Martens & Faucher** meta-analysis: **164 articles, 277 experiments** | MS had a **moderate effect, r = .35**, on worldview- and self-esteem-related outcomes. Stronger with **American** participants, **college students**, a **longer delay** between prime and measure, and **people-related** attitudes as the outcome. [S17] |
| **2022** | **Many Labs 4** (Klein et al.): preregistered, ≈17–21 labs, ≈1,550 analyzed | **No MS effect** on the classic worldview-defense measure, **whether or not the original TMT authors helped design the protocol**. The authors conclude the original was a false positive or depends on conditions no one currently understands. [S18] |
| **2023** | **Schindler, Hilgard, Fritsche, Burke & Pfattheicher** meta-analysis (MS × norm salience) | Uncorrected g = 0.34; **bias-adjusted estimates between about −0.36 and 0.15**, mostly nonsignificant, with "signs of publication bias and/or the exploitation of researcher degrees of freedom." Also reports that a 2018 bias correction of the 2010 meta-analysis gave **d ≈ 0.32–0.61** (vs. ≈0.75–0.82 originally). [S19] |

**The dispute is not fully settled.** A re-analysis of Many Labs 4 that kept only adequately powered sites found a **small** effect (Chatard et al., 2020); a Bayesian multiverse re-analysis found fairly robust evidence **for the null** (Haaf et al., 2020). [S18] The fair summary is: **the classic effect is, at best, much smaller and more fragile than the literature claimed, and may not exist.**

## Why a large literature could be wrong — the lessons

This is a standard illustration of the replication crisis, and each lesson maps onto [[concept-validity-threats]] and [[concept-effect-size]] (Unit 12):
- **Volume ≠ validity.** 277 experiments can all be biased in the same direction if studies with null results go unpublished (**publication bias**) and flexible analyses find something (**researcher degrees of freedom**). A meta-analysis inherits the biases of what it pools. [S19]
- **Moderators can be a warning sign.** An effect that is stronger in American college students with a particular delay may be real and context-dependent, or it may be the pattern that flexible analysis tends to produce. Many Labs 4 was designed to test whether the original authors' expertise was the missing ingredient. It wasn't. [S18]
- **Preregistered, multi-site, high-powered replications outweigh many small studies.** That is the logic of the "many labs" design.
- **Credit the self-correction.** One of the 2010 meta-analysts (Burke) co-authored the 2023 bias-adjusted meta-analysis that shrank the effect. [S19] That is science working as it should.

## What survives, and what it means clinically

- **The existential claim does not stand or fall with TMT.** Yalom's clinical thesis, that death awareness shapes anxiety and defenses in the consulting room, was never derived from the MS paradigm, and TMT's failure does not refute it. It does remove what was often cited as its *experimental* support. [S10][S18]
- **Death anxiety as a clinical phenomenon is separately supported.** It shows up across anxiety disorders, and it responds to exposure-based CBT. [S20][S21] See [[concept-death-anxiety]].
- **Don't cite TMT as settled** in a paper, a treatment rationale, or a conversation with a client. "Research shows that reminders of death make people cling to their beliefs" is exactly the kind of confident claim this repository treats as a bug.
