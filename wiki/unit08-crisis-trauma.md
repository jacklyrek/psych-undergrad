# Unit 8 — Crisis, Risk Assessment & Trauma-Informed Care

This is the unit the syllabus says carries the most weight for keeping a client and you safe from day one: *"A client may disclose suicidal thoughts in your first week of practicum. Freezing is not an option. This unit is where preparation directly protects a life."* Two skills sit at its center, and they pull in opposite directions in a way worth naming up front. One is **leaning in without flinching** — asking a frightened, shut-down, or actively suicidal person the direct questions most people are trained by ordinary life to avoid. The other is **restraint** — resisting the beginner's urge to excavate a trauma history "to understand," because with trauma the reflex to dig is itself a way to do harm. Crisis work is where the fix-it stance is most dangerous in both directions: doing too little (freezing, changing the subject, false reassurance) and doing too much (probing, over-controlling, rescuing).

This is a Tier 2 clinical-foundations unit and, per the syllabus, one of the three you'd do even if you had only a month (Units 1, 3, 8). It ties into CACREP's **Helping Relationships** and its crisis-and-trauma competencies. It rests on two earlier units and hands to a later one:

- **← Unit 3 (the rules).** A suicidal or homicidal disclosure is exactly where confidentiality reaches its limits — danger to self, danger to others ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]], [[concept-duty-to-warn]]). You cannot do crisis work without the ethical map from Unit 3, and the **prediction critique** from [[study-tarasoff]] lands here too: we assess risk to *manage* it, not to prophesy who will act.
- **← Unit 7 (the risk screen).** Every intake carries a risk screen, and item 9 of the PHQ-9 is a trip-wire that lands you here ([[concept-screening-tools]], [[concept-intake-interview]]). Unit 7 flags risk; Unit 8 is what you *do* about a positive flag.
- **→ [[unit11-neuroscience | Unit 11]] (the brain).** The neurobiology of trauma is a working-level preview of the [[concept-stress-response | stress-response]] and [[concept-autonomic-nervous-system | nervous-system]] material that Unit 11 develops in full.

It also borders the **[[aux-psychological-first-aid]]** elective (acute crisis support and grief) and the **[[aux-addiction]]** elective (overdose, means, and crisis in substance use) — both were written pointing forward to this unit, and are now wired into it.

## The stance: lean in on danger, hold back on trauma

Both halves of this unit correct a fear-driven reflex, and they correct it in opposite directions.

**On danger, the reflex is avoidance.** Beginners fear that asking about suicide directly will "plant the idea," so they soften it, talk around it, or don't ask. The evidence is unambiguous: **asking about suicide does not increase risk and may reduce distress** [S4]. The move is to ask plainly and non-anxiously — *"Are you thinking about killing yourself?"* — and then tolerate the answer. Freezing, changing the subject, or rushing to reassure ("I'm sure it'll be fine") are the failures, not the question.

**On trauma, the reflex is excavation.** The technically-minded helper wants the whole history "to understand the problem." But **trauma-informed care does not require the client to disclose or process the trauma** [S12]; pushing for the narrative before safety and trust exist is a classic way helpers re-traumatize. The move is to assume any client *may* be a survivor, create safety, and let the story come — or not — at the client's pace. This is the same don't-probe restraint the [[aux-psychological-first-aid]] elective teaches for acute crisis.

Both halves share one non-negotiable: **safety first, and safety continuously**. In the crisis models it is the thread through every step, not a box you check once [S9].

## What's contested (hold these carefully)

- **You can assess risk; you cannot predict the individual.** Instruments and risk-factor lists identify elevated-risk *populations* and guide *disposition* — they do not forecast who dies or when. This is the [[study-tarasoff]] prediction critique in a new setting: assess to **manage** risk, not to prophesy [S1][S3][S5].
- **No-suicide contracts don't work.** A client's promise not to self-harm has no evidence of reducing suicide, can create false reassurance, and gives the client nothing to *do*. Collaborative safety planning replaced it [S6][S7].
- **Trauma neurobiology: trust the direction, not the decimals.** The amygdala-up / hippocampus-and-PFC-down picture is well supported in *direction*; cortisol findings are inconsistent, and the **window of tolerance** and **polyvagal theory** are useful *heuristics*, not settled brain states. **van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* is popular-press** — good for framing, corroborate the mechanics elsewhere [S14][S15][S17].
- **Trauma-informed ≠ trauma-focused.** The universal stance (avoid re-traumatization) is not the specialized treatment (PE, CPT, TF-CBT, EMDR). Conflating them is what makes a beginner dig [S12][S13].
- **Self-care is ethics, not indulgence.** Unaddressed vicarious trauma impairs judgment and boundaries; ACA C.2.g makes practicing while impaired a breach [S19][S21].

## A note on the weight of this material

This unit is emotionally heavy, and the syllabus says so plainly: *if any of it touches something personal rather than purely professional, that's worth bringing to your own therapy or a trusted person.* The material here is kept clinical and precise on purpose. The counselor-cost pages ([[concept-vicarious-trauma]], [[concept-counselor-self-care]]) are not an afterthought — they're the unit acknowledging that doing this work well and doing it for a long time are the same problem.
