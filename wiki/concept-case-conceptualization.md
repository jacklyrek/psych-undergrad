# Case Conceptualization (the Five Ps)

This is the page the whole unit points at. **Case conceptualization** (a.k.a. case *formulation*) is
the counselor's working **explanation of the client** — a multidimensional, person-specific picture
of what's wrong, *how it got that way*, what keeps it going, and what to do about it. The syllabus's
claim is blunt and worth taking literally: conceptualization is **"the skill that separates having a
nice chat from doing therapy."** Diagnosis (Unit 6) tells you *what* — the category. Conceptualization
tells you *why this person, why now, and why it persists* — and only that second thing points to a
treatment [S4][S8].

## Diagnosis vs. formulation

| | **Diagnosis** ([[unit06-psychopathology]]) | **Case conceptualization** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | *What* is it? | *How* did it come to be, and what maintains it? |
| Kind | **categorical** — shared label | **idiographic** — this person's causal story |
| Basis | DSM criteria | history + theory + the client's meaning |
| Output | a code (for communication, billing, research) | a plan ([[concept-treatment-planning]]) |
| Failure mode | over-pathologizing; the map for the territory | staying descriptive; never explaining |

Macneil et al. (2012) put it directly: **diagnosing is insufficient** — you have to add the causal,
lifestyle, and personal factors, because two clients with the identical DSM label can need opposite
treatments [S4]. The diagnosis is an *input* to the formulation, not a replacement for it — the same
"diagnosis as hypothesis to revisit" stance from [[concept-dsm-structure]].

## The Five Ps — a theory-neutral scaffold

The most portable formulation framework, because it works whatever your orientation [S4][S8]. Four of
the Ps are the classic "4 Ps" (Weerasekera, 1993); adding the **presenting problem** at the front
makes five:

- **Presenting problem** — the concern the client can't manage, in their words (the *what*, from the
  [[concept-intake-interview | intake]]).
- **Predisposing factors** — the standing **vulnerabilities** that loaded the gun *before* anything
  happened: genetics/temperament, early adversity, [[concept-attachment-styles | attachment history]],
  chronic medical conditions. *Why this person was at risk.*
- **Precipitating factors** — the **proximal trigger** that pulled it: the loss, the conflict, the
  anniversary, the med change. *Why now.* (You caught the first read on this in the intake's "why
  today?".)
- **Perpetuating factors** — what **keeps it going** now, often independent of what started it:
  avoidance, reassurance-seeking, substance use, a reinforcing relationship pattern, sleep loss,
  hopeless beliefs. ***This is the load-bearing P*** — it's what your interventions actually target,
  because you can't change the past predisposition or undo the precipitant, but you *can* interrupt
  what maintains the problem.
- **Protective factors** — the **strengths and buffers**: supports, insight, prior recovery, faith,
  a reason for living. The counseling profession's strength-based identity lives here; a formulation
  that lists only deficits is half-done ([[concept-dsm-structure]] notes DSM-5 dropped the axis that
  forced this in — you supply it).

Memory hook: **predisposing = the loaded gun; precipitating = the trigger pulled; perpetuating = the
finger that won't come off; protective = the safety catch.**

## Cross it with the biopsychosocial grid

The Five Ps get sharper when each is asked across the three domains of
[[concept-biopsychosocial-model]] (Engel; systematized by Weerasekera) — a 4-Ps × 3-domains grid
[S8]:

| | **Biological** | **Psychological** | **Social** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Predisposing** | genetics, temperament | early trauma, attachment | poverty, family instability |
| **Precipitating** | illness, substance, med change | a loss, a threat, a reminder | job loss, breakup, move |
| **Perpetuating** | poor sleep, ongoing use | avoidance, hopeless beliefs | conflict, isolation, unemployment |
| **Protective** | physical health, good sleep | insight, coping skills | supportive relationships, community |

You don't fill every cell — you use the grid so you don't tunnel on one domain (the classic error of
seeing only biology, or only the trauma, or only the social mess).

## Theory is the lens that turns a formulation into a plan

The Five Ps are deliberately **theory-neutral** [S4] — but you are not. Your orientation from
[[unit02-theories]] decides *which* perpetuating factors you see and *what counts as changing them*:

- A **CBT** counselor formulates the perpetuating factors as maintaining **thoughts and behaviors**
  (avoidance, distortions) and plans to test and change them ([[theory-cbt]]).
- A **psychodynamic** counselor sees perpetuating **unconscious patterns and defenses** and plans to
  make them conscious ([[theory-psychodynamic]]).
- A **family-systems** counselor locates the perpetuating factor in the **relational structure**, not
  the individual, and plans to shift the system ([[theory-family-systems]]).
- A **solution-focused** or **narrative** counselor foregrounds **protective** factors and
  exceptions, and plans to amplify them ([[theory-postmodern]]).

Same client, same Five Ps, different treatment — because conceptualization *is* theory applied. This
is the Unit 2 ↔ Unit 7 thread the course-map flags.

## The stance: a hypothesis, held loosely, built *with* the client

- **A formulation is a testable hypothesis, not a verdict.** You revise it as the work reveals more —
  the same discipline as holding a diagnosis provisionally.
- **Formulate *with* the client, not *about* them.** Sharing a formulation ("here's how I'm making
  sense of this — does it fit?") is itself therapeutic and collaborative; a formulation the client
  never sees or disagrees with is the fix-it reflex in a lab coat.
- **Don't skip to solutions.** The pull (especially under time pressure) is to jump from presenting
  problem straight to intervention. The perpetuating-factors analysis is the step that makes the
  intervention land — skip it and you're treating a category, not a person.
