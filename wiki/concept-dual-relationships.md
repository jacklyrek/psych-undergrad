# Dual Relationships & Boundary Management

**The idea:** a **dual (multiple) relationship** exists when a counselor holds more than one role
with a client — therapist *and* friend, business partner, professor, in-law, fellow congregant.
Terminology note: APA's code says "multiple relationships"; the ACA Code mostly speaks of
**"nonprofessional relationships"** and boundary management. The 2014 ACA Code's key move is that **not all dual relationships are unethical** — some are prohibited outright, the rest are *managed* with a risk–benefit analysis, consent, consultation, and documentation. [S1][S11][S12]

## The prohibited list (A.5 — bright lines)

- **A.5.a — Sexual/romantic relationships with current clients: absolutely prohibited.** Extends to
  the client's romantic partners and family members, and to electronic as well as in-person
  contact. No risk–benefit analysis applies; this is the profession's clearest rule. [S1]
- **A.5.c — Former clients: prohibited for 5 years** after the last professional contact — and even
  after five years, only with **documented forethought** that the relationship isn't exploitive and
  holds no potential to harm. (The APA code's parallel rule uses 2 years; know whose code you're
  quoting.) [S1][S11]
- **A.5.d — Don't counsel friends or family** with whom you can't remain objective. [S1]
- **A.5.e — No personal virtual relationships with current clients** — don't friend/follow your
  clients on personal social media. [S1]

## The managed list (A.6 — judgment + paperwork)

- **A.6.a — previous relationships:** accepting an acquaintance as a client requires weighing risks
  and benefits, plus precautions (informed consent, consultation, supervision, documentation). [S1]
- **A.6.b — extending boundaries:** attending a client's wedding or graduation, buying a client's
  product, visiting a hospitalized family member — potentially beneficial, never automatic. [S1]
- **A.6.c — document boundary extensions *before* the interaction** (when feasible): rationale,
  potential benefit, anticipated consequences. If unintended harm occurs, show evidence of
  attempting to remedy it. [S1]
- **A.6.d — role changes** (individual → couples counseling; evaluator → therapist) require fresh
  informed consent and the right to refuse. [S1]

## Boundary crossing vs. boundary violation (the discrimination that matters)

| | Boundary **crossing** | Boundary **violation** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | departure from standard practice that is **not harmful or exploitative** — may even serve the work | departure that **harms or exploits** the client or puts the work at serious risk |
| Examples | shaking an offered hand; extending a session for a client in crisis; attending a client's graduation (documented); appropriate self-disclosure | sexual contact; entering business deals with a client; using client information for personal gain |
| Verdict | judgment call — sometimes good practice | unethical, sometimes illegal |

The heuristic embedded in the codes: ask **whose need does this meet?** A crossing serves the
client's interest; a violation serves the counselor's. Violations also rarely start as violations —
the "slippery slope" pattern (small unexamined crossings drifting toward exploitation) is why
A.6.c makes you *write the rationale down before acting*: documentation forces the examination. [S1][S11]

## The rural/small-community reality

The blanket "avoid all dual relationships" advice of older codes collapsed on contact with real
communities: in a small town, on a military base, in a tight-knit cultural or religious community,
or in recovery communities, the counselor *will* share a grocery store, a church, and a school with
clients. Overlap there is unavoidable and refusing all overlapping clients would deny care. The
modern standard is therefore **manage, don't merely avoid**: minimize role mixing where possible,
talk about unavoidable overlap openly, and document. [S11][S12]

> **What it sounds like:** "Since we live in the same town, we're going to run into each other —
> at Kroger, at games. Here's how I handle that: I won't greet you first, so you never have to
> explain who I am. If you say hi, I'll say hi. And anything we see or say out there stays out
> there — we can talk about it in here if you want, but I won't bring it up. Does that work for you?"

That's boundary management as an *informed-consent conversation* — which is exactly what A.6
intends ([[concept-informed-consent]]).

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Where the crossing/violation line sits** is contextual — the same act (a hug, self-disclosure,
  a gift) can be therapeutic with one client and harmful with another; culture changes the meaning too. Zur and others argue rigid "risk-management" boundary avoidance can itself harm care; more conservative voices emphasize slippery-slope risk. The codes deliberately split the difference (bright lines for sex and exploitation; judgment for the rest). [S11][S12]
- **Post-termination romance:** ACA says 5 years + documented non-exploitation; APA says 2 + a
  heavy multi-factor test; some ethicists argue "once a client, never a partner." Know the rule
  *and* that it's debated. [S1][S11]
