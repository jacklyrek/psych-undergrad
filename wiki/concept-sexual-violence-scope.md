# Sexual Violence: Definitions, Consent & Scope

Before any of the clinical material, the vocabulary — because in this area imprecise words produce wrong beliefs, and the wrong belief a counselor most often carries into the room is that sexual violence is a rare event committed by strangers against young women. Each part of that is false, and each falsehood has a cost: it makes you slower to recognize a disclosure, and it makes survivors who do not fit the picture — men, people assaulted by partners, people who did not fight — less likely to tell you at all.

## The definition

The CDC defines sexual violence as **"sexual activity when consent is not obtained or freely given."** [S1] Two features of that sentence do the work. **"Sexual activity"** is broader than intercourse and broader than physical contact — the same definitional page includes verbal sexual harassment and technology-facilitated sexual violence such as non-consensual sharing of intimate images. And **"obtained or freely given"** places the burden on the presence of consent rather than the presence of refusal: the question is not whether the person said no, it is whether they freely said yes.

## The categories

Federal public-health surveillance splits sexual violence into a structure worth knowing, because prevalence figures are reported by category and are meaningless without it. [S1]

| Category | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **Contact sexual violence** | The umbrella term: rape (completed or attempted), being **made to penetrate** someone else, sexual coercion, and unwanted sexual contact |
| **Rape** | Completed or attempted penetration without consent, including when the person is unable to consent |
| **Made to penetrate** | A distinct category — the person was forced to penetrate someone else. **This is how many men are sexually victimized**, and it is invisible in legal definitions that define rape solely as being penetrated |
| **Verbal sexual harassment** | Unwanted sexual comments in workplaces, public spaces, and elsewhere |
| **Technology-facilitated** | Non-consensual sharing of intimate images, coerced sexting, image-based abuse |
| **Child sexual abuse** | Sexual activity involving a person under 18 who cannot consent — does **not** require contact [S3] — see [[concept-child-sexual-abuse]] |

**"Made to penetrate" is the category most people have never heard of and the one that changes how you listen.** A man describing being forced to penetrate someone has been sexually assaulted; if your mental model of rape is only penetrative assault *of* a victim, you may hear his account and fail to name it, which is precisely the invalidation male survivors report and one reason they take decades to tell anyone. [S20]

## Consent, coercion, and incapacitation

The clinically usable version: consent is **freely given, informed, specific, and revocable**, and three conditions void it regardless of what was said.

- **Force or threat** — physical force, weapons, or threats to the person or others.
- **Coercion** — pressure, manipulation, blackmail, or abuse of authority. Someone who eventually says yes because refusing has been made costly has not freely consented. Coercion is where the "but she agreed" defense usually lives, and it is why consent is about freedom, not utterance.
- **Incapacity** — the person could not consent: unconscious, asleep, substantially impaired by alcohol or drugs, or **below the legal age of consent**, which is set by state law and makes the question of the minor's "agreement" legally irrelevant. [S3]

The counseling-relevant consequence of the incapacity clause is that **a survivor who was drinking is more likely to blame themselves and less likely to be believed** — by themselves first, often before anyone else gets a chance. This is where the self-blame material in [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]] meets the definitional material here: a person who believes their own intoxication disqualifies them may never use the word "assault" for what happened, and may present to you with depression, insomnia, or relationship problems instead.

## How common it is — and which survey said so

Prevalence numbers in this field circulate detached from their instruments, which makes them unusable and, quoted wrongly, discrediting. Every figure below is tagged with its source survey. [S2]

**Lifetime prevalence, CDC NISVS (2016/2017 data):** [S1]

- **Contact sexual violence:** nearly **half of women**; **more than 1 in 6 men**.
- **Completed or attempted rape:** **more than 1 in 5 women**; **1 in 31 men**.
- **Made to penetrate:** **1 in 26 men**.
- **Verbal sexual harassment** in workplaces or public spaces: almost **1 in 3 women**; **1 in 9 men** (workplace), **1 in 14 men** (public).
- **Technology-facilitated sexual violence:** more than **1 in 4 women**; **1 in 6 men**.

**Age of first victimization** — the figure that reframes this as a developmental problem rather than an adult one: **nearly 80% of female rape survivors were first victimized before age 25, and almost half before age 18.** For men made to penetrate, nearly 80% before 25 and about 40% before 18. [S1]

**Disparities.** Rape prevalence is markedly higher for **non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native and multiracial women** — more than 2 in 5. [S1] By sexual identity, **4 in 5 bisexual women** experienced contact sexual violence and **nearly half were raped**; about **3 in 5 lesbian women and gay men** experienced contact sexual violence. [S5] Note what is *missing*: the 2016/2017 NISVS sample was **too small to produce national estimates for transgender people**, which is a limitation of the survey, not evidence of lower risk. [S2] Saying "we don't have good national numbers" is the honest statement; inferring safety from a measurement gap is not.

**Reporting.** BJS's National Crime Victimization Survey estimated **46% of rape/sexual assaults were reported to police in 2023** — the highest share since 2010 — with a **margin of error of ±8.2%**. [S4] Two cautions: the interval is wide and the figure moves year to year, so quote the direction rather than the decimal; and **NCVS and NISVS measure different constructs** (crime-framed incidents vs. behaviorally-defined experiences), which is why their numbers never reconcile. The durable claim is that a large share is never reported, and that non-reporting is normal rather than suspicious.

## The perpetrator is almost always known

For children, **about 90% of child sexual abuse is perpetrated by someone known and trusted by the child or the child's family.** [S3] The adult pattern runs the same direction. This single fact dismantles the stranger-in-an-alley model and explains most of what is otherwise puzzling about survivor behavior: why disclosure is delayed (telling means accusing someone the family loves and depends on), why survivors protect the person who harmed them, why "why didn't you just leave" misunderstands the situation, and why an assault by a partner or friend can be harder to name than one by a stranger — there is no script for it, and the person is still in your life.

## "Victim" or "survivor"?

Both words are in active professional use and neither is universally correct. **"Victim"** is the legal and criminological term and is precise about harm done by someone else; some people find it accurate and object to being told to feel empowered. **"Survivor"** emphasizes agency and continuation past the event; some people find it presumptuous, especially early on, and some reject both. The practice rule is simple and is itself an application of this module's control principle: **use the word the person uses about themselves**, and if you do not know yet, describe rather than label — "what happened to you," "the assault," or their own phrasing. Correcting someone's word for their own experience is a small act of taking over. Related: **"secondary survivor"** names the partner, friend, or family member affected by someone else's assault — see [[concept-supporting-a-survivor]]. [S37]
