# The Neurobiology of Trauma (a working model)

You do not need to be a neuroscientist to do trauma-informed work, but you need a **working model** — enough to understand why a trauma survivor's reactions are *involuntary and physiological*, not choices or character flaws. The one sentence to carry: **in trauma, the brain's alarm system gets stuck on and its braking and context systems get turned down, so the body reacts to the past as if it were the present.** Everything below fleshes out that sentence. Read the direction of the findings as solid and the decimals as provisional — this is an area where the popular story runs ahead of the settled science (see the caveats at the end) [S14][S15].

## Three structures: an alarm, a context-stamp, and a brake

| Structure | Normal job | In trauma |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Amygdala** | threat detection — the smoke alarm | **hyperactive**; fires at over-generalized cues; excitatory to the stress axis |
| **Hippocampus** | memory + **context** — time-stamps events as *past* | reduced volume/function; **fails to mark the memory as over**, so it intrudes as if *now*; inhibitory to the stress axis |
| **Prefrontal cortex (mPFC)** | top-down regulation — the brake on the amygdala | **reduced activity**; can't dampen the alarm → "regulatory failure" |

The result is a system out of balance: the **amygdala** keeps sounding the alarm, the **hippocampus** can't file the experience away as finished, and the **prefrontal cortex** can't apply the brake [S14][S15]. Clinically, that *is* the picture of PTSD — hypervigilance, flashbacks that feel present, and difficulty calming down once triggered.

## The HPA axis: the stress hormone loop

The body's slower stress response is the **HPA axis**: the **h**ypothalamus releases **CRH** → the **p**ituitary releases **ACTH** → the **a**drenal glands release **cortisol**, which mobilizes energy for threat. Normally the loop is self-limiting: cortisol and hippocampal output feed back to shut off CRH (**negative feedback**) [S14]. Chronic or early trauma **dysregulates** this loop. Here honesty matters: the findings are **inconsistent** — some studies show elevated cortisol, others show blunted/low morning cortisol — and "dysregulated responses are **not consistently associated with psychopathology**" [S14]. So the takeaway is *dysregulation of the stress axis*, not a clean "high cortisol = trauma" biomarker. McEwen's **allostatic load** frames it well: chronic stress shifts the body's whole set-point, and running the emergency system too long wears the body down.

## Two memory systems: why the story is fragmented

A key working idea: trauma is encoded differently from ordinary experience [S15].

- **Explicit memory** (hippocampus-dependent): conscious, verbal, **narrative**, time-stamped — "this happened last year."
- **Implicit memory** (amygdala-dependent): non-conscious, **sensory, emotional, and bodily** — a smell, a posture, a surge of dread.

Under the extreme arousal of trauma, **explicit encoding is disrupted while implicit encoding is strengthened**. That's why a survivor may have **fragmentary sensory images and intense somatic/emotional reactions without a coherent story** — and why a trigger (a sound, a smell) can launch the body into full alarm before any conscious memory arrives. This is the defensible core of the popular phrase "the body keeps the score": the *body* reacts before the *narrative* catches up. It also reframes a client's self-blame ("my memory is patchy, so maybe it wasn't that bad" or "why can't I just tell the story?") as **normal trauma encoding, not evasion or exaggeration** — which is directly why [[concept-trauma-informed-care | trauma-informed care]] says *don't force the narrative*.

## Fight, flight, and freeze — freeze is not a choice

The threat response is usually taught as **fight or flight**, but the third — **freeze / tonic immobility** — is the one that matters most clinically. It's an **involuntary shutdown** (the deer in headlights; the assault victim who couldn't move or scream), mediated below conscious control [S15]. Survivors routinely blame themselves for it ("why didn't I fight back / run / say no?"), and naming freeze as an **automatic, hardwired response** — not consent, not weakness, not a failure of will — is often itself therapeutic.

## The window of tolerance (a clinical heuristic)

Siegel's **window of tolerance** is the arousal band in which a person can stay regulated — think, feel, and stay present without being overwhelmed [S16]:

- **Above** the window = **hyperarousal** (fight/flight): panic, rage, racing thoughts, hypervigilance.
- **Below** the window = **hypoarousal** (freeze/shutdown): numbness, dissociation, flatness, collapse.

Trauma **narrows and destabilizes** the window, so small triggers push a survivor out of it. The clinical use is concrete: **notice which side the client is on and help them back *into* the window before doing any content work** — grounding and slowing for hyperarousal, gentle re-orienting/re-engagement for hypoarousal. This is trauma-informed pacing made operational. Treat the window of tolerance as a **useful map, not a measured brain state** — it's a heuristic that organizes what you observe, not a validated neural construct.

## What's contested — read carefully

- **Direction solid, details soft.** Amygdala-up / hippocampus-and-PFC-down and the explicit/implicit split are well supported in *direction*; exact volumes and cortisol patterns vary across studies [S14][S15].
- **"The body keeps the score" is a metaphor with a real core and an overstated edge.** The real core: implicit/somatic encoding and physiological reactivity. The overstated edge: the strong claim that trauma is literally "stored in the body," and sweeping claims about **repressed/recovered memory** — memory science rejects the memory-as-videotape view; trauma memory is **reconstructive and suggestible**, not a sealed recording. **van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* is popular-press**: excellent for orientation and empathy, but corroborate its mechanisms against peer-reviewed work (done here) [S17].
- **Polyvagal theory** (Porges) — the vagal/social-engagement account behind a lot of trauma pop-neuroscience — is clinically popular but its specific neurophysiological claims are **scientifically contested**. Use its language ("the nervous system feels unsafe") as a bridge to clients, not as established fact [S17].
