# Privilege, Power & the Helping Relationship

**The idea:** privilege is "an invisible package of unearned assets" — McIntosh's (1989) knapsack
metaphor. Two properties make it clinically important rather than merely political: it is
**unearned** (conferred by group membership, not merit) and it is **invisible to its holder** —
McIntosh's essay is a list of ~50 mundane advantages ("I can turn on the television and see people
of my race widely represented"; "I can do well in a challenging situation without being called a
credit to my race") that she, as a White academic, had never had to notice. The invisibility is
the point: privilege is experienced as *normal*, which is exactly why counselor self-awareness
has to be trained rather than assumed ([[theory-tripartite-model]]'s awareness dimension;
the [[concept-msjcc]]'s first domain). [S5]

## Power inside the dyad

The counseling relationship is *itself* a power asymmetry before culture even enters: one person
discloses, the other doesn't; one is in distress, the other holds diagnostic language, notes,
fees, and (Unit 3) reporting power. Stack social privilege on top — the MSJCC's Quadrant I,
privileged counselor with marginalized client, is the modal arrangement in U.S. mental health —
and the client is managing two gradients at once. [S1][S2] Cultural humility's second commitment
(**redress the power imbalance**) targets exactly this: the client is the expert on their own
experience, and the counselor's category-knowledge never outranks the person
([[concept-cultural-humility]]). [S4]

One more asymmetry worth naming: the client may have spent a lifetime reading the privileged
group accurately (a survival skill), while the counselor has never needed the reverse. Assume the
client sees you more clearly than you see them.

## Broaching: making culture speakable (Day-Vines)

**Broaching** is the counselor's ability to consider — and *raise* — how race, ethnicity, culture,
and other sociopolitical factors bear on the client's concerns, rather than waiting for the client
to risk it first. Silence is not neutrality: to a client scanning for whether this counselor can
handle the topic, an unbroached difference reads as "we don't talk about that here."
Day-Vines et al. (2007) describe a **continuum of five broaching styles**: [S10]

| Style | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Avoidant** | never raises culture; treats it as irrelevant or too risky |
| **Isolating** | broaches once, ritually ("check the box"), never returns to it |
| **Continuing/incongruent** | keeps trying, but awkward and mechanical — technique without integration |
| **Integrated/congruent** | raises culture naturally where it bears on the work; can stay in the conversation |
| **Infusing** | cultural responsiveness extends beyond sessions into the counselor's whole professional life |

The progression parallels the counselor's own identity development — you broach at the depth
you've done your own work. [S10]

What it sounds like — White counselor, Black male client, first session, presenting with work
stress:

> **Avoidant:** (says nothing about difference, ever; hopes rapport covers it)

> **Isolating:** "Oh — and, I want you to know this is a safe space for people of all
> backgrounds." (Once, at intake, read off the mental script; never mentioned again.)

> **Integrated/congruent:** "You mentioned being the only Black manager on your floor. I'm aware
> I'm a White counselor and there may be parts of that experience I'll need you to teach me — and
> some of the stress you're describing may have to do with race, or it may not. I'm open to it
> either way. How does it feel to be talking about this with me?"

The congruent version does three things at once: names the difference, names its own limits
(humility), and *invites* rather than presumes ("it may not") — leaving the client in charge of
whether race is on the table. Research on broaching links it to trust, alliance strength, and
retention with clients of color — the dropout data in [[unit04-multicultural]] is partly a
broaching failure at scale. [S10][S15]

## The stance trap for the systems-minded

Privilege talk triggers two symmetrical errors. One is defensiveness ("I earned what I have") —
McIntosh's answer is that unearned advantage and personal effort coexist; the knapsack is about
the former. The other is the fix-it reflex inverted into performance: confessing privilege *at*
the client, which recenters the counselor and hands the client the job of granting absolution.
The clinical use of privilege-awareness is quieter: it calibrates your hypotheses (is this
"resistance," or a rational read of systems that have burned them?), your broaching, and your
advocacy ([[concept-msjcc]]). [S5][S4]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **McIntosh's essay is a personal-observational text, not an empirical study** — its authority is
  conceptual (it named a real phenomenon memorably) and it's the standard teaching source, but
  don't cite it as data. The measurable correlates live in the disparities literature. [S5][S15]
- **How much to broach, how early** is a clinical-judgment debate: too little reads as avoidance,
  too much can subordinate the client's stated concern to the counselor's agenda. The congruent
  ideal is *client-led openness*, not mandatory race talk in minute one. [S10]
