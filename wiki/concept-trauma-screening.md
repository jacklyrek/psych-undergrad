# Trauma Screening: What to Ask, and When Not To

The most common way a well-meaning organization gets trauma-informed care wrong is by deciding that being trauma-informed means **screening everyone for trauma**. It does not, it can cause harm, and the most popular instrument for doing it is being used for a purpose its own author says it cannot serve.

## First: screening is not the entry point

Trauma-informed care is a **universal stance** that assumes any client might be a survivor and therefore **does not require anyone to disclose anything** ([[concept-trauma-informed-care]], [[concept-retraumatization]]). If safety, predictability, transparency, and choice are applied to everyone, no screening is needed to trigger them.

So screening is a **separate decision** with its own justification, and the justification has to be clinical: *what will change based on the answer?* An organization that adds a trauma screen without changing anything else has not become trauma-informed — it has added a question.

## Screening vs. assessment vs. treatment

Three different things, routinely conflated, and Unit 7's [[concept-screening-tools]] logic applies unchanged.

| | **Screening** | **Assessment** | **Treatment** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Question** | Is there a signal worth pursuing? | What exactly is going on, and what maintains it? | Can we reduce it? |
| **Form** | Brief, standardized, often self-report | Clinical interview over time, in a relationship | A protocol ([[concept-sexual-trauma-treatment]], [[concept-trauma-informed-care]]) |
| **Output** | A flag, never a diagnosis | A formulation ([[concept-case-conceptualization]]) | Change |
| **Training** | Minimal | Clinical | Specific and trained |

**A screen is a flag, not a verdict** — the same rule Unit 7 states for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. A positive trauma screen means *look further*, not *this person has PTSD*, and certainly not *this person's problems are caused by trauma*.

## The ACE-screening problem

Here is the thing to be clear about, because you will encounter the practice and may be asked to do it.

**Robert Anda, the ACE study's co-principal investigator, wrote that the questionnaire was designed to research — not screen — and that the ACE score "is neither a diagnostic tool nor is it predictive at the individual level."** [S9]

The reasons are structural, not fixable by better administration: [S9][S11]

- The score is an **unweighted count of categories** that ignores **frequency, severity, duration, and developmental timing**. Two people scoring 4 may share nothing.
- It **omits** entire adversity categories — racial trauma, community violence, traumatic bereavement, medical trauma, and more — so a badly harmed child can score zero.
- It has **no standardized cut points** in the way blood pressure or lipids do. There is no validated threshold at which a clinical decision follows. [S9]

And the empirical test confirms it: ACE scores predict **mean group differences** in health outcomes but show **low accuracy in identifying which individuals** will have poor outcomes. [S10] A population-level instrument is being read as a personal risk score.

**This is a validity failure in exactly the sense [[concept-reliability-validity]] defines**: validity is relative to a stated purpose. The ACE questionnaire is a perfectly good research instrument. It is not a valid *screening* instrument, because it was never validated for that purpose and does not perform at it. NCTSN's clinical bottom line: an ACE score provides **insufficient information for treatment decision-making**, and comprehensive assessment is required to establish whether traumatic stress is present. [S11] A medical specialty society's formal position locates ACEs in **population-level** prevention work. [S12]

Real-world stakes: California's ACEs Aware initiative built universal ACE screening into Medicaid practice at scale, and drew sustained clinician criticism on precisely these grounds. [S13] This is not a theoretical dispute.

**What to do if asked to administer it.** Not refusal — most likely you will work somewhere that requires it. Administer it as what it is: a **conversation opener and a population-health data point**, never a risk score, never a prognosis, and never something whose number goes into a treatment decision on its own. Do not tell anyone their score means their future is determined; it does not. [S13]

## What a validated screen looks like instead

The contrast is worth seeing concretely.

**PC-PTSD-5** (Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5) — a trauma-exposure item, and if endorsed, five yes/no symptom items about the past month. **3+ is the optimally sensitive cut point** (chosen to minimize false negatives, which is what a screen is for); **4+ suggests probable PTSD warranting further evaluation**; there is evidence a lower cut point may fit women in VA primary care. [S14] Note what exists here that the ACE score lacks: a defined purpose, validation against a criterion, published cut points, and known performance characteristics — including the sensitivity/specificity trade-off [[concept-reliability-validity]] describes.

**LEC-5** (Life Events Checklist for DSM-5) — an inventory of potentially traumatic **exposures**. Deliberately **no formal scoring protocol**: it identifies whether someone has experienced listed events and does **not** establish that exposure met DSM-5 Criterion A, nor say anything about symptoms. [S15] Typically paired with a symptom measure such as the PCL-5.

The pairing teaches the distinction that matters: **exposure and symptoms are different constructs.** Most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD. An instrument that only counts events — which is what the ACE score is — cannot tell you who is symptomatic.

## The rule that governs all of it

**Do not screen for what you cannot respond to.**

Asking someone to disclose trauma creates an obligation. If a positive screen leads to nothing — no follow-up, no referral pathway, no change in care — then you have asked a person to open something painful for an institution's benefit and given nothing back. That is a re-enactment in the precise sense of [[concept-retraumatization]]: the disclosure was extracted, the person was not helped, and control sat entirely with the organization.

So before adding any trauma screen, the questions are: **What changes based on the answer? Who responds, and how fast? Is there somewhere to refer? Is the person told what will happen to this information before they answer?** If those have no answers, the screen should not exist. This is why screening sits inside SAMHSA's implementation domains rather than standing alone — it requires the rest of the system to mean anything ([[concept-tic-implementation]]). [S16]

Three practice notes that follow:

- **Tell people what happens to the information before they answer**, including mandated-reporting limits ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]], [[concept-mandated-reporting]]). Being asked about abuse without knowing what the answer triggers is exactly the ambush the framework exists to prevent.
- **Let people decline.** A screen no one may refuse is not consistent with a framework whose fifth principle is choice.
- **Don't screen at first contact by default.** Compelled disclosure to a stranger before any relationship exists is listed in [[concept-retraumatization]] for a reason. Sometimes it is necessary; it should be a decision, not a form.
