# Psychotropic Medication Classes (the counselor's map)

A large share of your clients will be on a psychotropic medication, considering one, afraid of one, or quietly not taking the one they were prescribed. You will not prescribe, adjust, or discontinue any of them — but you are often the professional a client sees most often and talks to most openly, which makes you a crucial part of the loop: the one who **notices**, **educates**, **coordinates** with the prescriber, and **refers**. To do that you need a working map of the five classes — what they treat, roughly how they work, their notable risks — plus a clear-eyed view of what the evidence does and doesn't support. This page is the medication depth Unit 6's disorder pages deferred, and it leans hard on the [[concept-neuron-neurotransmission | agonist / antagonist / reuptake]] vocabulary.

## The five classes at a glance

| Class | Treats | Rough mechanism | Watch-fors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Antidepressants** (SSRIs, SNRIs, others) | depression, **and most anxiety disorders**, OCD, PTSD | **block reuptake** of serotonin (SSRI) ± norepinephrine (SNRI) → downstream plasticity [S8] | **4–8 wks to work**; GI/sexual side effects; **black-box: ↑suicidal thoughts under 25**; **taper — don't stop abruptly** [S6] |
| **Anti-anxiety** (**benzodiazepines**) | acute anxiety, panic, insomnia — **short-term** | **GABA-A positive allosteric modulator** (enhances the brake) [S9] | **tolerance & dependence**; dangerous **withdrawal (seizures)**; sedation; taper [S9] |
| **Antipsychotics** (typical / atypical) | psychosis, schizophrenia, bipolar, adjunct in depression | **block dopamine D2** receptors (dampen aberrant salience) [S10] | typical → **tardive dyskinesia**; atypical → **metabolic** (weight/glucose/lipids); clozapine → blood monitoring [S6] |
| **Mood stabilizers** (**lithium**, anticonvulsants) | bipolar disorder (mania + maintenance) | mechanism only partly understood | lithium: **narrow therapeutic window**, blood-level + kidney/thyroid monitoring; **may lower suicide risk** [S6] |
| **Stimulants** (methylphenidate, amphetamines) | ADHD, narcolepsy | ↑ dopamine & norepinephrine availability | ↑HR/BP, appetite/sleep effects; **low misuse risk taken as prescribed** [S6] |

A few things worth holding beyond the table.

**Antidepressants are the anxiety first-line, not just depression drugs.** Counterintuitively, SSRIs/SNRIs — not benzodiazepines — are the **first-line, long-term** treatment for chronic anxiety disorders, because they don't build dependence [S6]. Benzos are for **short-term / acute** use. This is the single most useful med fact for a counselor whose caseload is heavy on anxiety.

**SSRI vs. benzodiazepine is the discrimination to own.** Both are "for anxiety," and clients conflate them, but they are opposites in almost every way that matters: mechanism (reuptake of serotonin vs. GABA enhancement), **timeline** (weeks to build vs. minutes to act), **dependence** (essentially none vs. real), and **role** (daily maintenance vs. short-term rescue). A client saying "the anxiety pill isn't working, it's been three days" is almost certainly describing an SSRI and needs the *timeline* explained — not a reason to quit.

**"Chemical" doesn't mean "addictive."** SSRIs are **not addictive** the way benzos or stimulants can be — but stopping them abruptly can cause a real **discontinuation syndrome** (below). Keep those two facts separate; clients routinely fuse them into "antidepressants are addictive," which is inaccurate and a barrier to care.

## What the evidence actually supports (and doesn't)

This is where the counselor's job is to be neither a medication cheerleader nor a medication skeptic, but accurate.

- **Antidepressants beat placebo — modestly.** Cipriani's network meta-analysis of **21 antidepressants** found **all** more effective than placebo for acute major depression [S6] — the drugs are *not* inert. But the average effect is **modest** (standardized mean difference around 0.3), larger for **severe** depression than mild, where the drug-placebo gap narrows. So: real, useful for many, oversold for some.
- **But not via a "chemical imbalance."** The 2022 umbrella review found **no consistent evidence** that depression is caused by low serotonin [S7] — see the myth section below. The drugs work; the popular *explanation* for why they work does not hold up. The current best account is **downstream neuroplasticity** (which is why they take weeks) [S8], covered on [[concept-neuron-neurotransmission]] and [[concept-neuroplasticity-therapy]].
- **Discontinuation is real and under-warned.** Stopping an antidepressant — especially abruptly, after long use, at high dose, or with **SNRIs/paroxetine** — commonly causes **withdrawal/discontinuation symptoms** (dizziness, "brain zaps," flu-like feelings, anxiety, insomnia). Estimates range widely: Davies & Read put it near **half** of those who stop, ~half of those calling it severe; a more conservative 2024 meta-analysis, subtracting placebo effects, put the **excess** incidence around **15%** (≈1 in 6–7) [S15]. Either way it's real, it's **reduced by slow tapering**, and it's **easily mistaken for relapse** — a distinction the *prescriber* makes, not you.

## The counselor's role: educate, coordinate, refer — never prescribe

This is the scope line, and it's bright. CACREP trains counselors in medications specifically "**for appropriate medical referrals and consultation**" [S16] — recognizing, not prescribing. In practice:

- **Educate to inform a prescriber conversation, not to give medical advice.** "SSRIs usually take a few weeks to work and can have some early side effects — that's worth telling your psychiatrist" is in scope. "You should switch to a different SSRI" is not.
- **Notice and report — you often see side effects and adherence first.** New agitation, sedation, involuntary movements (tardive dyskinesia), a client quietly stopping — you flag these back to the prescriber (with consent), you don't act on them medically.
- **Never advise starting, stopping, or changing a dose.** This is the highest-stakes rule on the page. A client who feels better and wants to quit their antidepressant or their lithium is having a **prescriber** conversation — abrupt discontinuation risks withdrawal (antidepressants), relapse, or danger (stopping lithium can be destabilizing; stopping benzodiazepines abruptly can cause seizures). "That's a great thing to bring to your prescriber — let's not change anything until you've talked to them" is the counselor's line.
- **Don't recommend supplements or OTC remedies either.** Even "have you tried St. John's Wort / melatonin?" is outside scope [S16] (and St. John's Wort dangerously interacts with SSRIs). Point the client to their physician or pharmacist.
- **You are not anti-medication or pro-medication.** Your job is to hold the client's ambivalence, provide accurate information, coordinate with the person who *does* prescribe, and support the treatment plan they and their prescriber choose. (This is Unit 3's [[concept-scope-of-practice]] and [[concept-helping-professions-compared | who-does-what]] applied to pharmacology.)

## The "chemical imbalance" myth — how to talk about it

Clients often arrive with, or ask for, the chemical-imbalance story: "I have a serotonin imbalance, so I need this pill to fix it." It's an appealing narrative — it's concrete, it's destigmatizing (a broken part, not a character flaw), and it was marketed for decades. But it's **not supported** [S7], and it has a cost: framing distress as a fixed brain defect can make people feel *more* helpless and *less* likely to believe that therapy, behavior change, or their own effort matters. The honest, still-hopeful version to offer a curious client:

> "We don't actually think depression is one chemical being low — that turned out not to hold up. But medication can still genuinely help a lot of people, probably by helping the brain adapt and rewire over a few weeks. And the good news in that is that *therapy and the things you do* change the brain the same way — so it's not the pill versus you. It can be both."

That reframe keeps the medication option open, corrects the myth, and — via [[concept-neuroplasticity-therapy]] — restores the client's agency. Hold the tension explicitly: **the mechanism story is wrong; the medication can still be right for this person.**
