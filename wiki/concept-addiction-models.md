# What Addiction Is: Diagnosis and the Competing Models

"Addiction" is a lay word; the clinical object is a **substance use disorder (SUD)** — or, for one
behavior, **gambling disorder**. This page pins down the diagnosis, the pharmacology terms everyone
confuses (tolerance / withdrawal / craving), and the three **models** that fight over what addiction
*fundamentally is*. Getting the models right matters because they quietly dictate how a helper talks
to a client — as a patient, a learner, or a moral agent.

## The diagnosis: DSM-5-TR substance use disorder

DSM-5-TR collapsed the old "abuse vs. dependence" split into a **single SUD** rated on a **severity
continuum**, diagnosed from **11 criteria** organized in **four groups**. [S5]

- **Impaired control (1–4):** using more/longer than intended; a persistent wish or failed attempts
  to cut down; much time spent obtaining/using/recovering; **craving** (a strong urge to use).
- **Social impairment (5–7):** failure to meet major role obligations (work/school/home); continued
  use despite recurrent social/interpersonal problems; giving up important activities.
- **Risky use (8–9):** use in physically hazardous situations; continued use despite knowing it's
  causing a physical or psychological problem.
- **Pharmacological (10–11):** **tolerance**; **withdrawal**.

**Severity = how many criteria:** **mild = 2–3, moderate = 4–5, severe = 6+.** [S5] Two criteria is
the floor — one is not a disorder.

> **The prescribed-medication carve-out.** Criteria 10–11 (tolerance and withdrawal) **do not count**
> when the drug is taken **as prescribed under medical supervision.** [S5] A pain patient who is
> physically dependent on opioids, or someone stable on an antidepressant, is *not* thereby
> "addicted." This is the clean way to separate **physical dependence** (a normal, expected
> pharmacological adaptation) from **addiction** (the compulsive, harm-defying behavioral syndrome).

## The three terms people mash together

- **Tolerance** — needing **more** of the drug for the same effect (the reward system down-regulates).
- **Withdrawal** — the aversive physical/emotional syndrome when the drug leaves (the mirror image of
  its acute effect). Note: not all drugs produce a documented withdrawal (e.g., hallucinogens).
- **Craving** — the **wanting**: an intrusive urge, often triggered by cues, that can persist long
  after withdrawal is over and is a major engine of relapse. [S3]

Tolerance and withdrawal are **physical dependence**; craving and the loss of control are the heart
of **addiction**. You can have dependence without addiction (the pain patient) and — for some
drugs — addiction with only modest physical dependence. Don't let the words collapse into each other.

## Substance vs. behavioral addiction

The same reward circuitry can be captured by a **behavior**, not just a chemical. DSM-5-TR took the
historic step of moving **gambling disorder** in beside the substance disorders — it is the **only
recognized behavioral (non-substance) addiction** — on the evidence that it shares symptoms,
neurobiology (reward-circuit activation, the **"near-miss"** effect that keeps a near-loss feeling
like an almost-win), and treatment response with substance addictions. [S12]

**Internet gaming disorder** sits in **Section III** ("conditions for further study") — described,
studied, but **not a formal diagnosis.** [S16] Popular terms like "sex addiction," "food addiction,"
and "phone addiction" describe real, sometimes-impairing behavior but are **not** DSM diagnoses —
the classification is genuinely contested, so name the behavior precisely rather than reaching for
"addiction" as a catch-all.

## The three competing models (this is contested territory)

What *is* addiction, underneath the diagnosis? Three families of answer, and a helper's stance leaks
from whichever they hold. [S13]

| Model | Core claim | "Stuck because…" | Risk of over-learning it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brain-disease (BDM)** — NIDA/Volkow | A **chronic brain disorder**: lasting changes to reward/stress/control circuits [S1] | …your brain circuitry has been hijacked | Can imply **permanence/helplessness**; "diseased brain" can feel like a life sentence |
| **Biopsychosocial** | Product of **biology × psychology × environment**; no single cause [S1][S15] | …of a convergence of genes, learning, pain, and context | So broad it can under-specify the mechanism |
| **Learning / developmental & "disorder of choice"** — Lewis, Heyman | A **learned, motivated pattern** of a normally-functioning plastic brain; deeply hard to change but not a "disease" [S13] | …you learned a powerful coping habit that now runs itself | Can slide back toward blame if mishandled |

The honest teaching position: the **brain-disease model earns its keep clinically** — it reduces
moral blame, frames relapse as expected, and supports funding and access to care. But its critics
are not cranks: **Marc Lewis** (a developmental neuroscientist) argues the same brain data reflect
**learning and neuroplasticity**, not pathology, and a widely-cited middle position holds addiction
is **"neither a brain disease nor a moral failing."** [S13] Carry the vocabulary of all three; teach
none as settled. What they *agree* on is the practical bottom line: the behavior is **extraordinarily
hard to change by will alone**, and blame is clinically useless.

## Why this matters in the room
The model you hold picks your verbs. Hold *pure* disease and you may over-medicalize and imply the
person is passive; hold *pure* choice and you drift back toward "just stop." The workable stance
borrows from all three: the drive is **real and biologically anchored** (so drop the blame), *and*
the person retains **agency you can recruit** (so build motivation and skills). That is exactly what
[[concept-stages-of-change]] and [[concept-treatment-and-recovery]] operationalize.
