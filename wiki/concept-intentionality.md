# Intentionality

**One line:** every experience is **directed at** something beyond itself. You don't just fear; you fear *something*. You don't just remember; you remember *something*, *as* something. Phenomenology calls this directedness **intentionality**, and treats it as the central structure of consciousness. [S1][S2]

> **Not "intention" in the everyday sense.** Intentionality has nothing special to do with *intending to do* something. It comes from the Latin *intendere*, "to aim or stretch toward." A perception, a mood-laden glance, a belief, and a daydream are all intentional because each is *about* something. Beginners get this wrong constantly.

## Where it comes from

- **Franz Brentano** (1838–1917), Husserl's teacher, proposed intentionality ("reference to a content, direction toward an object") as **the mark of the mental**, the thing that distinguishes mental phenomena from physical ones. [S1]
- **Husserl** made it the centerpiece of phenomenology and analyzed its structure much more finely. [S1][S2]

## Husserl's analysis: act, content, object

Every intentional experience has a two-sided structure: [S2]

| Term | Side | Example (remembering your grandmother's kitchen) |
|---|---|---|
| **Noesis** | the *act*: the mode or quality of directedness | *remembering* (versus imagining, perceiving, dreading) |
| **Noema** | the *object-as-intended*: the object as it is meant in this act | the kitchen *as warm, safe, smelling of bread* |

The key point is that **the same object can be intended in many different ways**, and the *way* it is intended is part of the experience. The same grandmother's kitchen can be remembered as a refuge or as a place of control. The object "is" the same; the noema differs. [S2] (Scholars disagree whether the noema is a mediating entity or simply the object itself considered in the phenomenological attitude. You don't need to settle it; just see that "how it is meant" matters.) [S2]

## Why a counselor should care

Intentionality gives a precise vocabulary for something counselors do by instinct: **emotions and moods are not free-floating inner states; they are ways the world shows up.**

- "I'm anxious" is incomplete phenomenologically. *About what? Given as what?* A future that looms? A body that can't be trusted? Other people as judges? Asking *what the feeling is directed at, and how that thing is given*, is a phenomenological question. It is also the heart of a good open question and a good reflection of feeling ([[concept-oars]]).
- Cognitive therapy's "automatic thoughts" and "appraisals" share the insight that experiences are *about* things *as* something ([[theory-cbt]]). But CBT then asks whether the appraisal is **accurate**, while phenomenology first asks **how it is lived**. Same starting observation, different next move. (Flag: this comparison is an interpretive bridge, not a claim from either literature.)

## Extensions later in the module
- **Heidegger** thought Husserl's picture was still too "theoretical": our most basic directedness is *practical involvement* (using the hammer), not consciousness of objects. See [[concept-being-in-the-world]]. [S1][S3]
- **Merleau-Ponty** located a bodily, pre-reflective **motor intentionality**: the hand already "knows" how to reach the cup before any thought about the cup. See [[concept-embodiment]]. [S4]
- **Moods** turn out to be intentional in a special, global way. They are directed not at an object but at *the world as a whole*. See [[concept-mood-attunement]]. [S3][S34]
