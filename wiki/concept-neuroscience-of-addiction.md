# The Neuroscience of Addiction: Dopamine and the Three-Stage Cycle

You don't need to be a neuroscientist to counsel someone with an addiction, but a working mental model of the machinery does two things: it kills the willpower story, and it explains why the person keeps using *even when they hate it* — the hallmark that baffles families. The core idea: drugs (and some behaviors) borrow the brain's reward learning system, then bend it until **wanting** outlasts **liking** and the person is using to feel *normal*, not high. [S3][S4]

## The reward circuit and dopamine

Rewarding activities — food, sex, connection — release **dopamine** in the brain's reward pathway (the mesolimbic circuit), tagging "do that again" and driving learning. Addictive drugs release dopamine **far more** than natural rewards and more reliably, producing an outsized "this matters" signal. [S3] With repetition the system adapts: it **down-regulates** (fewer receptors, blunted response), so ordinary pleasures feel flat and it takes more of the drug to feel anything — the neural face of **tolerance**. Crucially, dopamine underwrites **wanting** (incentive salience) more than **liking**; this is why an addicted person can crave a drug they no longer even enjoy.

## Cue reactivity: why the environment pulls the trigger

The reward system is a **learning** system, so it doesn't just tag the drug — it tags everything around it: the street, the smell, the friend, the 5 p.m. slump. Those become **cues** that fire craving on their own, often below awareness. [S3] This is why relapse so often happens on returning to old places and people, and why [[concept-relapse-prevention]] is built around identifying **high-risk situations** rather than relying on resolve.

## Koob & Volkow's three-stage cycle

The most useful map is Koob & Volkow's **three-stage cycle**, each stage tied to a brain region and a shifting motivation. As addiction deepens, the person spirals through it faster and more compulsively. [S4]

| Stage | Brain region | What's happening | The pull |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Binge / intoxication** | **Basal ganglia** | Reward + **incentive salience**; use becomes a habit | Chasing the high (**positive reinforcement**) |
| **2. Withdrawal / negative affect** | **Extended amygdala** | Reward systems dampened; **stress** systems recruited; anxiety, dysphoria, irritability | Escaping the low |
| **3. Preoccupation / anticipation** | **Prefrontal cortex** | Impaired executive control; craving; planning the next use | "Just this once" overrides the brakes |

## The shift that explains "why can't they just stop"

The single most clinically illuminating fact here: the **motivation flips**. Early use is dominated by **positive reinforcement** — you use to feel good. As the cycle grinds on, reward circuitry is blunted and stress circuitry is amplified, so use shifts to **negative reinforcement** — you use to **stop feeling bad** (to quiet withdrawal, dread, and craving). [S4] At the same time, stage 3 degrades the prefrontal cortex's braking function — exactly the machinery a person would need to override the urge. So the drive to use is rising while the capacity to resist it is falling. That is not weakness; it is a system driven into a corner. It also reframes the goal: for someone deep in the cycle, sheer white-knuckle abstinence fights biology, which is why the methods in [[concept-treatment-and-recovery]] add motivation, skills, medication, and support rather than demanding more willpower.

## A caution on the neuro-story
These circuits are real and replicated, but see [[concept-addiction-models]]: the **same data** are read by critics (Lewis) as **learning and neuroplasticity** rather than disease pathology. The circuit changes are also **not simply permanent** — the brain that adapted into addiction can adapt back out, which is the neuroscience underneath the hopeful half of the 40–60% relapse statistic. Use the neurobiology to remove blame and explain compulsion; don't weaponize it into "your brain is broken forever."
