# Informed Consent

**The idea:** clients have the right to choose — freely and knowledgeably — whether to enter and
whether to *stay* in counseling. Informed consent is how that right is honored: the counselor
reviews, **in writing and verbally**, what counseling is, what it costs, what could go wrong, and
what the limits of the promise are. It is grounded in the principle of **autonomy**. [S1]

## What the Code requires (A.2)

**A.2.a — consent is a process, not a signature.** "Informed consent is an ongoing part of the
counseling process, and counselors appropriately document discussions of informed consent
throughout the counseling relationship." [S1] The intake paperwork *starts* consent; it doesn't
finish it. When the work changes — a new technique, a role change (A.6.d), a disclosure decision —
consent is revisited.

**A.2.b — what must be explained.** Counselors "explicitly explain to clients the nature of all
services provided," including, but not limited to: [S1]

- purposes, goals, techniques, procedures of services;
- **limitations, potential risks, and benefits** (yes — counseling has risks: distress can rise
  before it falls, relationships can change);
- the counselor's **qualifications, credentials, relevant experience, and approach**;
- continuation of services if the counselor is incapacitated or dies;
- the **role of technology**;
- fees and billing;
- **confidentiality and its limits** — the piece clients most need and least expect
  ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]]);
- the client's right to refuse services or any recommendation, and the consequences of refusal.

**A.2.c/A.2.d — capacity and culture.** Consent language must be developmentally and culturally
appropriate. When a client cannot legally consent (minors, adults under guardianship), counselors
seek the guardian's consent **and** the client's *assent* — their agreement, sought in terms they
can understand — and include them in decisions to the extent possible. [S1]

**A.2.e — mandated clients** (court-ordered) get told what will be shared with whom, *before* the
work starts, and retain the right to refuse services — knowing the consequences of refusing. [S1]

## What it sounds like in the room

Not a form read aloud — a conversation. Contrast:

> **Counselor A (form-first):** "Before we start, sign here, here, and initial here. Standard
> stuff — limits of confidentiality, fees, that kind of thing. Okay! So what brings you in?"

> **Counselor B (consent as conversation):** "Before you tell me your story, I want you to know how
> this works, because you get to decide if it works for you. What you say here stays here — with a
> few exceptions I'll spell out, because they matter. I'm a counselor-in-training, which means I
> meet weekly with a supervisor who oversees my work. We'd meet weekly, and we'll check in
> regularly on whether this feels useful — if it doesn't, we'll change course or I'll help you find
> someone who fits better. What questions do you have about any of that?"

Counselor A got a signature; Counselor B got *consent* — and, research on the alliance suggests,
the beginning of trust ([[concept-therapeutic-alliance]]). Note B disclosed trainee status and
supervision: that's A.2.b's "qualifications" requirement working. [S1]

## Why "ongoing" is not just a nicety

Patients demonstrably don't retain consent information: studies find clients and surrogate
decision-makers "not remember[ing] relevant information weeks after" it was given. A one-time
recitation therefore can't carry the ethical load; the fix is briefer, clearer initial disclosure
plus revisiting the relevant limit *when a situation makes it live* — e.g., re-explaining the
harm exception before asking detailed risk questions. [S4] The counterweight: exhaustively listing
every rare exception up front would be "time prohibitive and unnecessarily frightening" — so the
skill is calibrated candor, not a legal recitation. [S4]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **How much detail up front** is genuinely debated (see above) — plain-language summary + process
  view is the consensus, but where exactly to draw the line is judgment. [S4]
- **Consent for minors varies by state** — some states let minors consent to outpatient mental
  health care at specific ages, especially for substance use or crisis; check your statute. [S6]
