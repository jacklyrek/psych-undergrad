# Responding to a Disclosure

This is the page the module exists for. Everything else is context for the two or three minutes in which someone tells you they were sexually abused or assaulted, and you respond — and that response is not a soft preliminary to the real clinical work. **It is a clinical intervention with measurable effects on outcome**, and it is one you will deliver long before you are trained, possibly this year, possibly by a friend rather than a client.

## The finding

Sarah Ullman's research program measured what happens to survivors after they tell people. The core result: **social reactions to disclosure predict PTSD symptoms.** Negative reactions predict more symptoms. Positive reactions predict **greater perceived control over recovery**, and perceived control in turn predicts **fewer** PTSD symptoms. [S14]

The mediator is what makes this usable. It is not that sympathy is soothing — it is that **a good response returns control to a person whose control was taken**, and restored control is the thing that tracks recovery. That single mechanism generates almost every practice rule below, and it explains why some warm, well-meant responses still harm: warmth that takes over decisions is still taking over.

## The two kinds of harmful response

Negative reactions are not one thing. Factor-analytic work splits them into two distinct types with different consequences: [S15]

| | **Turning Against** | **Unsupportive Acknowledgment** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What it is** | Overtly negative responses aimed at the survivor | Responses that acknowledge the assault but fail the person |
| **Examples** | Blaming, doubting, questioning their account, stigmatizing, treating them as damaged or as different now, pulling away | Distracting or changing the subject, **taking control and making decisions for them**, egocentric responses ("I can't handle this," making it about your own anger or grief) |
| **Effect** | **More strongly predictive of PTSD** — the more damaging of the two | Harmful, but less strongly associated with PTSD outcomes |

This split is what makes the evidence teachable. **Doubt and blame are the worst thing you can do** — worse than being awkward, worse than saying nothing useful. And the second column names the trap for helpers specifically: *taking control* is classified as a **negative** reaction. The person who immediately calls the police, books the appointment, drives them to the hospital, or tells the family is not being neutral-but-clumsy; they are delivering a measured harm while feeling helpful.

The same pattern scales up to institutions. Campbell's work on **secondary victimization** — "the second rape" — found that most survivors who engaged legal or medical systems did not get the services they needed, and that system contact left many feeling guilty, depressed, anxious, and unwilling to seek further help, with secondary victimization **positively correlated with PTSD symptoms.** [S19] Which is why **where you refer someone is part of your response**, not an administrative afterthought — see [[concept-reporting-and-advocacy]].

## What to say

Short, plain, and repeatable under stress. The core three: [S17]

- **"I believe you."**
- **"It's not your fault."**
- **"Thank you for telling me."**

Then hand back control: **"What would be helpful right now?"** or **"What do you want to happen next?"** — and be willing to hear "I don't know," which is an answer, not a failure. Add, if it fits: *"You don't have to tell me any more than you want to,"* and *"I'm glad you're here."*

## What not to say — and why each one harms

These are the documented harmful responses. [S17] The point is not to memorize a forbidden list but to see the shared structure: each transfers scrutiny from the person who did it to the person it was done to.

| Don't say | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **"Are you sure that's what happened?"** | Doubt — the Turning Against category, most strongly tied to worse PTSD [S15] |
| **"Why didn't you fight back?"** | Presumes resistance was possible; **most survivors in the key study could not move** — see [[concept-sexual-trauma-effects]] [S21] |
| **"Why didn't you report it / tell someone sooner?"** | Treats the normal course of disclosure as suspicious [S10][S11] |
| **"What were you wearing?" / "Had you been drinking?"** | Investigates the survivor. Even asked from curiosity, it reads as building a case against them |
| **"Why are you still upset? It was years ago."** | Tells them their reaction is wrong; shame on top of trauma |
| **"Are you going to report him?"** (early, repeatedly) | Pressure toward a decision that is theirs — and pressure to report is specifically named as retraumatizing |
| **"I'll kill him."** | Egocentric response — now they are managing **your** feelings. Very common from partners and parents; see [[concept-supporting-a-survivor]] |
| **"I know exactly how you feel."** | Takes the floor. If you have your own history, that is worth bringing to supervision or your own therapy, not into this moment |

## Believe, don't investigate

The single operating principle, and the one that resolves the tension counselors feel between "believe survivors" and clinical neutrality.

**You are not a fact-finder and it is not your role to establish what happened.** [S13] A counselor who responds to a disclosure by gathering details, checking consistency, or reserving judgment pending evidence has silently switched from a therapeutic role into a forensic one — and is bad at the forensic one, because they have no training in it and no way to avoid contaminating the account.

"I believe you" is **not** a forensic conclusion. It is a statement that you are taking the person seriously, treating their account as their account, and not requiring them to prove themselves before receiving help. That is compatible with the fact that you do not, and cannot, know what happened. What it is *not* compatible with is interrogation. And the evidence that this matters inside therapy specifically: **over a third of CSA survivors report harmful therapist responses to their disclosures** — disbelief, belittlement, blame — and inadequate clinician response predicts the abuse staying hidden, alliance damage, and dropout. [S18] This is not a hypothetical failure mode. It is a documented one, committed by licensed professionals.

**Do not ask for the narrative.** Not "tell me what happened," not "walk me through it." If the person volunteers details, receive them without flinching or redirecting. If they do not, nothing is missing — detailed retelling is not what makes people better ([[concept-sexual-trauma-treatment]]) and pressing for it re-enacts the loss of control that the assault created.

## When it is a child

The rules change in three specific ways — see [[concept-child-sexual-abuse]] for the full treatment. [S12][S13]

1. **Never promise secrecy**, before or after. If a child says "promise you won't tell" — "I can't promise that, but I promise I'll do everything I can to keep you safe, and I'll tell you what I'm doing."
2. **Stay visibly calm.** A child reads your shock or anger as aimed at them and stops talking.
3. **Do not develop the account.** Report on **reasonable suspicion** — you are not required to be sure — and leave the interview to the Children's Advocacy Center. Write down what the child said in **their** words as soon as possible.

## The counselor's own reaction

Two failure modes, both common in beginners. **Flinching** — visible shock, a changed face, a shift in tone — is read instantly as disgust or as "this is too much," and shuts the disclosure down. **Over-reacting** — tears, outrage, declaring how awful it is — makes the survivor comfort you, which is the egocentric response in the Unsupportive Acknowledgment column. [S15] The target is steadiness: unhurried, unshocked, unmistakably present.

If the material lands on something personal, that is information, not failure — take it to supervision and your own therapy, per Unit 8's [[concept-vicarious-trauma]] and [[concept-counselor-self-care]]. Do not take it to the client.
