# Existential Isolation (the Third Given)

The third given is the one most often confused with ordinary loneliness. Yalom's **existential isolation** is not about having too few friends. It is the fact that **no one else can fully share my experience from the inside**, however close they are. I come into existence alone and leave it alone, and there is a gap between my consciousness and yours that no relationship fully closes. [S10]

## Three kinds of isolation — keep them apart

| Kind | What it is | Fixable by more connection? |
|---|---|---|
| **Interpersonal** | being cut off from other people: few relationships, poor social skills, geography | **yes**; the target of social-skills work, group therapy, behavioral activation |
| **Intrapersonal** | being cut off from **parts of oneself**: disowned feelings, dissociated experience | **yes**, through integration; the classic target of psychodynamic and experiential work |
| **Existential** | the unbridgeable gap between any self and any other | **no**, in principle; it can be **faced and shared**, not solved |

These distinctions come from Yalom (1980) and appear in Unit 2's [[theory-existential]]. [S10] The clinical point is that the **wrong prescription** follows from the wrong diagnosis: urging a client to "get out more" does nothing for someone whose pain is that even the people who love them *can't know what it's like*. That describes many bereaved parents, many people with a terminal diagnosis, and many survivors of trauma.

## Measuring it: the Existential Isolation Scale

Social psychology has operationalized a closely related construct. **Pinel and colleagues (2017)** defined existential isolation as the felt sense that **others do not share or understand one's experiences**, and built a 6-item **Existential Isolation Scale**. It is distinct from interpersonal isolation and from loneliness: you can have many relationships and still feel no one *gets* your experience. [S24]

Two findings matter clinically:
- **It is higher in people with non-normative experiences**, such as racial and sexual minorities, whose experiences others are less likely to share. [S25] This is an existential reading of a familiar multicultural point ([[unit04-multicultural]]): feeling unseen is partly a structural problem, not only a personal one.
- **It predicts prolonged grief.** In bereaved adults in China and German-speaking countries, higher existential isolation went with **more prolonged-grief symptoms**, with culture moderating the strength of the link. Levels of existential isolation did **not** differ by culture or gender. [S25] Bereavement often includes the sense that *no one else is grieving this loss the way I am*, and that sense appears to keep grief stuck. See [[concept-grief-models]].

(Note the difference in rigor: Yalom's three-way distinction is a clinical framework; the Pinel scale is a measured construct with validation data. They overlap but are not identical. The scale measures the *felt* sense of unshared experience, whereas Yalom's given is a claim about the human condition.)

## The defense: fusion

Faced with isolation, the characteristic defense is **fusion**: dissolving the boundary between self and other so there is no gap to feel. It shows up as merging into a partner ("we" with no "I"), compulsive sexuality or relationships used as reassurance, over-identification with a group or cause, or an inability to tolerate being alone. [S10] This links to Yalom's **ultimate rescuer** defense against death ([[concept-death-anxiety]]): someone else will carry me.

The existential answer is **not** fusion and **not** a retreat into self-sufficiency, but relationship between two people who **remain separate**. Yalom's formula is that isolation cannot be eliminated, but it can be **shared**. [S10]

## Buber: I–Thou as the relational answer

**Martin Buber** distinguished two basic ways of relating: [S7]
- **I–It**: the other as an object to be used, categorized, managed, or known about.
- **I–Thou**: genuine encounter with the other as a whole, irreducible person. The real event happens in **"the between"**, not inside either person.

I–Thou does not bridge existential isolation by merging; it depends on the other staying **other**. That is why it is the ideal the existential therapeutic relationship reaches for. It also marks a limit: much clinical work (assessment, diagnosis, treatment planning) is necessarily I–It, and moments of I–Thou cannot be produced on demand.

Buber held a public dialogue with **Carl Rogers** in 1957. [S7] It is widely reported (though the SEP entry doesn't detail it) that the two disagreed over whether the therapy relationship can be fully **mutual**. Buber doubted it, given the asymmetry of roles; Rogers thought moments of real mutuality occur. The question is still worth asking about your own work. See [[person-carl-rogers]], [[concept-core-conditions]].
