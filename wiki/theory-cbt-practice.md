# CBT in Practice

The *theory* of CBT — the cognitive model, Beck vs. Ellis, the learning tradition — is Unit 2's [[theory-cbt]]. This page is the **hands-on toolkit**: what a CBT counselor actually *does*, session by session, and the concrete techniques (the thought record, Socratic questioning, behavioral experiments) that turn the model into work. CBT is the **most-requested and most-researched** approach — the Beck Institute cites **"more than 2,000 studies"** supporting it [S1] — and its protocol-like structure is what makes it approachable. The risk that structure carries is running it *at* a client instead of *with* one; the antidote is baked into CBT's own core method (below).

## The defining features

The Beck Institute describes CBT as **"a time-sensitive, structured, present-oriented psychotherapy"** [S1]. Unpack that:

- **Present-focused.** CBT starts with what's maintaining the problem *now*, not its origins. (History matters for understanding, but the leverage is in the present.)
- **Structured.** Sessions and the overall course have a shape (below).
- **Time-limited.** Often ~8–20 sessions for a focal problem [S4], though this varies.
- **Goal-oriented and collaborative.** Counselor and client set explicit goals and work as a team.
- **Skills-building.** The aim is for the client to **become their own therapist** — CBT teaches methods the client keeps using after therapy ends [S1].
- **Homework is central.** Between-session practice is where most change happens; a CBT session without a "next step" is underpowered [S3].

## The structure of a session

A typical CBT session follows a repeatable arc [S2] — the structure *is* part of the treatment, because it models orderly problem-solving:

1. **Mood check** (often a brief measure — PHQ-9/GAD-7 from [[concept-screening-tools]]).
2. **Agenda-setting** — collaboratively, "what should we make sure we cover today?"
3. **Bridge from last session** — what stuck, what didn't.
4. **Review of homework** — non-negotiable; skipping it teaches the client homework is optional.
5. **The work** — one or two agenda items, using the techniques below.
6. **New homework**, set collaboratively and tied to the work.
7. **Summary and feedback** — the client summarizes; you ask how the session landed.

## Collaborative empiricism: the stance that keeps CBT honest

CBT's core method has a name — **collaborative empiricism** [S1][S4]. The counselor and client work *together* (collaborative) to treat the client's automatic thoughts as **hypotheses to be tested against evidence** (empiricism), not as errors to be argued away. This is the crucial stance distinction from the caricature of CBT as "positive thinking" or "telling people their thoughts are wrong." You are not the arbiter of reality correcting a faulty client; you are a co-investigator asking, *"what's the evidence? is there another way to see this? what would you tell a friend?"* This is why **Beck tests** while **Ellis disputes** (see [[theory-cbt]]) — and why CBT, done well, is not the fix-it stance this curriculum warns against, even though it looks the most like it.

## The technique toolkit

**Cognitive restructuring** — the second-wave core. Identify an automatic thought, examine the evidence for and against it, and build a more accurate, balanced alternative. The workhorse tool is the **thought record** ([[concept-cognitive-triangle]] holds the mechanics): a worksheet with columns for **situation → automatic thought → emotion (rated) → evidence for → evidence against → balanced alternative → re-rated emotion** [S3].

**Socratic questioning / guided discovery** — the *how* of restructuring. Rather than supplying the alternative thought, you guide the client to examine their own thinking through questions ("What makes you sure? What's the evidence on the other side? What would you tell a friend who said this?"). The client generates the insight, which is both more convincing and more durable [S3] (the **generation effect** from the learning-science rulebook).

**Behavioral experiments** — beliefs tested in the real world, not just on paper. A client who believes "if I speak up in the meeting, I'll be humiliated" designs a small experiment to gather data. Often more powerful than verbal restructuring because *experience* outweighs argument [S3].

**Behavioral activation** — the first-wave engine, big enough for its own page ([[concept-behavioral-activation]]): scheduling rewarding/meaningful activity to break the depressive avoidance cycle. Dismantling research suggests this behavioral piece may do much of CBT's work on its own [S5][S8].

**Exposure** — for anxiety: graded, repeated approach to feared situations so new learning can occur; the anxiety-disorder counterpart to activation ([[concept-anxiety-disorders]], [[concept-ocd]] — where exposure with response prevention is first-line).

**Identifying cognitive distortions** — teaching the client to *label* systematic thinking errors (all-or-nothing, catastrophizing, overgeneralization, mind reading, personalization) so they can catch them in real time [S3].

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Is the "cognitive" part necessary?** Jacobson's **dismantling** trial found behavioral activation *alone* did as well as full CBT-with-restructuring, and Dimidjian 2006 found BA matched medication and beat cognitive therapy for severe depression [S5][S8]. CBT works — but *why* it works may be more behavioral than the cognitive-triangle story implies. Hold the model as a useful map, not a proven mechanism.
- **Structure vs. relationship.** The protocol can seduce a technical helper into treating therapy as procedure. Outcomes still ride on the alliance ([[theory-common-factors]]); collaborative empiricism is the structural reminder that CBT is done *with* someone.
