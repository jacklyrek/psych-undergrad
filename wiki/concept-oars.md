# OARS — the Core MI Skills

**OARS** is the set of four core interviewing skills that Motivational Interviewing runs on: **O**pen questions, **A**ffirmations, **R**eflective listening, **S**ummaries [S10]. If they feel familiar, they should — **OARS is the Unit 1 microskills set** ([[concept-microskills-hierarchy]], [[concept-attending-and-listening]], [[concept-empathy]]) reused, with one thing added: a **direction**. In plain listening you reflect to *understand*; in MI you deploy the same skills to *selectively draw out and strengthen change talk* ([[theory-motivational-interviewing]]). Same engine, aimed at ambivalence. This is the "**Microskills ↔ OARS**" cross-unit thread the course-map flags — name it when you use it.

## The four skills

**O — Open questions.** Questions that can't be answered in a word, inviting the client to reflect and elaborate ("What worries you about your drinking?" not "Do you drink a lot?"). They open space; closed questions collect facts. MI favors a high ratio of open to closed, because the goal is to get the *client* talking — especially voicing their own reasons for change [S10].

**A — Affirmations.** Genuine recognition of the client's strengths, efforts, and worth — "I see you, and what you do matters" [S10]. Not empty praise ("good job!") and not the counselor's approval (which would make worth contingent — the [[concept-unconditional-positive-regard]] distinction). An affirmation names something real: *"It took real effort to come in today when part of you didn't want to."* Affirmations build the alliance and counter the shame that fuels sustain talk.

**R — Reflective listening.** The core skill and, per Miller & Rollnick, **the key to expressing empathy** [S10]. You make a *hypothesis* about what the client means and say it back as a statement (not a question). Reflections range in depth:
- **Simple reflection** — restates content or feeling ("You're worried about your health").
- **Complex reflection** — adds meaning, infers the unspoken, or continues the paragraph ("Part of you knows this has to change, even though the thought of it scares you").
In MI, reflection is also *selective*: when a client voices change talk, you reflect **that** — amplifying it by having them hear it again. A **higher reflection-to-question ratio predicts better outcomes** [S10].

**S — Summaries.** Periodically gathering what the client has said into a fuller picture ("So on one hand…, and on the other…, and you mentioned…"). Summaries show you're listening, tie threads together, and — the MI-specific move — let you **collect and hand back the change talk** so the client hears their own case for change assembled in one place [S10].

## Reflections vs. questions (the discrimination that matters)

Beginners over-question. A string of questions — even open ones — turns the session into an interrogation and puts the *counselor* in the driver's seat. The MI corrective is to **reflect more than you ask**: for many statements, a reflection ("That sounds exhausting") does more than a follow-up question ("How does that make you feel?") because it demonstrates understanding rather than demanding more disclosure. When in doubt, reflect. This is the single most common OARS error to catch in yourself.

## How OARS produces change talk

OARS is not neutral listening; it's the delivery system for MI's mechanism. You **ask** an evocative open question ("What might be some good reasons to cut back?"), the client answers with **change talk**, you **reflect** it back (they hear it twice), you **affirm** the strength it reveals, and you **summarize** a cluster of it at the end of a segment (they hear their own case, assembled). The client, not you, has spoken every argument for change — which is exactly why it sticks (the **generation effect**, and the disarmed [[theory-motivational-interviewing|righting reflex]]).

## The stance traps

- **Question-stacking.** Firing questions feels productive and is the opposite of MI. Watch your reflection-to-question ratio.
- **Cheerleading instead of affirming.** "You're doing amazing!" is the counselor's approval; a real affirmation names a specific strength the client showed and doesn't make worth contingent.
- **Reflecting sustain talk as much as change talk.** Simple listening reflects everything equally; MI is *selective* — you reflect and dwell on the change talk, and let sustain talk pass with a light, non-amplifying reflection.
