# Supporting the Acutely Bereaved — including the Death of a Child

This is the anchor case for the module: someone's child has just died, and you are in the room. The death of a child is one of the most severe losses there is and a top risk factor for prolonged grief ([[concept-grief-models]]). [S11] The good news for a helper is that being useful here requires no special eloquence — it requires **presence, restraint, and follow-through.** The bad news is that several things you'll *want* to say are actively harmful, so the discipline is mostly about what you **don't** do.

## Presence over fixing
The deepest instinct — *make this better* — is the one to suspend. You cannot make it better and attempts to (silver linings, reframes, reasons) tell the parent you can't bear to stay in it with them. What actually helps: **be there, stay, and tolerate the unbearable** alongside them. Allow **silence**; if the parent wants to fill it, they will, and if not, your steady company is itself the help. [S15] This is PFA's "Listen" without any pull toward "process" — accompany, don't excavate ([[concept-pfa-core-actions]]).

## Say the child's name
Bereaved parents overwhelmingly want their child **acknowledged** — soon after the death and for years after. **Use the child's name.** Speak about the child. Many people avoid the topic for fear of "reminding" the parent, but the parent has not forgotten; the silence reads as the child being erased. Mentioning the child, sharing a memory of them, is among the most consoling things you can do. [S14]

## What helps — concretely
- **Do a specific useful thing** rather than "let me know if you need anything." A grieving parent cannot project-manage their own support. Bring a meal, take the other kids to school, handle a load of laundry, drive them to an appointment. [S15]
- **Follow the parent's lead** on whether and how much to talk; let them set the pace and topic. [S14]
- **Check in for the long haul** — and especially on the hard dates: the **birthday**, the **anniversary of the death**, holidays. Support evaporates after the funeral exactly when the acute shock wears off and the absence sets in. Being the person still there at six months matters enormously. [S14][S15]
- **Tolerate the oscillation.** Per the dual process model, the parent will swing between devastation and functional, even ordinary moments. Don't read a calm or practical hour as "doing great" or as coldness — both modes are normal grief ([[concept-grief-models]]). [S6]

## What harms — phrases to retire
Each of these is common, well-meant, and wounding to a bereaved parent: [S14]

- **"Everything happens for a reason."** Most bereaved parents do not believe their child died for a reason; this asks them to find meaning in the unbearable on your schedule.
- **"They're in a better place."** A grieving parent often cannot imagine a better place for their child than alive, in their arms.
- **"I know how you feel."** Unless you have lost a child, you don't — and even then, every loss differs. It pulls focus toward you.
- **"At least…"** ("at least you have other children," "at least it was quick"). Any sentence that starts by minimizing the loss lands as minimizing the child.
- **Comparisons and timelines** ("you'll feel better in a year," "time heals"). They impose a schedule on something that doesn't keep one.

The throughline: don't explain the death, don't rank it, don't hurry it. Acknowledge it and stay.

## Ring Theory: who comforts whom ("comfort in, dump out")
A simple rule for not adding to a grieving person's load. [S13] Draw concentric rings: the **person at the center** of the crisis (here, the bereaved parent), then rings outward for those progressively less central — partner, close family, friends, acquaintances. The rule:

- **Comfort goes IN** — toward the center. To anyone closer to the loss than you, you offer support.
- **Complaints/needs go OUT** — away from the center. Your *own* distress about the death ("I can't believe this, I'm devastated") you process with someone **further out** than you, never dump on the parent.

So you don't tell a bereaved mother how hard *her* child's death is on *you*, or ask her to reassure *you*. If you need to grieve too (and you may), take it to your own outer ring. [S13]

## When to refer — don't carry clinical risk alone
Acute, even ferocious, grief is normal and not your cue to refer. Escalate to professional help when you see signs beyond ordinary bereavement — and especially:

- **Suicidal ideation or intent**, or expressed wish to die/join the child → this is a risk situation; hand off to crisis/clinical care now (see **[[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | Unit 8 — risk assessment]]**), don't manage it solo.
- **Inability to function** in basic ways that **persists and isn't oscillating** — no movement toward restoration-oriented coping at all over time.
- **Symptoms consistent with Prolonged Grief Disorder** past the time threshold (12 months adult / 6 months child), given the elevated PGD risk after a child's death. [S11][S12]
- Substance use escalating to cope; psychosis; self-neglect endangering health.

Referral is not abandonment — "I want to keep showing up *and* connect you with someone who does this all day" is the model. This is PFA's **Linkage** action ([[concept-pfa-core-actions]]).

## Look after yourself, too
Sitting with a parent whose child died has a cost. Helpers absorb some of it (vicarious/secondary stress — a named clinical phenomenon [[concept-vicarious-trauma | Unit 8]] covers). Use your **own** support (your outer ring per Ring Theory), don't make the bereaved person carry your reaction, and treat your self-care as part of being able to keep showing up — not a luxury. A depleted helper disappears, and disappearing is the thing grieving parents say hurt most.
