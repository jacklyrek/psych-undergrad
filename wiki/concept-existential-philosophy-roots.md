# Existential Philosophy: The Roots

Existential therapy borrowed its vocabulary (anxiety, authenticity, bad faith, the absurd, being-toward-death) from a century of philosophy. The words mean specific things, and much confusion in clinical writing comes from using them loosely. This page covers what each thinker actually contributed, as much as a counselor needs, not a philosophy survey. [S1]

## What "existentialism" is (and isn't)

It is **not a system with members**. The Stanford Encyclopedia calls it an "intellectual movement" held together by overlapping themes, and notes that the term (coined by Gabriel Marcel in 1943) was **explicitly accepted only by Sartre and Beauvoir**. [S1] Kierkegaard and Nietzsche died decades before the word existed; Heidegger and Camus rejected it. The label is applied after the fact to a family resemblance.

The shared starting point is the human **situation** rather than a fixed human **nature**:

- **Existence precedes essence** (Sartre's phrasing). There is no blueprint for a human being that you are born to fulfill. You exist first and become *what* you are through what you do. [S1][S4]
- **Facticity ↔ transcendence.** We are always both *already made* (a body, a history, a language, a social position we did not choose: **facticity**) and *self-making* (able to interpret, respond to, and go beyond those facts: **transcendence**). Existence is the tension between the two. [S1]
- **Anxiety is structural, not pathological.** Unlike fear, which has an object, existential anxiety is the felt sense of our own freedom and finitude, the recognition that "we alone are responsible for our choices." [S1]
- **Authenticity vs. conformity.** We mostly live by default, doing "what one does." Authenticity means owning one's existence rather than handing it to convention, and it does **not** mean finding a hidden "true self." [S1]

## The thinkers, one clinical idea each

### Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) — anxiety and despair
Danish, Christian, writing against abstract philosophical systems. **"Truth is subjectivity"**: the truths that matter most cannot be grasped from a detached, objective distance; they have to be lived. [S2]
- **Anxiety** is **"the dizziness of freedom."** It is what we feel looking down into our own possibilities. Kierkegaard's goal was not to eliminate it but to learn "to be anxious in the right way." [S2] This is the ancestor of Rollo May's **normal anxiety** ([[theory-existential-therapy-schools]]).
- **Despair** (*The Sickness unto Death*) is the failure to become a self. Its two forms map neatly onto clinical presentations: **despair of possibility** (lost in fantasy and endless options, never committing) and **despair of necessity** (fatalism: "nothing can change, this is just how it is"). [S2]
- **Three spheres of existence:** aesthetic (life as spectacle, avoiding boredom), ethical (committed choices sustained over time), religious (faith beyond universal ethics). [S2]

### Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) — nihilism and affirmation
A precursor rather than an existentialist; the SEP does not classify him as one. [S3]
- **"God is dead"** is a diagnosis, not a celebration. Once the religious framework that grounded Western morality becomes unbelievable, **nihilism** threatens: the collapse of all values. The task is a **revaluation of values** rather than despair. [S3]
- **Eternal recurrence** as a **test**: could you affirm your life if you had to live it again, exactly, forever? **Amor fati** means loving one's fate rather than merely enduring it. [S3]
- Clinically, Nietzsche matters mostly through Frankl, who often quoted him: *he who has a why to live can bear almost any how* ([[theory-logotherapy]]).

### Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) — being-toward-death (brief)
Covered in depth by the sibling elective at [[concept-being-in-the-world]]. What matters here: we are **thrown** into a world we did not choose; we mostly live as **das Man** ("the they," the anonymous "one does…"); **anxiety**, unlike fear, has no object and reveals our finitude; and **being-toward-death** — owning our death as *our* ownmost possibility rather than an event that happens to "people" — "individualizes" us and makes authentic existence possible. Heidegger also describes an **existential guilt** that is not moral wrongdoing: we are responsible for a life built on grounds we never chose. [S8] Yalom's treatment of death draws directly on this ([[concept-death-anxiety]]). Heidegger was a member of the Nazi Party from 1933 to 1945 and never publicly renounced it; the SEP entry discusses how far this reaches into the philosophy. [S8]

### Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) — freedom and bad faith
The movement's public face. [S4]
- **"Condemned to be free."** We cannot *not* choose; even refusing to choose is a choice. Hardship does not dictate its own meaning; we have to decide what it means. **Anguish** is the felt weight of that. [S4]
- **Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is self-deception about our freedom. The famous **café waiter** plays at *being* a waiter too perfectly, as though "waiter" were his fixed essence rather than a role he keeps choosing. [S4] Bad faith can go either way: **over-identifying with facticity** ("that's just who I am, I can't help it") or **over-identifying with transcendence** (pretending one's past, body, or situation doesn't constrain one at all). [S1] See [[concept-existential-freedom]].
- **The look.** Caught peeping through a keyhole, I suddenly experience myself as an **object** in another's gaze: the root of shame and of the struggle for recognition. [S4]
- **Later Sartre revised himself.** Freedom became **freedom-in-situation**, constrained by history, scarcity, and social structures. [S1][S4] Early Sartre's radical freedom is the version most often (and most unfairly) used against clients.

### Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) — freedom within oppression
The corrective that therapy most needs. [S5]
- **Freedom is social.** "We cannot affirm freedom without also affirming the freedom of others"; evil is the denial of freedom, one's own or others'. [S5]
- **Oppression constrains the *exercise* of freedom** by withholding the material conditions it needs (security, education, leisure). The freedom is still there, but it cannot be exercised. [S5]
- **The Second Sex**: "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman"; woman constructed as the **Other** to man's subject. [S5]
- She catalogued the ways people flee freedom: the **serious man**, who hands his values to an external authority, the **nihilist**, the **adventurer**, and others. [S5]

### Albert Camus (1913–1960) — the absurd and revolt
Rejected the existentialist label, but belongs in any clinical account. [S6]
- **The absurd** is not the world's meaninglessness alone. It is the **clash** between the human demand for meaning and the world's **silence**. [S6]
- *The Myth of Sisyphus* opens: "There is only one really serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." Camus names three responses to the absurd: **physical suicide**, **philosophical suicide** (escaping into a hope or system that explains it all away), and **revolt**, which means living lucidly *with* the absurd, without false comfort and without surrender. "**One must imagine Sisyphus happy.**" [S6] Camus's answer to his own opening question is *no*: the absurd calls for revolt, not suicide. But a client in crisis quoting Camus is still a client who needs a risk assessment ([[concept-existential-practice]]).

### Martin Buber (1878–1965) — I–Thou
Jewish philosopher of dialogue. [S7]
- **I–It** relates to the other as an object to be used, known, or categorized. **I–Thou** is genuine encounter with the other as a whole person. The real event happens in **"the between"**. [S7]
- Buber held a public dialogue with **Carl Rogers** in 1957, a direct bridge between existential philosophy and counseling ([[concept-existential-isolation]], [[person-carl-rogers]]). [S7]

### Paul Tillich (1886–1965) — three anxieties
Protestant theologian; *The Courage to Be* (1952). [S9] Tillich sorted existential anxiety into three kinds, each with a relative and an absolute form:

| Anxiety | Relative form | Absolute form |
|---|---|---|
| **Ontic** | fate | death |
| **Moral** | guilt | condemnation |
| **Spiritual** | emptiness | meaninglessness |

The **courage to be** is the self-affirmation that takes this anxiety *into* itself rather than eliminating it. [S9] The overlap with Yalom's four givens is obvious (death, meaninglessness), and so is the difference: Tillich has **guilt** where Yalom has **isolation**. It is a useful reminder that the "givens" can be carved up in more than one way.

## Compare: three thinkers on the same problem

| | The problem | The failed response | The recommended response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kierkegaard** | freedom's dizziness | despair (of possibility / of necessity) | become a self through committed choice (for him, ultimately faith) |
| **Sartre** | radical freedom | bad faith | own the choice, take responsibility |
| **Camus** | the absurd | physical or philosophical suicide | revolt: lucid living without appeal |
