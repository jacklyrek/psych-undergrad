# Duty to Warn / Duty to Protect

**The idea:** when a client poses a serious danger of violence to an **identifiable other person**,
the counselor's obligation to protect that person can override confidentiality. The doctrine comes
from the *Tarasoff* case ([[study-tarasoff]]); Justice Tobriner's formulation is the line every
program quotes: **"The protective privilege ends where the public peril begins."** [S3]

## Warn vs. protect — the distinction exams love

These are **not** synonyms, and the difference *is* the history:

- **Duty to warn** (*Tarasoff I*, 1974): the original, narrower ruling — the clinician must **warn
  the intended victim** of the threat. [S3]
- **Duty to protect** (*Tarasoff II*, 1976): the rehearing replaced warning with a broader duty to
  "take reasonable precautions to protect" the third party. Warning is now just **one option on a
  menu** — often not the best one. [S3]

The menu of reasonable protective steps: [S3]

- increase session frequency; intensify treatment
- arrange **removal of weapons** from the client's home (means restriction — reappears in Unit 8)
- initiate or adjust **medication** (via a prescriber — see [[concept-helping-professions-compared]])
- **voluntary or involuntary hospitalization**
- involve family or other third parties in the treatment
- notify the **identifiable victim and/or police** — when less intrusive measures won't suffice

This matters clinically: "duty to warn" framing pushes new clinicians toward the most
relationship-destroying option first. The actual duty, in most formulations, is to *do something
effective*, with warning as a last resort — and with **minimal disclosure** governing what you say
if you do warn ([[concept-confidentiality-limits]], B.2.e). [S1][S3]

## When is the duty triggered?

The classic threshold (per APA's 1987 formulation, echoed in most statutes): [S3]

1. a **clear threat** of killing or seriously injuring
2. a **specific, identifiable victim** (generalized anger at "everyone" typically doesn't trigger
   Tarasoff duties — though it absolutely triggers clinical risk work),
3. **intent and ability** to carry it out, with some **imminence**.

The ACA Code's own standard (B.2.a) is broader and simpler: disclosure permitted/required "to
protect clients or identified others from **serious and foreseeable harm**" — and **consult** when
in doubt. Consultation plus documentation is the universal safety net: it converts "my judgment"
into "the professional standard of care." [S1]

## The legal reality: it depends on your state

There is no single national duty. As compiled by StatPearls (2012-era count, still the standard
citation): **23 states** mandate warn/protect by statute; **~10** impose it through common law;
**~11** make it **permissive** (you *may* disclose without liability, but aren't required); and a
handful provide **no guidance**. [S3] Two extensions worth knowing: *Jablonski v. United States*
(duty includes reviewing prior treatment records for violence history) and *Ewing v. Goldstein*
(in California, a credible threat communicated to the therapist **by the client's family** can
trigger the duty — not just the client's own words). [S3]

> **Keep the duties straight** (`duty-concepts` cluster): duty to warn/protect concerns **danger to
> an identifiable third party** and involves clinical discretion in most states. **Mandated
> reporting** ([[concept-mandated-reporting]]) concerns **abuse of protected classes** (children,
> vulnerable adults), is **statutory in all 50 states**, and involves essentially **no
> discretion** — suspicion itself triggers the report. Confusing these two is the classic Unit 3
> exam error.

## What it looks like in the room

> **Client:** "I swear, if I see him with her again I'm going to put him in the ground. I have my
> brother's gun."
>
> **Weak response (avoid):** "Let's take a breath — I'm sure you don't mean that." *(Minimizes; no
> assessment; if he does mean it, you've closed the door.)*
>
> **Stronger response:** "I hear how much rage is in that. I need to take it seriously, and I want
> to understand exactly where you are with it. When you say put him in the ground — is that a plan
> you've thought about, or the size of the anger talking? … You remember when we started, I said if
> I ever believed someone was in real danger I'd have to act to keep people safe. I want us to work
> this together so nobody gets hurt and you don't lose your future to this."

Note the sequence: take it seriously → assess (plan, means, intent, timeline) → *recall the
already-disclosed limit* (this is why the informed-consent speech matters) → prefer collaborative
protective steps; warn/notify only as needed. Then **document the assessment and consult**. [S1][S3][S4]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Prediction is the weak link.** StatPearls is blunt: "guidelines on accurately predicting and
  assessing dangerousness are lacking." The law demands a judgment the science can't make reliably —
  which is exactly why consultation + documentation, not clairvoyance, is the standard. [S3]
- **Did Tarasoff chill treatment?** A long-running debate: critics argue duty-to-protect rules
  deter dangerous clients from disclosing (or entering treatment at all); defenders note the
  catastrophic alternative. Empirical evidence is thin either way. [S3][S4]
- **Scope creep:** some jurisdictions have extended protect-duties to property, to
  self-harm-adjacent situations, or to disease exposure — boundaries remain unsettled. [S3]
