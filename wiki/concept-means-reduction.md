# Means Reduction (Lethal-Means Safety Counseling)

Of everything in this unit, **means reduction is the intervention with the clearest life-saving logic** — and the one beginners are most likely to skip because the conversation feels intrusive. **Means reduction (lethal-means safety counseling)** is collaboratively putting **time and distance** between a suicidal person and the most lethal methods, until the crisis passes [S8]. It is step 6 of the [[concept-safety-planning | safety plan]], but it deserves its own page because the reasoning behind it is not obvious and, once understood, changes how you run the conversation.

## Why it works: four facts that make method access decisive

1. **Case fatality varies enormously by method.** Roughly **85–90% of firearm suicide attempts are fatal**, versus a **few percent** for overdose or cutting [S8]. The method a person reaches for in a crisis largely determines whether they live — so the *same person* with the *same intent* has a very different chance of surviving depending only on what's within reach.
2. **Suicidal crises are often brief and impulsive.** The interval between deciding and acting is frequently **minutes to an hour**. If a highly lethal method isn't at hand during that window, the acute urge often passes [S8].
3. **Substitution is incomplete.** The common objection — "they'll just find another way" — overstates it. Many people do **not** switch to an equally lethal method when the first is unavailable; removing the most lethal option is not simply redirected [S8].
4. **Most attempt survivors don't die by suicide.** About **9 in 10** people who survive an attempt do **not** go on to die by suicide later [S8]. Getting someone through the acute crisis alive is not a stopgap — for most people, it is the outcome.

Put together: because crises are transient and lethality is method-dependent, **buying time by reducing access to the most lethal means saves lives at the population level**, even accounting for some substitution. This is why it is a public-health strategy, not just a clinical nicety.

## What the conversation actually is

It targets the two most important categories for most clients [S8]:

- **Firearms** — the highest-priority means because of case fatality. Best option: **temporary off-site storage** with a trusted person, a gun shop, a range, or a police department while the person is at risk. If off-site isn't possible: locked, **unloaded**, with **ammunition stored separately** — and ideally the key/combination held by someone else.
- **Medications** — limiting quantities on hand, locking them up, using a lockbox, and disposing of excess (many overdoses use what's already in the house).

The framing is **collaborative and explicitly time-limited**: *"While things are this hard, can we make it so the most dangerous options aren't right there? We can get your firearm stored with your brother for a few weeks and get it back when you're through this."* It is **not confiscation**, not a demand, and not permanent — it's a favor the person does for their future self, arranged *with* them and, for firearms, with a responsible other actually holding the item.

## Do it even when it's uncomfortable

The evidence-practice gap here is stark: one survey found that while ~**78% of emergency departments assess** whether a patient has access to lethal means, only ~**35% actually provide means counseling** [S8]. The assessment question ("do you have access to a firearm?") is easy; the *counseling* — helping arrange to reduce that access — is what gets dropped, usually out of the clinician's discomfort, not the client's. Counselors' worry that raising firearms will rupture the alliance is generally unfounded when the tone is collaborative and concerned rather than confiscatory. **Skipping the conversation to spare your own discomfort is the failure mode**, and it's the same avoidance reflex that keeps clinicians from asking about suicide directly ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]]).

## The stance traps

- **Assessing access without acting on it.** Knowing there's a gun in the house and doing nothing about it is the most common — and most consequential — miss.
- **Confiscation framing.** "You have to get rid of your guns" invites a fight. "Let's store them somewhere safe for a few weeks" invites cooperation.
- **Treating it as optional politeness.** This is the highest-yield step in the safety plan; it is not the throwaway last line.
- **Only counting the client's action.** For highly lethal means, safety depends on a **responsible other** physically holding or securing the item — not on the at-risk person's willpower.
