# Bowlby, Ainsworth & Main

Attachment theory is unusual in psychology for having a clean division of labor across three
researchers: **Bowlby** built the theory, **Ainsworth** built the method that made it measurable, and
**Main** extended both into the hardest category and into adulthood. Knowing who did what keeps the
concepts straight (and answers a common exam question).

## John Bowlby (1907–1990) — the theorist

A British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, Bowlby broke with the psychoanalysis of his day by insisting
that a child's tie to the mother was **not** a secondary consequence of feeding (the drive-reduction
view) but a **primary, evolved survival system.** [S3][S10] His sources were unusual for a clinician:

- **Ethology** — Lorenz's *imprinting* in geese and, later, **Harlow's** rhesus monkeys who clung to
  a soft cloth surrogate over a wire one that dispensed milk, showing that *contact comfort*, not
  food, drove the bond.
- **His own clinical work** with delinquent, separated, and institutionalized children (the "Forty-Four
  Juvenile Thieves"; a 1951 WHO report on **maternal deprivation**), which convinced him that early
  separation and disrupted care had lasting effects.

From these he built the core apparatus — the **attachment behavioral system**, the **secure base /
safe haven**, the **internal working model**, **monotropy**, and a **sensitive period** — and set it
out in his trilogy *Attachment and Loss* (1969–1980). He also made the clinical bridge this whole
unit rests on: the **therapist functions as an attachment figure** in long-term therapy. [S3] (See
[[theory-attachment]].)

## Mary Ainsworth (1913–1999) — the methodologist

American-Canadian developmental psychologist who worked with Bowlby at the Tavistock Clinic and then
turned his theory into observable data — the step that made attachment a *science* rather than a
compelling idea. Two field studies anchor her contribution: [S9]

- **The Uganda study (1950s)** — naturalistic observation of Ganda infants and mothers, where she
  first identified individual differences in security and the role of the mother as a **secure base**
  for exploration.
- **The Baltimore study (1960s–70s)** — intensive home observation plus the lab procedure she is
  famous for, the **[[concept-attachment-styles|Strange Situation]]** (published in *Patterns of
  Attachment*, 1978), which yielded the three organized classifications (secure, avoidant, resistant)
  and the original ~70/15/15 proportions.

Her substantive claim was the **maternal-sensitivity hypothesis**: sensitive, consistent
responsiveness produces security. It launched decades of research — and, honestly, decades of
qualification (the modest r ≈ .24 effect, the transmission gap, temperament). [S3][S9] The method
outlived the clean causal story.

## Mary Main (1943–2023) — the extender

Ainsworth's student, based at Berkeley, who did two things the original framework needed:

- **The disorganized/disoriented (D) category** (with Judith Solomon, 1990) — for the infants whose
  behavior didn't fit any organized strategy (freezing, contradictory approach–avoidance, dazed
  states), typically where the caregiver is both the source of and the solution to fear. [S7]
- **The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)** (with Kaplan & George, mid-1980s) — which moved attachment
  measurement into adulthood by scoring the **coherence of an adult's narrative** about their own
  attachment history, not their romantic self-report. The AAI is what demonstrated the
  **intergenerational transmission** of attachment (and exposed the transmission gap). [S3][S8]

Between them, these three take you from "infants need their mothers" (a slogan) to a measured,
lifespan, clinically usable theory — with Main's contributions being exactly the parts most relevant
to trauma-affected and hard-to-reach clients.
