# Elective Module — Understanding & Changing Addictive Behavior

> **This is an off-syllabus elective module, not a numbered unit.** It lives in the `aux-`
> namespace (see the *Ad-hoc / elective modules* section of [`../CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md)) and
> cross-links forward into the planned **Unit 6 — Psychopathology & the DSM-5-TR** (where substance
> use disorders are a diagnostic category) and **Unit 9 — Evidence-Based Modalities** (where MI,
> CBT, and the change methods live). Built on request: *what addiction is, and how people actually
> break it.*

> **The one-line version:** addiction is a **chronic, relapsing** condition in which the brain's
> reward, stress, and self-control circuits are hijacked so that a person keeps using **despite
> mounting harm** — not a simple failure of willpower or morals. [S1] "Breaking it" is rarely a
> single act of will; it is a **process** — building motivation, changing behavior, and preventing
> relapse — and it is what the evidence-based methods in this module are for. [S2][S6]

## Why willpower is the wrong frame (and why that matters clinically)

The folk model of addiction is a morality play: the person *could* stop, they just don't *want* to
enough. Two things break that frame. First, the neuroscience: repeated use produces lasting
functional changes in the brain circuits for reward, stress, and executive control, so the drive to
use grows while the capacity to inhibit it shrinks — the person is fighting a rigged fight. [S1][S4]
Second, the outcome data: **40–60% of people relapse**, a rate comparable to diabetes, hypertension,
and asthma — and, as with those, relapse means the treatment needs resuming or adjusting, **not that
it failed.** [S2] The clinical payoff of dropping the willpower frame is concrete: it replaces
blame (which drives shame, which drives more use) with a **treatable-condition** stance that keeps
the person engaged.

That said — see the caution below — "it's a brain disease, not a choice" is itself a **contested**
claim, and overstating it has its own costs. Hold the frame loosely.

## The map

1. **[[concept-addiction-models]]** — what addiction *is*. The **DSM-5-TR substance use disorder**
   (11 criteria in four groups; mild/moderate/severe), the line between **substance** and
   **behavioral** addiction (**gambling** is the only recognized behavioral one), tolerance /
   withdrawal / craving, and the **three competing models** (brain-disease vs. biopsychosocial vs.
   learning/choice). [S5][S12][S13]
2. **[[concept-neuroscience-of-addiction]]** — the machinery. **Dopamine** and the reward circuit,
   Koob & Volkow's **three-stage cycle** (binge/intoxication → withdrawal/negative affect →
   preoccupation/anticipation), and the shift from chasing a high (**positive reinforcement**) to
   escaping a low (**negative reinforcement**). [S3][S4]
3. **[[concept-stages-of-change]]** — the readiness map. Prochaska & DiClemente's **Transtheoretical
   Model**: precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance, with relapse as an
   expected loop. Change is a **spiral, not a staircase**, and you meet people where they are. [S6]
4. **[[concept-relapse-prevention]]** — staying changed. Marlatt's **Relapse Prevention**:
   high-risk situations, coping and **self-efficacy**, the **abstinence violation effect** (why one
   slip becomes a landslide), and the lapse-vs-relapse distinction. [S7]
5. **[[concept-treatment-and-recovery]]** — what actually works. **Motivational Interviewing**, CBT,
   **contingency management** (gold standard for stimulants), **medications** (methadone/buprenorphine
   cut opioid mortality), **mutual-help** (AA/12-step — now Cochrane-supported — and the secular
   **SMART Recovery**), and **harm reduction vs. abstinence.** [S8][S9][S10][S11][S14]

## The stance, in one paragraph

You cannot want someone's recovery more than they do, and trying to — arguing, warning,
fixing — reliably backfires. The **righting reflex** (the helper's urge to correct the person toward
health) provokes the person to defend the very behavior you want them to drop. [S8] The counseling
task is closer to the opposite: surface the person's *own* ambivalence and reasons for change
(**change talk**), match your move to the **stage** they're actually in rather than the one you wish
they were in, and treat a lapse as **data for the plan**, not a verdict on the person. [S6][S7][S8]
Presence and patience over persuasion.

## What's genuinely contested / easy to over-learn
- **"Brain disease" is a model, not a settled fact.** The dominant NIDA brain-disease model is
  challenged by **learning/developmental** accounts (Marc Lewis) and **"disorder of choice"**
  theorists; a common middle view is that addiction is **"neither a brain disease nor a moral
  failing."** The disease frame usefully reduces blame and supports treatment access, but critics
  warn it can imply permanence/helplessness. Learn the vocabulary of all three. [S13] — see
  [[concept-addiction-models]].
- **Abstinence is not the only legitimate goal.** **Harm reduction** (reducing use and its dangers —
  clean supplies, naloxone, reduced-use goals) saves lives and engages people who won't accept
  abstinence; a rigid abstinence-only stance can push the highest-risk people away from care. The two
  are increasingly integrated, not opposed. [S11] — see [[concept-treatment-and-recovery]].
- **Not all "addictions" are equal in the manual.** Only **gambling disorder** is a recognized
  behavioral addiction in DSM-5-TR; **internet gaming disorder** is a "condition for further study"
  (Section III), and "sex/food/phone addiction" are popular usage, not formal diagnoses — real
  behavior, contested classification. [S12][S16]
- **MOUD is not "replacing one addiction with another."** This common belief is wrong and dangerous:
  methadone/buprenorphine at therapeutic doses stabilize rather than intoxicate and **measurably
  reduce death.** [S9]
