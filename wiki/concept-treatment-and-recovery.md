# Treatment & Recovery: What Actually Works

There is no single "cure" for addiction and no one method that fits everyone — but there is a real
**evidence base**, and knowing which tool matches which problem is the difference between hopeful
vagueness and actual help. This page is the toolkit: the counseling method (**MI**), the behavioral
methods (**CBT, contingency management**), the **medications**, the **mutual-help** groups, and the
**harm-reduction vs. abstinence** debate that frames all of it. The organizing truth from
[[concept-stages-of-change]]: **match the method to the person's stage and problem**, not to your
preferred ideology.

## Motivational Interviewing (MI): the engine for ambivalence

MI (Miller & Rollnick) is the method for the **contemplation** stage — a person stuck in ambivalence.
Its counterintuitive core: **you do not argue people into change.** [S8]

- **Resist the righting reflex.** The helper's instinct to correct, warn, and fix ("you need to quit")
  reliably provokes the client to defend the behavior — people talk themselves *deeper* into the side
  they argue. Resisting this urge is rule one.
- **Evoke change talk, don't supply it.** The goal is to draw out the client's **own** reasons,
  ability, need, and desire to change (**change talk**) rather than reinforcing their reasons to stay
  the same (**sustain talk**). People are persuaded by what they hear *themselves* say.
- **Roll with resistance.** Don't meet a push with a push; "resistance" is a signal to change your
  approach, not to argue harder.
- **RULE**, the pocket version: **R**esist the righting reflex, **U**nderstand the client's
  motivations, **L**isten with empathy, **E**mpower the client. [S8]

MI's relational skills (open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries — **OARS**) are the same
engine as [[unit01-helping-skills]]; MI adds the directional goal of eliciting change talk.

## The behavioral methods

- **Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT).** Identify and change the thoughts, cues, and situations that
  drive use; build coping skills. Overlaps heavily with [[concept-relapse-prevention]] (which is a
  CBT-family model). Well-established and widely used — though note the Cochrane finding below that
  manualized 12-step facilitation can **outperform** CBT specifically on continuous abstinence. [S10]
- **Contingency management (CM).** Provide **tangible rewards** (vouchers, prizes) contingent on
  **verified abstinence** (e.g., a clean urine screen). Unglamorous and underused, but CM is described
  as the **most effective, "gold-standard" treatment for stimulant use disorder** — the class
  (cocaine, methamphetamine) that has **no FDA-approved medication.** [S11] The mechanism is
  behavioral: it makes not-using immediately, concretely rewarding, competing with the drug's
  reinforcement.

## Medications (pharmacotherapy)

Medication is central for some substances and irrelevant for others — a key discrimination.

- **Opioid use disorder — MOUD.** Three FDA-approved: **methadone** (full agonist), **buprenorphine**
  (partial agonist), **naltrexone** (antagonist). The headline: **methadone and buprenorphine — not
  naltrexone — are proven to reduce overdose and all-cause mortality**, roughly halving fatal-overdose
  risk. [S9] They stabilize brain chemistry, kill cravings, and let the person engage in the rest of
  treatment. **MOUD is not "swapping one addiction for another"** — at therapeutic doses these
  stabilize rather than intoxicate; withholding them for a "medication-free" ideal is not
  evidence-based and costs lives. [S9]
- **Alcohol use disorder.** **Naltrexone** and **acamprosate** are first-line; **disulfiram** (an
  aversive) is second-line and adherence-dependent.
- **Stimulants (cocaine/meth).** **No FDA-approved medication** — which is exactly why **contingency
  management** carries the load here. [S11]

## Mutual-help groups

- **Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) / 12-step, via Twelve-Step Facilitation (TSF).** Long dismissed as
  "unproven," AA got a major evidence upgrade: a **2020 Cochrane review** found **manualized AA/TSF
  more effective than other established treatments (including CBT) for continuous abstinence**, and at
  least as good on other outcomes — at **substantially lower cost**, largely by linking people to a
  free, durable recovery network. [S10] It is spiritually framed ("higher power"), lifelong, and
  sponsor-based, which fits some people and not others.
- **SMART Recovery** — the main **secular, self-empowerment alternative** (founded 1994). A
  **4-Point Program** (build motivation; cope with urges; manage thoughts/feelings/behaviors; live a
  balanced life) built on **CBT/REBT and MI** — no higher power, no lifelong label. Evidence base is
  thinner than AA's, but it fits clients who bounce off the 12-step frame. [S14]

Matching matters: pushing an atheist client toward "give it to God," or a person who needs structure
toward pure self-direction, is another form of stage/fit mismatch.

## Harm reduction vs. abstinence (contested — hold both)

The old assumption was **abstinence or nothing.** **Harm reduction** rejects that as a precondition:
reduce the **dangers** of use for people who aren't ready or able to stop — **naloxone** (overdose
reversal), clean supplies/needle exchange, safer-use education, **reduced-use** goals, and MOUD as
harm reduction. [S11] The evidence and the field increasingly treat these as **integrated, not
opposed**: harm reduction keeps people **alive and in contact with care**, which is the precondition
for any later abstinence. A rigid abstinence-only stance can push the highest-risk people **away** from
help — and even abstinence-oriented CM programs are being pressured to accommodate reduced-use goals.
[S11] The stance: meet the goal the client will actually pursue, and keep them alive to revise it.

## Recovery is a long game, not an event

Reframe from [[aux-addiction]]: **40–60% relapse**, comparable to other chronic diseases, and relapse
means **resume/adjust treatment, not "it failed."** [S2] "Recovery" is increasingly understood as an
ongoing process supported by **recovery capital** — the internal and external resources (health,
housing, relationships, meaning, community) that make a changed life sustainable. Treatment initiates
change; recovery capital and mutual-help sustain it.

## Why this matters in the room
The two failure modes are **method-ideology** (forcing every client into your favorite modality) and
**therapeutic nihilism** ("nothing works with addicts"). Neither is true. What works is
**stage-matched, problem-matched, and often combined**: MI to build motivation, CBT/RP for skills,
medication where it's proven (opioids, alcohol), CM where it's the best tool (stimulants), mutual-help
for durable support, and harm reduction to keep the person alive long enough for any of it to land.
