# Mandated Reporting

**The idea:** every U.S. state designates certain professionals — mental health professionals
included — as **mandated reporters** who must report **suspected** abuse or neglect of a **child**
(all 50 states + DC) and, in most states, of **elders and vulnerable adults**. This is a *statutory
legal duty*: it is not optional, not a clinical judgment call about what's best for the client, and
it overrides confidentiality by design. [S5][S6]

## The elements (learn the italicized words)

- **Trigger: *reasonable suspicion*, not proof.** You report when you have reasonable cause to
  suspect — you do **not** investigate first, and you don't need to be sure. Investigation is the
  child-protective agency's job, not yours. Reporting on suspicion that turns out unfounded is the
  system working as intended. [S5][S6]
- **Timing: *immediate*.** Statutes typically require prompt reporting (often an oral report within
  24–48 hours, followed by a written one — specifics vary by state). [S5]
- **Immunity: *good faith*.** All states and DC provide immunity from liability for good-faith
  reports — even ones that prove unfounded. [S5]
- **Penalty for failure:** criminal charges (misdemeanor to felony), fines or jail, civil liability
  for harm that follows a failure to report, and licensure consequences. [S5]
- **Who's protected:** minors everywhere; **elder adults and dependent/vulnerable adults** (adults
  unable to protect themselves due to age or disability) in most states. [S5]
- **What counts:** physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, and in many states emotional abuse and
  exposure to specified dangers; some states require reporting **past** abuse disclosed by adult
  survivors under some circumstances — a genuinely tricky area; know your statute. [S5][S6]

## Mandated reporting vs. duty to warn (the confusable pair)

| | Mandated reporting | Duty to warn/protect |
|---|---|---|
| Source | **statute**, all 50 states | case law/statute, **varies by state** ([[study-tarasoff]]) |
| Protects | children, elder/vulnerable adults | identifiable third party threatened by client |
| Trigger | *reasonable suspicion* of abuse/neglect | *serious, foreseeable* threat of violence |
| Discretion | essentially **none** — suspicion → report | substantial clinical judgment (assess, choose protective steps) |
| You tell | child protective services / adult protective services | victim, police, or take other protective action |

The deep difference: mandated reporting is a **bright-line rule** (the legislature already made the
judgment call); duty to protect is a **standard** (you exercise judgment against it). New
clinicians reverse these — agonizing over whether to report clear-suspicion abuse (don't agonize;
report) while treating a specific threat as a formality (don't; assess carefully). [S3][S5]

## What it sounds like in the room

The stance challenge: making a report *while keeping the relationship*. It is usually possible —
and usually best — to tell the client, unless doing so would endanger the child. Contrast:

> **Avoidant (common failure):** say nothing, report secretly, hope it never comes up. *(When the
> client finds out — they usually do — the alliance rupture is far worse than honesty would have
> been.)*

> **Honest and warm:** "I need to stop us for a second, because something you just said matters a
> lot. You remember at the start I told you that if I heard a child might be being hurt, I'm
> required by law to report it? What you've described about how your boyfriend disciplines your son is in that category. I have to make that call. I want to do it *with* you if you're willing — and
> I want you to know this doesn't end our work. My job is still to be in your corner. Let's talk
> about what happens next."

Note the moves: recall the pre-disclosed limit ([[concept-informed-consent]] doing its job) →
name the duty plainly (no apology for the law, no blame of the client) → offer to make the report
collaboratively → recommit to the relationship. Research and practice lore agree the relationship
often survives a well-handled report — it rarely survives a concealed one. [S4][S5]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Does mandatory reporting deter help-seeking?** A live policy debate: some researchers argue
  broad mandates discourage struggling parents from disclosing and flood the system with unfounded reports; defenders point to detection gains. As a clinician your duty is unambiguous either way —  the debate is about the *law*, not your compliance with it. [S5]
- **State variation is real:** who is mandated (in ~18 states, *everyone* is), what standard
  applies, whether clergy/attorney privileges carve out — the Child Welfare Information Gateway
  maintains the state-by-state compilation; read yours before practicum. [S6]
