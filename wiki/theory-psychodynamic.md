# Psychodynamic Theory

**Stuck because:** unconscious, unresolved conflicts — usually rooted in early childhood — keep
shaping how a person feels and behaves, outside their awareness. **Better by:** bringing that
material into awareness (**insight**), processing the feeling, and working through the patterns as
they replay in the therapy relationship. [S1]

This is the oldest of the modern talking therapies and the root the others grew out of (often by
reacting against it). Freud's full **psychoanalysis** — the couch, multiple sessions a week, years of
work — has waned; the contemporary **psychodynamic** version is "less intensive" but keeps the core ideas. [S1][S5]

## The core ideas

**The unconscious.** Most of mental life happens below awareness. The unconscious is *"a vast
reservoir of often repressed or forgotten material that continues to influence behaviors and
emotions, even if people are unaware of its influence."* [S1] Symptoms are meaningful — they're the
visible end of a hidden conflict.

**Defense mechanisms.** Unconscious strategies the mind uses to manage anxiety and keep threatening material out of awareness. [S1][S2] Anna Freud systematized them and noted they can be **adaptive or maladaptive**; part of the work is making them visible. [S1] Common ones:

- **Repression** — pushing a threatening thought out of awareness.
- **Denial** — refusing to accept an external reality.
- **Projection** — attributing one's own unacceptable feeling to someone else ("*you're* angry at me").
- **Displacement** — redirecting a feeling onto a safer target (snapping at the dog, not the boss).
- **Rationalization** — inventing a logical-sounding reason for an emotionally driven act.
- **Reaction formation** — behaving as the opposite of how one actually feels.
- **Sublimation** — channeling an unacceptable impulse into a socially valued activity (often viewed
  as the most adaptive). [S2]

**Transference.** The client unconsciously redirects feelings and relational patterns from early
relationships **onto the therapist** — reacting to you as if you were a parent, say. [S1] Rather than
a nuisance, it's treated as *data*: the pattern that causes trouble in life shows up live in the room
where it can be examined and changed. **Countertransference** is the therapist's own unconscious
reaction back toward the client — to be monitored (often in supervision), not acted on. [S1]

## How change happens
Three mechanisms recur in the research: **insight** (uncovering the unconscious pattern), **affect**
(actually feeling and processing the emotion, not just naming it), and the **therapeutic alliance**
(the safe relationship that makes the other two possible). [S1] Classic techniques: **free
association** ("say whatever comes to mind"), **dream analysis**, and **interpretation** of defenses
and transference. [S5]

## Lineage (so the sub-schools don't blur)
- **Freud** (1890s) — drives, the unconscious, classical psychoanalysis.
- **Anna Freud / ego psychology** — the ego and its defenses.
- **Object relations** — early relationships with caregivers become internalized templates ("objects")
  that shape all later relating. [S1]

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)
- **Falsifiability.** The unconscious, defenses, and transference are clinically evocative but hard to
  operationalize and test cleanly — a long-standing scientific criticism. [S1]
- **Evidence base.** Reasonable support for depression and anxiety, but "limited or less robust"
  evidence for OCD, PTSD, and psychosis; treatment can be long and costly. [S1]
- **Past-heavy.** Critics (especially CBT) argue the deep focus on childhood can crowd out the
  present-day, changeable maintainers of a problem. [S5] — contrast [[theory-cbt]].
