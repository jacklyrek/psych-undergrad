# Experimental vs. Correlational (vs. Quasi-Experimental) Designs

The single most useful thing this unit can give you is a reflex that fires every time you read a claim: **what kind of study is this, and does it license the claim being made?** Ninety percent of bad science reporting — and a fair amount of bad clinical reasoning — is one specific error: treating a **correlational** finding as if it proved **causation**. "People who journal are less depressed" is not "journaling reduces depression." Learning to tell the designs apart, and to know exactly what each one can and cannot support, is the core literacy skill.

## The one question that separates the designs: was something manipulated, and was assignment random?

Two yes/no questions sort essentially every quantitative design:

1. **Did the researcher manipulate a variable** (deliberately assign an intervention/condition), rather than just measure what was already there?
2. **Was assignment to conditions random**?

- **True experiment** — **yes** to both. The researcher manipulates the independent variable **and** uses **random assignment** to conditions. Only this design supports a **causal** claim [S1][S2].
- **Quasi-experiment** — **yes** to manipulation, **no** to random assignment. There is an intervention, but people fall into groups by a pre-existing feature (clinic A vs. clinic B, this school year vs. last). It sits **between** the other two on internal validity [S3].
- **Correlational (non-experimental)** — **no** manipulation at all. The researcher **measures** two or more variables as they naturally occur and looks at their relationship. It supports claims of **association**, not causation [S1][S2].

The **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** — the design at the top of the treatment-evidence hierarchy ([[concept-evidence-based-practice]]) — is simply a true experiment applied to a treatment: participants are **randomly assigned** to the therapy vs. a control/comparison, so that (on average) the groups differ **only** in the treatment.

## Why only the experiment gets to say "caused"

A causal claim requires **three** things, and correlational designs can only ever deliver the first [S2]:

1. **Covariation** — the two variables actually move together. (Correlation gives you this.)
2. **Temporal precedence** — the cause comes **before** the effect. (Correlational snapshots often can't establish which came first.)
3. **No plausible alternative explanation** — nothing *else* explains the covariation. (This is the one random assignment is built to buy.)

**Random assignment** is the engine. By allocating people to conditions by chance, it makes the groups **equivalent on average at the outset** — on everything, measured or not, including confounds you never thought of. So if they differ **afterward**, the manipulation is the only systematic difference left standing. That is what "control" means and why the experiment earns the word *caused* [S1].

## The two traps of correlational data

A correlation of *r* between X and Y can arise three ways, and the data alone cannot tell you which:

- **X → Y** (what the headline assumes),
- **Y → X** — the **directionality problem** (maybe less-depressed people have the energy to journal, not the reverse),
- **Z → both X and Y** — the **third-variable problem** (a lurking common cause — e.g., having free time and stability drives both journaling *and* lower depression) [S1].

This is why "**correlation does not imply causation**" is the unit's mantra. Correlational research is not *bad* — it is essential when manipulation is impossible or unethical (you cannot randomly assign people to trauma, poverty, or a diagnosis), it is often the **only** ethical option, and it generates the hypotheses experiments then test. It just cannot, by itself, support a causal verdict.

## Random assignment vs. random sampling — a beginner's-mistake pair

These sound alike and do **opposite** jobs (this is a classic exam confusable):

- **Random *assignment*** — dividing your participants into conditions **by chance**. It serves **internal validity** (equating the groups → causal inference). An experiment without it is not a true experiment.
- **Random *sampling* / selection** — drawing your participants **from the population** at random. It serves **external validity** (a representative sample → generalizable results) — see [[concept-validity-threats]].

A study can have one without the other: a tightly randomized experiment on 200 undergraduates (strong assignment, weak sampling) vs. a national survey with no manipulation at all (strong sampling, no assignment).

## Where this lands clinically

You will read all three kinds of study, and the design tells you how much weight to put on the claim. An **RCT** showing a therapy beat a control supports "this treatment *works*." A **correlational** study linking a parenting style to an outcome supports "these tend to go together — worth exploring," not "this parenting *causes* that." A **quasi-experiment** comparing two clinics is suggestive but confounded (the clinics' clients differ). Matching the strength of your belief to the strength of the design **is** the skill — and it hands directly to how you rank and appraise evidence in [[concept-evidence-based-practice]].
