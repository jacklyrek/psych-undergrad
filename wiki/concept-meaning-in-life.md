# Meaning & Meaninglessness (the Fourth Given)

The fourth given is the collision between **our need for meaning** and a universe that **does not supply one ready-made**. [S1][S10] It is the given that existential philosophy, existential therapy, and empirical psychology have studied most, and they have landed in slightly different places.

## Two philosophical stances on the same problem

| | **Camus: the absurd** | **Frankl: the will to meaning** |
|---|---|---|
| The situation | we demand meaning; the world is silent; the **absurd** is the clash [S6] | the primary human motive is a **will to meaning** [S12] |
| Is meaning "out there"? | **no**; any claim that it is amounts to "philosophical suicide" [S6] | **yes, in a sense**: meaning is *discovered* in each situation, not invented; ultimately, a **super-meaning** beyond comprehension [S12] |
| The right response | **revolt**: live lucidly without appeal; "imagine Sisyphus happy" [S6] | **respond** to the meaning each situation offers, through creation, love, or attitude [S12] |

Most existential therapists work somewhere between the two: meaning is neither simply given nor arbitrarily made up, but **found through engagement**. Yalom's clinical conclusion is that meaning is a **byproduct**. Chase it directly and it recedes; **engage** (with work, people, a cause, creativity) and it tends to arrive. [S10] Frankl makes the same point: meaning comes through **self-transcendence**, by turning toward something or someone other than oneself. [S12]

## The empirical side

Existential therapy says a lot about meaning; empirical psychology has measured some of it.

**Presence vs. search.** The **Meaning in Life Questionnaire** (Steger et al., 2006) separates **presence of meaning** (my life has meaning) from **search for meaning** (I am looking for it). [S22] They are distinct factors, not opposite ends of one scale. A person can have a lot of both, or neither. The scale has **no item overlap with distress measures**, so it is not just measuring depression backwards. [S22]

> Clinically, "searching for meaning" is not automatically a problem. It can be healthy seeking. What deserves attention is high **search** with low **presence**: someone who is looking and not finding.

**Global vs. situational meaning (Park, 2010).** Park's integrative model distinguishes: [S23]
- **Global meaning**: a person's general beliefs, goals, and sense of purpose ("the world is basically fair," "hard work pays off," "I'm here to raise my kids").
- **Situational meaning**: the meaning a person gives a specific event.
- Distress comes from a **discrepancy** between the two, for example when a child's death violates "the world is basically fair." **Meaning-making efforts** try to close the gap, either by reinterpreting the event or by revising global beliefs, and the result is **meaning made**.

Park's verdict on the evidence is sobering: theory has outrun data, some parts of the model are well supported and others are not, and the **quality** of meaning-making may matter more than the **amount** of it. [S23] Endless rumination on "why did this happen?" is meaning-making *effort* without meaning *made*, and more of it is not better.

## Meaninglessness in the room

Yalom separates two things clients bring: [S10]
- **Cosmic** meaning: is there a meaning to life *as such*?
- **Terrestrial** (personal) meaning: what is *my* life for?

Clients rarely need an answer to the cosmic question. They usually need help with the terrestrial one, and the path there is almost never argument. When a client says "nothing matters," arguing ("but your kids matter!") tends to produce an argument back. Getting curious about *when* things stopped mattering, what used to matter, and what the client is still drawn toward despite the emptiness usually goes further. This is the same reasoning as avoiding the **righting reflex** in [[theory-motivational-interviewing]].

> **Safety note.** "What's the point of anything?" can be philosophy, depression, or suicidal ideation, and often it is all three. **Assess risk first** ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]], Unit 8). Camus framed suicide as *the* philosophical question and answered it with revolt, not death. [S6] A client quoting him is not thereby safe.
