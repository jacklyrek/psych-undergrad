# Attachment Theory

**The idea:** human infants are born with an evolved behavioral system whose job is to keep a
protective caregiver close. Attachment is "an organized system of behaviors that has a predictable
outcome (i.e., proximity) and serves an identifiable biological function (i.e., protection)." [S3]
It is not a byproduct of feeding or reward (the behaviorist view Bowlby rejected) and not a sign of
weakness to be trained out — it is a survival system as basic as hunger, and it runs for life. The
specific *shape* your attachment system takes is learned from how your earliest caregivers responded
when you were distressed, and it becomes a template — an **internal working model** — for closeness
ever after. That is why this is, per the syllabus, *the single most clinically useful developmental
idea*: the template walks into your office.

## Bowlby: the system and its logic

John Bowlby ([[person-bowlby-ainsworth]]) built the theory from ethology (Lorenz's imprinting,
Harlow's monkeys who chose a cloth "mother" over a wire one that fed them) and his own work with
separated and institutionalized children. Its load-bearing concepts:

- **Secure base and safe haven** — "the heart of attachment theory." [S3] A trusted caregiver is
  simultaneously a **base** the child explores *from* (attachment and exploration are a see-saw:
  confident kids explore *more*, not less) and a **haven** the child returns *to* when frightened.
  Both at once. A counselor who is a secure base does the same thing — steadies the client enough to
  go toward the hard material.
- **Internal working model (IWM)** — from the first year, the child builds a mental representation,
  a **"secure base script"** ("when I am hurt, I go to my mother and receive comfort"), of *whether
  the self is worth caring for* and *whether others are reliable*. Secure people carry coherent
  versions of this script; insecure people carry ones with gaps and distortions. It runs largely
  outside awareness and shapes adult relationships. [S3]
- **Monotropy and a sensitive period** — Bowlby argued for a bias toward one principal attachment
  figure (**monotropy**) and a developmental window in which attachments form most readily. Both
  claims are softer than he first put them — children form multiple attachments, and the "critical
  period" is better called *sensitive* — but the core survives. [S10]

### The four phases of attachment development [S10]

| Phase | Age (approx.) | What's happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-attachment** | birth – ~6 weeks | innate signals (crying, smiling, grasping) directed at *any* responsive adult |
| **Attachment-in-the-making** | ~6 weeks – 6–8 months | prefers familiar people; still accepts care from others |
| **Clear-cut attachment** | ~7 – 24 months | a specific primary figure; **separation anxiety** and stranger wariness peak |
| **Reciprocal (goal-corrected) partnership** | 24 months + | grasps that the caregiver has separate goals and will return; can negotiate |

## Ainsworth: measuring the template (the Strange Situation)

Bowlby gave the theory; Mary Ainsworth ([[person-bowlby-ainsworth]]) gave it a measurement. The
**Strange Situation** is a ~20-minute lab procedure of **eight ~3-minute episodes** for infants
roughly 9–18 months old, staging separations and reunions with the caregiver and a stranger.
Crucially, the classification is driven **not by how distressed the baby gets at separation but by
how the baby uses (or doesn't use) the caregiver at reunion.** Observers score proximity/contact
seeking, contact maintaining, avoidance, resistance, and search behavior. [S9]

The result is a small set of **attachment styles** — the confusable set this unit is built to teach
you to tell apart, covered on their own page: **[[concept-attachment-styles]]** (secure, avoidant,
resistant/ambivalent, and the later-added disorganized). The one-line map, with Ainsworth's
original Baltimore proportions: secure ~70%, avoidant ~15%, resistant ~15%. [S9]

### The maternal-sensitivity hypothesis — and its limits

Ainsworth proposed that **sensitive, consistent responsiveness** to the infant's signals produces
security; consistent unresponsiveness produces avoidance; **inconsistent** responsiveness produces
resistance. [S9] This is the intuitive causal story — and it is real but partial:

- The measured **sensitivity → security correlation is only modest (r ≈ .24)** — sensitivity
  matters, but it is nowhere near the whole cause. [S9]
- The **"transmission gap"**: a parent's own **state of mind** about attachment (measured by the
  Adult Attachment Interview) predicts the infant's classification *beyond* what observed sensitive
  behavior explains. Something is transmitted that we can't fully see in the parenting behavior — one
  of the field's central unsolved problems. [S3]
- **Temperament** (Kagan) is a live rival: maybe an infant's inborn reactivity drives the
  classification. The **interactionist** resolution (Belsky & Rovine) — temperament *and*
  caregiving, interacting — is where most of the field now sits. [S9]

So don't teach or believe "sensitive parenting → secure baby" as a clean law. It's a strong
tendency inside a multi-causal system.

## Attachment doesn't stop at infancy: adult attachment

The template persists, and adults were given their own measures: [S8]

- **Hazan & Shaver (1987)** wrote the first self-report of adult **romantic** attachment, mapping
  Ainsworth's three infant patterns onto three adult styles.
- **Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991)** produced the **four-category** model most therapists use, built
  on **two dimensions** — **anxiety** (about abandonment and one's own worth) and **avoidance** (of
  intimacy and dependence):
  - **Secure** = low anxiety, low avoidance
  - **Preoccupied** = high anxiety, low avoidance (the adult echo of resistant/ambivalent)
  - **Dismissing** = low anxiety, high avoidance (the adult echo of avoidant)
  - **Fearful** = high anxiety, high avoidance (the adult echo closest to disorganized)
- The **Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)** assesses an adult's *state of mind* about attachment from
  the **coherence of their narrative**, not romantic self-report — classifying secure/autonomous,
  dismissing, preoccupied, or unresolved. It's the AAI that shows the intergenerational link (and
  the transmission gap). [S3][S8]

## Attachment in the room (the Unit 1 handoff)

Bowlby characterized the **therapist as an attachment figure** in long-term therapy. [S3] That makes
attachment the developmental theory of the [[concept-therapeutic-alliance]]:

- A **preoccupied** client may seek reassurance, escalate distress to hold your attention, and merge
  the boundary — the pull is to over-soothe or to pull back, either of which confirms the model.
- A **dismissing** client may minimize problems, keep sessions intellectual, and treat needing you
  as weakness — the pull is to read them as "not really engaged" and give up.
- A **fearful/disorganized** client both wants and fears closeness — approach and flight in the same
  session; the alliance itself feels dangerous to them.

The clinical move is the same in every case and is pure Unit 1: **be a reliable secure base** —
consistent, non-anxious, neither flooding nor withdrawing — so that the relationship becomes a slow
disconfirmation of the old model (a "corrective attachment experience"). This is also where the
**fix-it reflex** costs the most: you cannot argue a client out of an internal working model built
over years; you can only be a different kind of relationship, repeatedly, and let the model update.

## Stability, and the crucial caveat: risk, not destiny

Attachment shows meaningful continuity, but early attachment is "not expected to be perfectly
predictive of later outcomes." [S3] Insecurity is a **risk factor for later psychopathology *if*
subsequent development occurs in the context of other risk factors** — probabilistic, moderated by
everything that comes after, and revisable. Security is a **protective buffer**, not a guarantee.
The clinical translation: an attachment history explains and predicts *tendencies*; it never
sentences a client to an outcome. (See [[unit05-development]] on equifinality/multifinality.)

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Sensitivity is only a modest cause; temperament and the transmission gap are unresolved.** [S3][S9]
- **Cross-cultural validity is genuinely in question** — the Strange Situation may be an "imposed
  etic." See [[concept-attachment-styles]] and [S6].
- **Adult "attachment style" quizzes overstate fixity.** Styles are dimensional (two continua), shift
  by relationship and over time, and self-report captures conscious romantic beliefs, not the AAI's
  state of mind. Useful, not deterministic. [S8]
