# Unit 7 — Assessment, Diagnosis & Case Conceptualization

Unit 6 gave you the diagnostic *categories*. This unit is the machinery that turns a real person in a chair into one of them — and then past it. The syllabus's framing sentence is the one to hold: case conceptualization is **"the skill that separates having a nice chat from doing therapy."** The intake is your first full session with nearly every client [S7]; everything downstream — the plan, the referral, the risk decision, the note the insurer reads — is built from what you gather and how you organize it.

This is a Tier 2 clinical-foundations unit feeding CACREP's **Assessment & Testing** area. It sits on three earlier units and hands to a fourth:

- **← Unit 2 (theory as lens).** Case conceptualization is theory *doing work*: your orientation decides what counts as the cause and what counts as the fix ([[unit02-theories]]).
- **← Unit 6 (diagnosis).** The DSM label is an *input* to the formulation, not the endpoint of thinking ([[concept-dsm-structure]]).
- **← Unit 5 (the normal baseline) and Unit 4 (culture).** You can't read a mental status exam or a screening score without knowing what's developmentally normal ([[unit05-development]]) and without the humility not to misread a cultural difference as pathology ([[unit04-multicultural]]).
- **→ [[unit08-crisis-trauma | Unit 8]] (risk).** The risk screen embedded in every intake is where this unit and crisis work overlap — built out there in full.

The unit moves in the order a case actually moves: **gather → describe → measure → judge the tools → explain → plan.**

## Assessment is not a phase — it's the whole relationship, thinly

The beginner's picture is a pipeline: assess first, then treat. The reality is that **assessment and intervention run together.** The intake interview is already an intervention — being carefully listened to is the first dose of the common factors from [[unit01-helping-skills]] — and every later session re-assesses (Is the plan working? Has risk changed?). Sommers-Flanagan's frame is that the **clinical interview is the foundational assessment tool** and that rapport and information-gathering are not a trade-off: the relationship *is* part of the data, and a client who feels judged will hand you a worse history [S7]. This is the same stance caution that runs through the whole curriculum — the reflex to *collect all the data efficiently* serves the helper; the client is served by being met.

## Four things a counselor assesses (and where each lives)

Sommers-Flanagan names four assessment-interview types [S7]; this unit covers three, and the fourth is [[unit08-crisis-trauma | Unit 8]]:

1. **Intake interview** — the most common; gathers the initial picture. → [[concept-intake-interview]]
2. **Mental status examination** — a semi-structured description of current state. → [[concept-mental-status-exam]]
3. **Psychodiagnostic interview** — pins a DSM impression to guide treatment. → Unit 6 supplies the categories; [[concept-case-conceptualization]] is where the impression becomes a plan.
4. **Suicide / risk assessment** — the highest-stakes interview. → [[concept-suicide-risk-assessment | Unit 8]] (previewed by item 9 of the PHQ-9 and the intake risk screen).

## The through-line: measure, but don't be captured by the measure

This unit hands the technically-minded student a genuine strength — structured instruments, scoring rules, reliability coefficients — and a matching trap. **Measurement-based care works**: routinely giving a PHQ-9 or GAD-7 at each visit catches non-response and deterioration earlier than clinical impression alone, and the effect is strongest for the patients who would otherwise quietly not improve [S12]. And a screen is calibrated to *over*-flag on purpose (high sensitivity), a validity coefficient is meaningless outside the norming population [S5], and a treatment plan built only from what a scale can score can shrink a person to a number and import the billing incentives flagged in [[concept-dsm-structure]]. The discipline the unit teaches: **let the numbers sharpen the clinical picture, never replace it.** A positive screen is a question, not an answer.

## The practice rep (from the syllabus)

Take a character from a novel or show and write a **one-page conceptualization**: presenting problem, relevant history, your hypothesis about **what maintains the problem** (the perpetuating factors — this is the load-bearing part), and **two measurable goals**. The "what maintains it" line is where conceptualization lives; anyone can list symptoms. (Built as a production item in the bank.)

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **A screen is not a diagnosis, and the cut-point is not a law.** ≥10 on the PHQ-9/GAD-7 is a flag calibrated for a population, not a threshold of truth (≥8 is optimal in some samples) [S3][S10].
- **Reliability ≠ validity, and neither licenses use.** A consistent test can be consistently wrong, and a valid one misfires outside its norms — the culture-fair-assessment worry from [[unit04-multicultural]] lands squarely here [S5][S11].
- **The MSE is subjective.** It's a snapshot that varies between clinicians and only *supports* a diagnosis; over-reading one MSE is a classic beginner error [S1].
- **"Measurable" can crowd out "meaningful."** SMART goals and outcome monitoring catch real problems the eye misses — and can also reduce the work to what a form can score [S12][S13].
