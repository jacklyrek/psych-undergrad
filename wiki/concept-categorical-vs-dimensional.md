# Categorical vs. Dimensional: Comorbidity and the Limits of Diagnosis

The DSM is built on a **categorical** assumption: disorders are discrete kinds — you have major depression or you don't, the way you have strep or you don't. The accumulated evidence says mental suffering doesn't carve that way, and this page is the case file: the problems (comorbidity chief among them), the dimensional alternatives, and why the categories survive anyway. The syllabus's framing — *diagnosis as shorthand, not verdict* — is not a soft disclaimer; it is roughly the scientific consensus position.

## Four cracks in the categorical system

The dimensional literature's indictment runs on four counts [S18]:

1. **Reliability.** In the DSM-5 field trials, "~40% of diagnoses examined … showed poor inter-rater agreement" — two trained clinicians, same patient, different labels, at rates no medical lab would tolerate.
2. **Comorbidity** (expanded below): categories that co-occur constantly are suspect as distinct entities.
3. **Heterogeneity.** "Individuals with the same diagnosis can have distinct, even non-overlapping sets of symptoms" — two MDD diagnoses can share almost nothing ([[concept-mood-disorders]]'s 5-of-9 structure allows it), yet the label treats them as one condition.
4. **Continuity.** "Most psychopathology falls along a continuum with normality, without the sharp break implied by categories" — no common disorder has been empirically shown to be a true natural category. The threshold (5 symptoms, not 4; 6 months, not 5) is a drawn line, not a joint of nature.

## Comorbidity: the crack that became a research program

The NCS-R numbers ([[unit06-psychopathology]]): among people meeting 12-month criteria for any disorder, **45% meet criteria for two or more** (22% two, 23% three+), and severity concentrates brutally in the comorbid few — the ~7% of the sample in the most comorbid classes hold ~44% of all serious cases. [S17] Textbook example: OCD, with 41% also meeting MDD criteria. [S1]

Two readings of these facts. The clinical one: *always keep assessing after the first diagnosis fits.* The scientific one: if "distinct" disorders co-travel this reliably, maybe the categories are slicing one underlying continuum at many points — which is exactly what the dimensional models found.

## The dimensional alternatives

- **HiTOP** (Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology) — a consortium model that replaces categories with a hierarchy of continuous dimensions: symptom components → syndromes → subfactors → **six spectra** (internalizing, antagonistic externalizing, disinhibited externalizing, thought disorder, detachment, somatoform) → a general factor at the top. A client becomes a *profile* ("internalizing: severe; antagonistic externalizing: mild") instead of a stack of six comorbid diagnoses. Dimensional scores beat categories at predicting chronicity and impairment — but note the consortium's own honesty: there is "no direct evidence yet that implementation of HiTOP diagnoses in clinical settings will improve treatment outcomes." [S18]
- **The p factor** — Caspi & Moffitt's Dunedin-cohort finding: disorder co-occurrence fits three spectra (internalizing, externalizing, thought disorder) under **one general psychopathology factor**, analogous to *g* in intelligence. Higher p → more impairment, stronger family history, worse developmental course. The p factor "explains why it is challenging to find causes, consequences, biomarkers, and treatments with specificity to individual mental disorders." [S19]
- **RDoC** — NIMH's research framework: study functional domains (e.g., threat response, reward) from genes to behavior across the normal-to-abnormal range, instead of studying DSM categories. Know its self-limit cold: "RDoC is not meant to serve as a diagnostic guide, nor is it intended to replace current diagnostic systems." Research scaffolding, not a clinical manual. [S20]
- **Inside the DSM itself:** the personality-disorders Alternative Model ([[concept-personality-disorders]]) and SUD's 11-criterion severity gradient ([[concept-substance-use-disorders]]) are dimensional thinking already in the tent.

## The other flank: diagnostic inflation

Allen Frances — chair of the DSM-*IV* task force — attacks from a different angle: not "the categories are unreal" but "the categories keep swallowing normal life." His evidence: ADHD diagnoses tripled in twenty years, childhood bipolar rose forty-fold, autism twenty-fold; one prospective cohort accumulated an 83% rate of *some* mental disorder by age 21. His mechanism: elastic boundaries plus pharmaceutical marketing that "sell[s] psychiatric diagnoses by convincing potential patients and prescribers that expectable life problems were really mental disorders." [S21] The counterargument is real too: lowered thresholds catch treatable suffering earlier, and rising counts partly reflect better detection ([[concept-neurodevelopmental-disorders]]). The bereavement-exclusion fight ([[concept-mood-disorders]]) is this debate in miniature — both sides defending clients, against different errors.

## So why does the categorical DSM survive?

Because categories are *usable*. They give clinicians a shared shorthand, researchers an entry criterion, insurers a billable code ([[concept-dsm-structure]]), and clients — sometimes — a name that converts "I'm broken and alone" into "this is a known thing that happens to people and has treatments." Don't skip that last one: a diagnosis can be received as stigma or as relief, often both. The dimensional models' own implementation barriers (assessment burden, no validated cutoffs, no reimbursement pathway) are listed by their proponents. [S18]

**The working counselor's synthesis:** diagnose categorically because the system runs on it; *think* dimensionally because the evidence runs that way. Hold every diagnosis as a compressed, culture-checked ([[concept-dsm-structure]]), revisable hypothesis about a person — Unit 7's [[concept-case-conceptualization | case conceptualization]] is where the compression gets unpacked.
