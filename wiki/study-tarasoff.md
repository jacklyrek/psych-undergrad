# Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California

**The one-line holding:** a therapist who knows (or should know) that a client poses a serious
danger of violence to an identifiable person has a legal duty to take reasonable steps to protect
that person — confidentiality notwithstanding. *"The protective privilege ends where the public
peril begins."* (Tobriner, J.) [S3]

## The facts

Fall 1968: **Prosenjit Poddar**, a graduate student at UC Berkeley, became fixated on fellow
student **Tatiana Tarasoff** after she didn't reciprocate his interest. In 1969 Poddar entered
counseling at the university health service with psychologist **Dr. Lawrence Moore**. In session,
Poddar confided his intention to kill Tarasoff (identifiable, though not named — "an unnamed girl,
readily identifiable"). Moore took it seriously: he notified **campus police**, who detained Poddar
briefly and released him after he appeared rational and promised to stay away. Moore's superior,
the director of psychiatry, ordered the clinical notes destroyed and no further action taken.
No one warned Tatiana or her family. In October 1969, Poddar shot and stabbed Tatiana Tarasoff to
death. Her parents sued the university's Regents, the therapists, and the police. [S3]

Poddar was convicted of second-degree murder; the conviction was overturned on appeal
(instructional error), and he returned to India rather than face retrial. [S3]

## Two rulings, two duties

| | Tarasoff I (1974) | Tarasoff II (1976, rehearing) |
|---|---|---|
| Duty announced | duty to **warn** the intended victim | duty to **protect** the intended victim |
| What it requires | notify the victim of the threat | "reasonable precautions" — warning is one option among several (hospitalize, treat, notify police, means removal…) |
| Why it changed | — | professional objections that warning was sometimes useless or harmful; protection is the actual goal |

The California Supreme Court's 1976 opinion is the one that governs and the one people mean by
"Tarasoff" — though, confusingly, everyday professional shorthand still says "duty to warn."
[[concept-duty-to-warn]] unpacks the operational difference. [S3]

**The court's logic:** the special relationship between therapist and patient supports imposing a
duty toward *third parties*; when the therapist determines — or under professional standards should
determine — that the patient presents a serious danger of violence to another, the therapist "bears
a duty to exercise reasonable care to protect the foreseeable victim." Notice the irony of the
facts: Dr. Moore *did* act (he called the police); liability attached to the *system's* failure to
protect the actual victim. [S3]

## Why the case matters beyond California

- It reframed confidentiality as a **qualified** promise everywhere: nearly every U.S. jurisdiction
  now has statute or case law descended from it — **mandatory** in ~23 states, common-law in ~10,
  **permissive** in ~11, silent in a few. The professional codes absorbed it (ACA B.2.a's "serious
  and foreseeable harm"). [S1][S3]
- Progeny cases extended it: *Jablonski v. United States* (must review prior records for violence
  history); *Ewing v. Goldstein* (CA: threats reported by the client's **family** can trigger the
  duty). [S3]
- Its federal-court cousin, *Jaffee v. Redmond* (1996), pulls the other direction — creating the
  psychotherapist–patient **privilege** in federal proceedings precisely because confidentiality is
  essential to treatment — with a "dangerous patient" exception that echoes Tarasoff. The pair
  bracket the field: *Jaffee* protects the seal; *Tarasoff* pierces it. [S2][S3]

## Criticisms and limits (flag these — they're real)

- **The prediction problem.** The dissent in Tarasoff and fifty years of research agree: clinicians
  cannot reliably predict violence, and validated tools are lacking. The duty effectively asks for
  foresight the science doesn't deliver; the defensible response is thorough assessment,
  consultation, and documentation. [S3]
- **The deterrence worry.** If dangerous clients know disclosures can be reported, they may not
  disclose — or not seek treatment — arguably making society *less* safe. Empirically unresolved. [S3]
- **Patchwork law.** "What does Tarasoff require?" has 50 answers. In a permissive state, warning
  without a mandate is a *choice* the ethics code's minimal-disclosure rule must discipline. [S3]
