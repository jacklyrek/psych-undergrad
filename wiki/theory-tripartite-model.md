# The Tripartite Model (Awareness · Knowledge · Skills)

**The idea:** multicultural counseling competence isn't a personality trait or a demographic fact
about the counselor — it's three learnable characteristics. Sue, Arredondo & McDavis (1992), in a
*Journal of Counseling & Development* position paper explicitly framed as "a call to the
profession," defined the multiculturally competent counselor by: [S3]

1. **Awareness** — of the counselor's *own* values, biases, assumptions, and worldview, and of the
   limits those place on their work. Not awareness of the client — awareness of **you**.
2. **Knowledge** — of the client's worldview, customs, history, and sociopolitical context
   (including how the mental-health system itself has treated the client's group).
3. **Skills** — culturally appropriate intervention strategies: the ability to *adapt what you do*,
   not just what you know.

Each characteristic is crossed with beliefs/attitudes, knowledge, and skills dimensions; Arredondo
and colleagues (1996) later operationalized the matrix into **31 specific competencies**. It became
the most widely used and most researched model of multicultural competence in counseling — the
skeleton of every "diversity course" syllabus for two decades, and the direct ancestor of the
[[concept-msjcc]]. [S3][S11]

## Keeping the three apart (the exam trap)

The dimensions are confusable precisely because real competence uses all three at once. The
discrimination test:

| Dimension | The question it answers | Failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | *What am I bringing into the room?* | a counselor who treats their own norms as the neutral default |
| Knowledge | *What shapes this client's world?* | warm, well-meaning cluelessness; "I treat everyone the same" |
| Skills | *Can I adapt what I actually do?* | culturally informed lecture-knowledge, delivered through unmodified techniques |

Worked contrast — one client, three different failures. The client is a first-generation Chinese
American student whose parents oppose her changing majors:

> **Awareness failure:** "It sounds like it's time to set some boundaries with your parents — this
> is *your* life." (Accurate reflection of the counselor's individualist value system, presented as
> if it were mental health itself.)

> **Knowledge failure:** "Wow, they're really controlling, huh?" (No concept of filial piety or of
> family-as-unit decision-making; pathologizes an interdependent norm as dysfunction.)

> **Skills failure:** "Let's do a two-chair exercise where you confront your mother." (Technique
> selected from the counselor's toolkit with no adaptation — a confrontation exercise imposed on a
> client for whom direct confrontation of a parent may be the *problem framing itself*.)

> **Competent:** "I'm hearing two things that both matter to you — the major that fits you, and
> being a good daughter, which I know isn't a small thing. I don't want to assume how those weigh
> against each other for you. Can you tell me what honoring your family looks like from inside?"
> (Awareness: doesn't export her own autonomy norms. Knowledge: recognizes the value conflict as
> cultural, not pathological. Skill: turns it into an open exploration the client steers.)

## What the research did with it

Thirty years of measurement built on this model — and the results are the unit's humbling
subplot. Self-report instruments derived from it (MCI, MAKSS, CCCI-R) correlate with **social
desirability**, and after controlling for that, self-reported competence showed *no association*
with actual multicultural case-conceptualization ability (Constantine & Ladany, 2000). Meanwhile,
what does predict process and outcome is the **client's perception** of the counselor's competence
(r ≈ .58–.72 with alliance; r ≈ .29 with outcomes — Tao et al., 2015). [S11][S9] Translation: the
model tells you what to develop; it does not license you to certify yourself as done. That
critique is half of why [[concept-cultural-humility]] exists.

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **The self-assessment problem** (above) — the model's dimensions are sound as training targets,
  but the instruments built to measure them are shaky. [S11]
- **"Competence" as a word** — Tervalon & Murray-García's endpoint critique lands directly on this
  model: you can complete a curriculum; you cannot complete a culture. Most programs now teach
  tripartite + humility as a pair. [S4]
- **Scope creep by design:** the 1992 paper was written about race/ethnicity; later frameworks
  (and the [[concept-msjcc]]) extend it to all intersecting identities. Know which scope a question
  is asking about. [S3][S1]
