# Common Factors & the Therapeutic Relationship

**The claim:** much of what makes psychotherapy work is *shared across* the different schools —
the relationship, the client's hope and expectation, a believable rationale, agreement on goals —
rather than the specific techniques that distinguish one therapy from another. This is the "common
factors" position, and it's the empirical reason Unit 1 comes first. [S3]

## Why it matters in the room
If the relationship is a primary active ingredient, then your attending, empathy, and alliance-
building (the rest of this unit) aren't "soft skills" you do before the real work — they *are* a
large part of the real work. This reframes the beginner's instinct to rush to a technique. [S3]

## The evidence backbone
- **The alliance predicts outcome, reliably.** The Flückiger et al. (2018) meta-analysis found an
  alliance–outcome correlation of **r ≈ .28** (95% CI [.256, .299]) across **295 studies and more
  than 30,000 patients** spanning 1978–2017. It is one of the most replicated associations in
  psychotherapy research. [S2]
- **It holds across orientations.** The effect appears in CBT, psychodynamic, and humanistic
  treatments alike — consistent with the alliance being a *common* factor rather than the property
  of one school. [S2][S4]
- **The APA recognizes it.** Since 2002 the alliance has been treated as an empirically validated
  relationship factor in APA reviews. [S4]

## Wampold's Contextual Model (the theory behind the numbers)
Wampold frames psychotherapy as working through three pathways: a treatment helps when (1) it
offers a **cogent rationale the client accepts**, (2) it is delivered by a clinician with whom the
client has a **genuine therapeutic relationship**, and (3) it involves **actions the client expects
to help.** On this model, specific techniques still matter — but largely *because* they give the
client and clinician something credible to do together inside a working relationship. [S3]

## Read the nuance, don't over-learn it
- The alliance–outcome figure is **correlational.** A strong alliance may cause improvement, *or*
  early improvement may strengthen the alliance, or both reinforce each other. Don't state it as
  proven one-directional causation. [S2]
- "**Relationship beats technique**" is a slogan that overshoots the data. The contemporary
  reading (e.g. Steindl et al., 2023) is that relationship and technique are interdependent — the
  technique works *through* the relationship, not in competition with it. Treat them as partners. [S3]
- Common-factors enthusiasm can be taken too far into "all therapies are equivalent, only rapport
  matters." That's stronger than the evidence; specific treatments do outperform controls for
  specific conditions.
