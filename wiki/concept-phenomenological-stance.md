# The Phenomenological Stance in Counseling

**One line:** the phenomenological stance is a disciplined way of listening. **Set aside** what you think this must be (epoché), **describe** the experience before explaining it (description), and **don't decide in advance** what matters most (horizontalization). It is how Husserl's philosophical method became a clinical habit, shared across person-centered, Gestalt, and existential therapy. [S37][S38][S39]

This is the stance side of the module: less about knowing what phenomenology *says* than about **resisting the urge to explain, categorize, and fix before you've understood.**

## Spinelli's three rules

Ernesto Spinelli, of the British existential-phenomenological school, distilled the method into three working rules for therapy. [S39] (The existentialism module gives them in brief at [[concept-existential-practice]]. This is the full treatment.)

**1. The rule of epoché (bracketing).** Set aside, as far as you can, your initial biases, theories, and expectations about what the client is presenting, so that it can show itself on its own terms. [S39] It is borrowed from Husserl's epoché ([[concept-husserl-phenomenology]]).
- *In practice:* notice the moment a label arrives in your head ("classic avoidant attachment," "this is grief," "she's catastrophizing") and hold it lightly, *as a hypothesis in brackets*, while you keep listening.

**2. The rule of description ("describe, don't explain").** Stay with *what* the client experiences and *how*, rather than jumping to *why*. [S39] Gestalt therapy puts the same rule as "what and how, not why": explanations and interpretations are considered less reliable than what is directly perceived and felt. [S38]
- *In practice:* "What is it like when the panic starts? Where do you feel it? What happens next?" before "Why do you think that is?" or "That sounds like your father again."

**3. The rule of horizontalization.** Treat every item of description as having, initially, **equal significance**. Don't rank what the client says by your theory of what's important. [S39]
- *In practice:* the throwaway comment ("anyway, my sister moved out, but that's not important") gets the same careful attention as the "main" problem. Often it *is* the main problem.

## The same stance across schools

| School | Its version of the stance | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Person-centered** (Rogers) | theory "basically phenomenological in character"; understand the client from their **internal frame of reference**, the **phenomenal field** they alone perceive [S37] | [[theory-person-centered]], [[person-carl-rogers]] |
| **Gestalt** (Perls, Yontef) | phenomenological method of **awareness**; what and how over why; the here-and-now; **dialogic** (I-Thou) relationship with *inclusion* (entering the other's experience without judging or interpreting) [S38] | this page |
| **Existential** (Spinelli; van Deurzen; Yalom) | the three rules; the therapist as fellow explorer rather than expert interpreter [S39] | [[concept-existential-practice]], [[theory-existential]] |
| **Daseinsanalysis** (Boss) | phenomenological *seeing* of symptoms and dreams as ways of being, not decoded mechanisms [S14] | [[theory-daseinsanalysis]] |
| **Psychiatric assessment** (Jaspers) | **static understanding**: describe the experience precisely, in the patient's own terms, before explaining it [S22] | [[concept-understanding-vs-explanation]] |

## Why this is hard (and why it matters)

- **The fix-it reflex and the explain-it reflex are the same reflex.** Explaining too early is a way of *managing your own discomfort* with not knowing. That is the stance problem this curriculum keeps returning to ([[unit01-helping-skills]]).
- **Premature explanation silences.** A client who hears their experience quickly slotted into your theory learns that the details don't matter, and stops offering them.
- **Description often *is* the intervention.** Being helped to describe an experience precisely, perhaps for the first time, is frequently experienced as relief and as being understood. It also produces better data for any later formulation ([[concept-case-conceptualization]]).

## The limits (don't over-learn it)

1. **Complete bracketing is impossible.** Heidegger and the hermeneutic tradition hold that all understanding runs through prior understanding. Even Merleau-Ponty said the reduction can never be completed. [S1][S48] The realistic goal is **reflexivity**: noticing your assumptions and holding them lightly. Qualitative researchers call this **bridling** rather than bracketing. [S45]
2. **Bracketing is not "having no clinical judgment."** You still assess risk, you still know the DSM, you still conceptualize. The stance governs the *order* (understand first) and the *grip* (hold explanations loosely), not whether you think at all. **Risk assessment is never bracketed** ([[concept-suicide-risk-assessment]]).
3. **Description can be misused as detachment.** A precise description of someone's world, offered without real relationship, is not phenomenological care. That is one lesson of the Ellen West case ([[study-ellen-west]]).
4. **Cultural humility is the same move.** Bracketing your own culture's "obvious" is a direct application of the stance ([[concept-cultural-humility]], [[concept-lifeworld]]).

## Micro-practice (to try in role-plays)
- For one session, ban yourself from asking "why." Use only *what*, *how*, *when*, *where*, and reflections.
- After a session, write down the first explanation that came to mind in the first five minutes. Then check: did it turn out to be right? What did it make you miss?
- Pick the least "important" thing the client said and ask about it next time.
