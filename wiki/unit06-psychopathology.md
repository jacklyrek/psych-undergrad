# Unit 6 — Psychopathology & the DSM-5-TR

This unit teaches the language of diagnosis — the major categories of mental disorder and the manual
that organizes them — while holding onto the syllabus's framing sentence: **diagnosis is a
shorthand, not a verdict.** You will be expected to formulate diagnostic impressions in practicum,
and knowing the criteria cold is also the best protection against the opposite error: calling
ordinary distress a disorder. Roughly a quarter of U.S. adults meet criteria for *some* DSM disorder
in a given year (26.2% twelve-month prevalence), and nearly half will at some point in their lives
[S17][S1] — numbers that read either as "mental illness is common" or as "the categories are drawn
too wide," and that tension is itself part of the curriculum.

This is a Tier 2 clinical-foundations unit. It feeds CACREP's **Assessment & Diagnosis** territory,
sits on the developmental baseline from [[unit05-development]] (you can't call something abnormal
without knowing what's normal for the age), and hands forward to [[unit07-assessment | Unit 7]]
(turning diagnosis into assessment and [[concept-case-conceptualization | case conceptualization]])
and Unit 8 (risk). The cultural competence of
[[unit04-multicultural]] is load-bearing here: misdiagnosis is what cultural incompetence looks like
when it holds a pen.

## What is a "disorder" in the first place?

The DSM's working answer has three recurring ingredients, and they show up in almost every criteria
set in this unit: a **clinically significant disturbance** in cognition, emotion, or behavior; that
reflects a **dysfunction** rather than an expectable response to a stressor or a culturally
sanctioned pattern; and that causes **distress or impairment** in functioning. [S1][S23] The
"distress or impairment" clause is the one beginners under-weight: symptoms alone are not a
diagnosis. Plenty of people hear a voice occasionally, check the stove twice, or feel wrecked for
weeks after a loss — the criteria are written (imperfectly) to keep those people out of the manual.
The clause is also your daily protection against over-pathologizing: **when impairment is absent,
the diagnosis usually is too.**

And a person is never the diagnosis. The style rule — "a client with schizophrenia," never "a
schizophrenic" — is not politeness theater; labels that fuse person and disorder measurably feed
stigma and biased assumptions, which is why person-first language is the professional standard.
[S1] The map is not the territory: a diagnosis compresses a person into a billing-ready noun so
clinicians can communicate, research can aggregate, and treatment can be planned. Compression loses
information. Unit 7's [[concept-case-conceptualization | case conceptualization]] is where the
information comes back.

## Comorbidity: the norm, not the exception

Memorize one set of numbers: of people meeting criteria for any disorder in a year, **55% have one
diagnosis, 22% have two, and 23% have three or more** — and severity concentrates dramatically in
the highly comorbid few. [S17] Anxiety plus depression is the everyday example; substance use
disorders co-travel with nearly everything. Clinically this means: never stop looking after the
first criteria set fits. Scientifically it means something more uncomfortable — categories that
co-occur this much may not be distinct things (see
[[concept-categorical-vs-dimensional]]).

## Culture, insurance, and the other real-world facts

Diagnosis happens inside institutions. Three facts to carry:

- **Culture first.** "Behavior that is aberrant in one culture can be standard in another" [S23];
  the DSM's own Cultural Formulation Interview exists because criteria applied without context
  misdiagnose. Details on [[concept-dsm-structure]]; foundation in [[unit04-multicultural]].
- **Insurance runs on diagnosis.** In U.S. practice, reimbursement generally requires a covered
  diagnosis code, which quietly pressures clinicians toward diagnosing — one of the counseling
  profession's standing complaints about the medical model it must operate inside. [S3]
- **Diagnosis is shared turf.** Counselors diagnose in most settings (check your state's scope
  rules — [[concept-scope-of-practice]]); psychiatrists prescribe
  ([[concept-helping-professions-compared]]). You'll coordinate across that line constantly.

## The practice rep (from the syllabus)

Pick the big three — **major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, PTSD** — and learn
their criteria well enough to **explain each to a layperson in two minutes**: what it is, what it
isn't, and what would make a clinician think of it. The two-minute constraint is the point; it
forces criteria out of list form and into understanding. (Each has a production item in the item
bank.)

## What's contested (don't over-learn it)

- **Whether the categories are real.** Poor field-trial reliability for many diagnoses, massive
  comorbidity, and within-diagnosis heterogeneity power serious dimensional alternatives (HiTOP, p
  factor, RDoC) — none of which is ready to run a clinic. [S18][S19][S20]
- **Where normal ends.** Frances's "diagnostic inflation" case (ADHD tripled, childhood bipolar up
  forty-fold) vs. the access argument that lowered thresholds catch treatable suffering earlier.
  [S21]
- **Grief.** The bereavement exclusion is gone (DSM-5) and prolonged grief disorder is in
  (DSM-5-TR) — evidence-based moves that still might medicalize mourning. [S22][S2] The PFA
  elective's [[concept-grief-models]] carries the same tension from the support side.
- **The criteria you'll memorize here are paraphrases.** This unit's numbers come from
  peer-reviewed secondary references, not the (paywalled) manual itself; before you lean on a
  criterion with a real client, read it in the DSM-5-TR. [Sources file, note 4]
