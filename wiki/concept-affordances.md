# Affordances

**One line:** we don't first perceive neutral objects and then work out what to do with them. We directly perceive **what the environment offers us for action**: this surface is walk-on-able, this handle is graspable, this person is approachable. James J. Gibson called these offerings **affordances**. [S7][S8]

Gibson (1904–1979) was an American perceptual psychologist, **not** a phenomenologist. He is in this module because his ecological psychology arrived, from inside experimental psychology, at conclusions strikingly close to Heidegger's and Merleau-Ponty's. Affordance-style reasoning also runs through Peterson's phenomenology lecture: "matching your hand to it is part of the perception of the object." [S5][S47]

## The definition

> "The affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill." Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979) [S7]

Three features make the concept powerful:
1. **Relational.** An affordance belongs to neither the environment alone nor the animal alone. It belongs to the *fit* between them. A knee-high surface affords sitting *for an adult human*, not for an ant or a toddler. Gibson said affordances imply "the complementarity of the animal and the environment." [S7]
2. **Directly perceived.** Gibson rejected the dominant *inferential* view of perception (Helmholtz, Gregory, Rock), in which the brain builds a picture from impoverished sensory data by unconscious inference. He argued the world's structure contains enough information (in the "ambient optic array") to be picked up directly by an actively moving observer. [S8][S5]
3. **Meaningful.** Affordances are *for good or ill*: food and poison, shelter and cliff edge. Perception is, from the start, perception of **meaning for action**, not of meaningless shapes that meaning is later added to. [S7][S8]

## Relation to phenomenology

| Idea | Gibson | Phenomenology |
|---|---|---|
| We first encounter things as usable, not as neutral objects | affordances | Heidegger's **ready-to-hand** ([[concept-being-in-the-world]]) [S3] |
| Perception is organized around bodily capacities | affordances are relative to the animal's body | Merleau-Ponty's **motor intentionality**, "I can" ([[concept-embodiment]]) [S4] |
| Organism and environment are a single system | "complementarity" | **being-in-the-world** as a unitary phenomenon [S3] |

The convergence is real, but the methods differ. Gibson did experiments on perception; the phenomenologists described structures of experience. Don't say "Gibson was a phenomenologist." [S5]

## Why this matters for counseling

- **The "field of affordances" shrinks in depression.** Phenomenological psychiatrists describe depression partly as a **flattened field of affordances**: things stop *inviting* action. The coffee cup no longer "calls" to be picked up and the phone no longer offers connection. [S33] That is a more precise description of anhedonia and amotivation than "lack of willpower."
- **Social affordances.** Other people afford (or fail to afford) comfort, safety, contempt. A client raised where adults afforded danger may still *perceive* danger-affordances in neutral faces, not as a conclusion they reason to but as what they directly see. That fits trauma's perceptual quality ([[concept-neurobiology-of-trauma]]). (Interpretive bridge.)
- **Changing the environment changes what's possible.** If affordances are relational, then **modifying the environment is a legitimate intervention**, not a cop-out. Examples: means reduction in suicide prevention (removing the affordance; [[concept-means-reduction]]), cue control in addiction, and structuring the physical day in behavioral activation. (Interpretive bridge. Each of those interventions has its own evidence base on its own page.)

## What's contested
- **Direct vs. indirect perception** is still a live dispute. Most mainstream cognitive neuroscience remains representational and inferential (predictive processing is currently the dominant inferential framework). The 40th-anniversary special issue on Gibson notes that no opponents of the approach submitted papers. That shows the debate persists; it does not show it has been settled. [S8]
- Whether affordances are properties, relations, or dispositions is debated among ecological psychologists themselves.
