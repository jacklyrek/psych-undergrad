# Motivational Interviewing

Motivational Interviewing (MI) is the tool for the problem every other tool assumes away: **the client isn't sure they want to change.** CBT, BA, and the rest presuppose a client who's on board. MI is what you use *before* that — with the person who is ambivalent about their drinking, their relationship, their health behavior, their treatment itself. Developed by **William Miller and Stephen Rollnick** (first book 1991, on addictive behavior; the current definition-shaping 3rd edition is 2013) [S11], MI is best understood not as a *treatment* but as **"a person-centered counseling style for addressing the common problem of ambivalence about change"** [S10]. It's the Unit 1 stance ([[theory-common-factors]], [[concept-empathy]]) sharpened to a purpose.

## Ambivalence is the target, not the enemy

MI's founding insight is that **ambivalence is normal** — holding two conflicting feelings about change at once ("I know the drinking is hurting me *and* it's the only thing that helps me relax") is the ordinary human condition of being stuck, not a deficit of knowledge or willpower [S10]. The person doesn't need to be *told* why to change (they usually know) or *scared* into it (that backfires). They need help **resolving the ambivalence in the direction of change** — and the resolution is far more durable when they voice it themselves.

## The righting reflex: the trap MI is built to disarm

Here is the whole curriculum's stance problem, named as a technical hazard. The **righting reflex** is the helper's natural urge to jump in, warn, correct, and steer the client toward the healthy choice [S10]. It feels like caring. But it reliably produces the opposite of its aim: when you argue *for* change, an ambivalent client — to stay balanced — argues *against* it, and **people talk themselves into their own positions.** Push the "you should quit" side and the client voices the "but I don't want to" side, rehearsing and strengthening the very sustain talk you're trying to reduce. MI's discipline is to **hold the righting reflex** and arrange the conversation so the *client* makes the arguments for change. If you learn one thing from this unit's stance thread, learn this: **your urge to fix, out loud, is anti-therapeutic here.**

## The spirit of MI: PACE

MI is a *way of being with* people before it's a set of techniques; run the techniques without the spirit and it becomes manipulation. The spirit has four elements — **PACE** [S10]:

- **Partnership** — a collaboration between experts: you on change, the client on their own life. Not the authority fixing the passive patient.
- **Acceptance** — comprising absolute worth ([[concept-unconditional-positive-regard]]), accurate empathy, **autonomy support** (the choice is genuinely theirs), and affirmation.
- **Compassion** — actively prioritizing the client's welfare, not your agenda or the agency's.
- **Evocation** — the motivation and resources are *already in the client*; you draw them out rather than install them. "You have what you need; let's find it," not "let me give you what you lack."

## The four processes

MI 3rd edition maps the conversation onto four sequential-but-recursive **processes** [S10][S11]:

1. **Engaging** — establish a trusting, respectful working relationship. Nothing else works without it (Unit 1 again).
2. **Focusing** — agree on a **target**: what change are we actually talking about? Keeps the conversation from sprawling.
3. **Evoking** — the heart of MI, and what distinguishes it: elicit the client's *own* motivations, so they voice the arguments for change. This is where **change talk** is drawn out and reinforced.
4. **Planning** — once the client is ready, develop a change plan they own and drive. Move here too early and you trip the righting reflex.

## Change talk vs. sustain talk (DARN-CAT)

The mechanism MI actually operates on is *client language*. **Change talk** is any client speech favoring change; **sustain talk** favors the status quo. More change talk (and less sustain talk) predicts actual behavior change, so the counselor **selectively evokes, reflects, and reinforces change talk** [S10]. Change talk comes in two stages, remembered as **DARN-CAT**:

- **Preparatory (DARN):** **D**esire ("I want to…"), **A**bility ("I could…"), **R**easons ("I'd sleep better if…"), **N**eed ("I have to…").
- **Mobilizing (CAT):** **C**ommitment ("I will…"), **A**ctivation ("I'm ready to…"), **T**aking steps ("I already…").

You listen for DARN, evoke more of it, and watch for the shift to CAT as the signal a client is moving toward planning.

## "Resistance" is discord — and it's a two-way street

MI deliberately **retired the word "resistance"** as a client trait [S10]. What looks like resistance is usually **discord** in the *relationship* — a signal that you've gotten ahead of the client, pushed too hard, or tripped the righting reflex. It's information about *your* stance, not a defect in the client. The response is not to push harder but to **change direction or listen more carefully** — using double-sided reflections, amplified reflection, reframing, and emphasizing the client's autonomy. Arguing produces sustain talk; rolling with it dissolves the discord.

## The evidence

MI shows **small-to-strong, significant effects** compared to no treatment, with the strongest support for **alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis** use [S10]. It's frequently used as a **brief prelude** (even a single session, "Motivational Enhancement Therapy") that boosts engagement with a fuller treatment. A key process finding: **counselor MI-consistent behavior** (open questions, reflections, affirmations) predicts more client change talk, which predicts better outcomes — and a **higher reflection-to-question ratio** predicts positive outcomes [S10]. The style, done faithfully, is the active ingredient.
