# Unit 4 sources — Multicultural & Social-Justice Competence

Evidence base for Unit 4 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-06. Anchored on the profession's **two framework documents** — the 1992
multicultural counseling competencies (Sue, Arredondo & McDavis) and their 2015 successor, the
MSJCC (official ACA-endorsed PDF + ACA's own companion article) — triangulated with the primary
articles behind each core concept (Tervalon & Murray-García 1998; Berry 1997; Sue et al. 2007;
McIntosh 1989; Day-Vines et al. 2007), peer-reviewed outcome research (Hook 2013; Tao 2015
meta-analysis), and the published critiques (Lilienfeld 2017; the MCC-measurement literature).
**Anchor text (not online):** Sue, Sue, Neville & Smith, *Counseling the Culturally Diverse* — used
as the structural model for how programs teach this unit; every substantive claim here is
corroborated online._

## Tier: primary framework documents (the ground truth)

- **[S1] Ratts, Singh, Nassar-McMillan, Butler & McCullough (2015) — *Multicultural and Social Justice Counseling Competencies* (official PDF, counseling.org).**
  https://www.counseling.org/docs/default-source/competencies/multicultural-and-social-justice-counseling-competencies.pdf
  _The primary document — endorsed by AMCD (June 29, 2015) and ACA (July 20, 2015). Authority: highest (it IS the object of study)._
  Structure: **four developmental domains** — (1) counselor self-awareness, (2) client worldview,
  (3) counseling relationship, (4) counseling and advocacy interventions; the first three each carry
  **aspirational competencies AKSA** — attitudes & beliefs, knowledge, skills, **action** (the
  addition to the 1992 triad). **Four quadrants** crossing counselor × client privileged/marginalized
  statuses: I privileged counselor–marginalized client; II privileged–privileged; III marginalized
  counselor–privileged client; IV marginalized–marginalized — identities are fluid and
  intersectional, so counselor and client each hold both privileged and marginalized statuses at
  once. Advocacy domain is organized on a **socioecological model** with six levels: intrapersonal →
  interpersonal → institutional → community → public policy → international/global.

- **[S2] Ratts, Singh, Butler, Nassar-McMillan & McCullough — *Multicultural and Social Justice Counseling Competencies: Practical applications in counseling* (Counseling Today / counseling.org).**
  https://www.counseling.org/publications/counseling-today-magazine/article-archive/article/legacy/multicultural-and-social-justice-counseling-competencies-practical-applications-in-counseling
  _The authors' own plain-language companion in ACA's magazine. Authority: high (same authors, ACA outlet)._
  Quadrant wording ("reflect the fluidity of identities and how dynamics of power, privilege and
  oppression impact the counseling relationship"); worked advocacy examples (school counselor gets a
  wheelchair-accessible route fixed; connecting clients to identity-affirming community
  organizations); explicit framing of MSJCC as an update of the 1992 competencies that moves beyond
  "majority counselor / minority client" to bidirectional, intersectional privilege dynamics.

- **[S3] Sue, Arredondo & McDavis (1992). *Multicultural Counseling Competencies and Standards: A Call to the Profession.* Journal of Counseling & Development, 70(4), 477–486.**
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1556-6676.1992.tb01642.x
  _The founding document of the tripartite model (paywalled; abstract + heavy secondary corroboration via [S2][S11][S12]). Authority: highest for what the 1992 model says._
  Defines multicultural counseling competence as three characteristics: (1) **awareness** of the
  counselor's own values, biases, and worldview; (2) **knowledge** of the client's worldview,
  customs, and sociopolitical context; (3) culturally appropriate intervention **skills** — each
  crossed with beliefs/attitudes, knowledge, and skills dimensions. Operationalized into 31
  competencies by Arredondo et al. (1996). The most widely used and researched MCC model in the
  literature. [S11][S12]

- **[S4] Tervalon, M., & Murray-García, J. (1998). *Cultural Humility Versus Cultural Competence: A Critical Distinction in Defining Physician Training Outcomes in Multicultural Education.* J Health Care Poor Underserved, 9(2), 117–125.**
  https://melanietervalon.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/CulturalHumility_Tervalon-and-Murray-Garcia-Article.pdf
  (mirror: https://med.umn.edu/sites/med.umn.edu/files/cultural_humility_versus_cultural_competence.jhealthpoorunderserved.1998.pdf)
  _The primary article that coined "cultural humility" (author-hosted + UMN-hosted PDFs; content corroborated via [S13] and secondary summaries). Authority: highest for the concept. Cited in 1,500+ peer-reviewed articles by 2019._
  Cultural humility = "a lifelong commitment to self-evaluation and self-critique, to redressing
  the power imbalances … and to developing mutually beneficial and non-paternalistic … partnerships
  with communities." Three commitments: (1) lifelong self-evaluation & self-critique, (2) redressing
  power imbalances in the clinician–client dyad, (3) institutional accountability / non-paternalistic
  partnership. Core critique of "competence": it implies a masterable endpoint — a finite body of
  knowledge one can complete — which misrepresents culture and invites false confidence.

- **[S5] McIntosh, P. (1989). *White Privilege: Unpacking the Invisible Knapsack.* Peace and Freedom Magazine.**
  https://med.umn.edu/sites/med.umn.edu/files/2022-12/White-Privilege_McIntosh-1989.pdf
  _The primary essay (UMN-hosted PDF; also hosted by the National SEED Project, which McIntosh founded). Authority: highest for the concept; the standard teaching text on privilege._
  Privilege as "an invisible package of unearned assets" — the knapsack metaphor; ~50 concrete
  everyday examples; the key move is that privilege is **unearned and invisible to its holder**
  ("invisible systems conferring dominance"), which is why counselor self-awareness work is required
  rather than optional.

## Tier: peer-reviewed primary research (lean on these)

- **[S6] Sue, D. W., Capodilupo, Torino, Bucceri, Holder, Nadal & Esquilin (2007). *Racial Microaggressions in Everyday Life: Implications for Clinical Practice.* American Psychologist, 62(4), 271–286.**
  https://www.law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/default/files/event/263076/media/slspublic/Sue-RadicalMicroagressionsInEverydayLife.pdf
  (PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516773/)
  _Peer-reviewed (American Psychologist); the taxonomy's primary source. Authority: high._
  Microaggressions = "brief and commonplace daily verbal, behavioral, or environmental indignities,
  whether intentional or unintentional, that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative racial
  slights and insults." Taxonomy: **microassault** (explicit, usually conscious derogation —
  name-calling, deliberate exclusion), **microinsult** (rude/demeaning slights, often unconscious —
  "you're so articulate"), **microinvalidation** (negating the person's experiential reality —
  "I don't see color," "anyone can succeed if they work hard"). Clinical relevance: they occur *in*
  therapy and rupture the alliance.

- **[S7] Berry, J. W. (1997). *Immigration, Acculturation, and Adaptation.* Applied Psychology: An International Review, 46(1), 5–34.**
  https://iaap-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1464-0597.1997.tb01087.x
  _The canonical acculturation framework (paywalled; abstract + corroboration via [S14]); cited 16,000+ times. Authority: highest for the model._
  Two independent dimensions: (1) maintain heritage culture/identity? (2) seek contact/participation
  with the larger society? Crossing them yields four strategies: **integration** (yes/yes),
  **assimilation** (no/yes), **separation** (yes/no), **marginalization** (no/no — originally
  "deculturation"). Acculturative stress; integration consistently predicts the best psychological
  and sociocultural adaptation, marginalization the worst.

- **[S8] Hook, Davis, Owen, Worthington & Utsey (2013). *Cultural Humility: Measuring Openness to Culturally Diverse Clients.* Journal of Counseling Psychology, 60(3), 353–366.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25602610/ (correction notice; original 2013 JCP)
  _Peer-reviewed; the empirical operationalization of cultural humility in therapy. Authority: high._
  Defines cultural humility as an **other-oriented (rather than self-focused) interpersonal
  stance**, marked by respect and lack of superiority toward the client's cultural background.
  Client-rated therapist cultural humility → stronger working alliance → better therapy outcomes
  (alliance mediates). Notable: it is the **client's perception** that carries the effect.

- **[S9] Tao, Owen, Pace & Imel (2015). *A Meta-Analysis of Multicultural Competencies and Psychotherapy Process and Outcome.* Journal of Counseling Psychology, 62(3), 337–350.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26167650/
  _Peer-reviewed meta-analysis (20 independent samples). Authority: high — the best single summary of whether MCC "works."_
  Client-perceived therapist MCC correlates strongly with process variables (r ≈ .58–.72 with
  alliance, satisfaction) and **moderately with outcomes (r ≈ .29)**. Key caveat: effects are for
  *client perceptions*; therapist self-reported MCC is a much weaker predictor.

- **[S10] Day-Vines, N. L., et al. (2007). *Broaching the Subjects of Race, Ethnicity, and Culture During the Counseling Process.* Journal of Counseling & Development, 85(4), 401–409.**
  https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ775546
  _Peer-reviewed (JCD); the standard framework for raising culture in session. Authority: high._
  Broaching = the counselor's ability to consider and *raise* how sociopolitical factors (race,
  ethnicity, culture) influence the client's concerns. Continuum of five styles: **avoidant** →
  **isolating** → **continuing/incongruent** (awkward, mechanical) → **integrated/congruent**
  (meaningful, substantive) → **infusing** (extends beyond sessions into the counselor's life).
  Broaching done well strengthens the alliance and outcomes; a 2021 JCD update
  (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jcad.12380) adds concrete strategies.

## Tier: critiques & the state of the evidence (surface, don't smooth over)

- **[S11] Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy — *The State of Multicultural Counseling Competencies Research*.**
  https://www.societyforpsychotherapy.org/the-state-of-multicultural-counseling-competencies-research/
  _APA Division 29's practitioner-research outlet. Authority: moderate-high; a candid literature review._
  Measurement problems: self-report MCC instruments (MCI, MAKSS, CCCI-R) correlate with **social
  desirability**, and after controlling for it, self-reported MCC showed **no association** with
  actual multicultural case-conceptualization ability (Constantine & Ladany, 2000). Heavy reliance
  on analogue studies and pseudo-clients; "surprisingly little empirical research" with real
  clients. Client-perceived competence ≠ therapist self-rated competence.

- **[S12] Lilienfeld, S. O. (2017). *Microaggressions: Strong Claims, Inadequate Evidence.* Perspectives on Psychological Science, 12(1), 138–169.**
  https://scottlilienfeld.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/lilienfeld2017-3.pdf
  _Peer-reviewed critique (author-hosted PDF). Authority: high — the standard scientific counterweight._
  The microaggression research program's five premises (clear operationalization; uniformly negative
  interpretation by minority group members; implicitly aggressive motives; valid assessment via
  subjective report alone; adverse mental-health impact) are each under-supported; definitions are
  elastic; proposes "perceived racial slight" as a more neutral term; recommends abandoning the
  "aggression" implication pending better evidence. **Sue's rejoinder conceded many points** while
  defending the lived-experience research tradition. Both sides continue: Williams (2019,
  *Perspect Psychol Sci*) answers Lilienfeld point-by-point.

## Tier: professional bodies & disparities data (context)

- **[S13] Wikipedia — *Cultural humility* (used as a corroborated index to the primary literature, not as an authority).**
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_humility
  _Tertiary; every claim taken from it is anchored to [S4]/[S8]. Authority: orientation only._
  Confirms the Tervalon & Murray-García definition/dimensions and the competence-as-endpoint
  critique; notes 1,500+ citations by 2019.

- **[S14] Simply Psychology — *What Is Acculturation and Why It Happens* + Maricopa open textbook, *Berry's Model of Acculturation*.**
  https://www.simplypsychology.org/acculturation-definition.html ·
  https://open.maricopa.edu/culturepsychology/chapter/berrys-model-of-acculturation/
  _Tertiary teaching summaries (one SEO-tier, one open textbook). Authority: orientation only; used to corroborate the standard reading of [S7], not for load-bearing claims._
  Standard presentation of the two dimensions, four strategies, and the
  integration-best/marginalization-worst adaptation gradient.

- **[S15] NIMHD Research Spotlight — *Examining Why Mental Health Service Use and Dropout Rates Vary Across Racial/Ethnic Groups* (+ PMC: mental-illness stigma in Black, Latina/o, and Asian Americans).**
  https://www.nimhd.nih.gov/news-events/research-spotlight/examining-why-mental-health-service-use-and-dropout-rates-vary ·
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568160/
  _Federal research institute (.gov) + peer-reviewed (PMC). Authority: high._
  Among U.S. adults with mental disorders, racial/ethnic minority clients are roughly **half as
  likely as White clients to receive treatment** and more likely to drop out before completing it;
  stigma runs higher in several minority communities and predicts treatment avoidance; barriers
  include language access, cost, mistrust of providers, and culturally shaped understandings of
  distress and help-seeking. This is the outcome data that makes the unit clinical, not decorative.

- **[S16] American Counseling Association (2014). *ACA Code of Ethics*, Standard C.2.a.**
  (see Unit 3 sources file, [S1] there)
  _Already on file from Unit 3._ Multicultural competency is an **ethical requirement across all
  specialties** — boundaries of competence explicitly include "multicultural competency" — which is
  the formal bridge from Unit 3 to this unit.

## Points of disagreement / uncertainty found (surfaced in readings)

1. **"Competence" vs. "humility" is a live framing debate.** Tervalon & Murray-García's critique —
   competence implies a masterable endpoint — is widely accepted in training programs, yet the
   field's official frameworks (MSJCC) still use "competencies." Most current writers treat them as
   complements (humility is the *stance*, competencies the *learnable content*), but the tension is
   real and worth knowing by name. [S4][S1][S13]
2. **Self-reported multicultural competence is a weak, possibly invalid signal.** It correlates
   with social desirability, and controlling for that erases its association with actual
   case-conceptualization ability. What predicts outcomes is the **client's perception** of the
   counselor's competence/humility — a humbling result that is itself an argument for humility.
   [S11][S9][S8]
3. **The MCC→outcome effect is moderate, not overwhelming** (r ≈ .29 in Tao et al.), and the
   evidence base leans on analogue studies and pseudo-clients. Strong claims in either direction
   outrun the data. [S9][S11]
4. **Microaggression research is genuinely contested.** Sue's taxonomy is standard curriculum AND
   Lilienfeld's critique (elastic definitions, subjective-report-only assessment, unproven causal
   mental-health claims) is published in a top journal — with Sue conceding several points. Learn
   both; a program will expect the taxonomy, a good program will also expect the critique. [S6][S12]
5. **Berry's four boxes are tidier than reality.** Widely taught, but later work criticizes the
   categories as static and context-blind (people integrate at work and separate at home; the
   "choice" of strategy is constrained by the receiving society's openness). Berry himself pairs
   each individual strategy with a society-level condition (multiculturalism, melting pot,
   segregation, exclusion). [S7][S14]
6. **MSJCC's advocacy mandate has boundary questions.** How far "counseling and advocacy
   interventions" should extend (in-office empowerment vs. public-policy activism) is debated within
   the profession; the framework itself brackets advocacy "with, and on behalf of" clients at six
   socioecological levels but leaves scope judgments to the counselor. [S1][S2]
