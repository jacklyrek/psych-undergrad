# Unit 6 sources — Psychopathology & the DSM-5-TR

Evidence base for Unit 6 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-17. Anchored on **peer-reviewed clinical references** (NCBI Bookshelf StatPearls
for every major disorder category; the *World Psychiatry* DSM-5-TR overview by First et al. — the
manual's own editors), triangulated with **epidemiology primaries** (Kessler's NCS-R), the
**dimensional-critique literature** (HiTOP, the p factor, RDoC, Frances), and
**counseling-profession sources** (The Professional Counselor / NBCC on the multiaxial removal and
what diagnosis means for counselors). The **DSM-5-TR itself is the anchor text** (not freely
online); criteria summarized here are taken from peer-reviewed secondary clinical references
(StatPearls), which is the standard teaching practice — quote the manual itself before relying on
any criterion in a real clinical or exam context. The APA's own dsm5.org/psychiatry.org pages block
automated access (403) and are cited only via the First et al. overview [S2]._

## Tier: the manual — structure, history, and what the TR changed

- **[S1] OpenStax Psychology 2e, §15.2 *Diagnosing and Classifying Psychological Disorders* (open textbook).**
  https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/15-2-diagnosing-and-classifying-psychological-disorders
  _Peer-reviewed open textbook. Authority: high for orientation-level DSM history and the standard criticisms._
  DSM-I (1952) had 106 diagnoses in 130 pages, organized on a format the U.S. Army developed in
  WWII; DSM-III (1980) had 265 diagnoses in 886 pages; DSM-5 (2013) contains 237 disorders. Each
  entry gives diagnostic features, diagnostic criteria, prevalence, and risk factors; comorbidity
  rates are documented (e.g., "41% of people with OCD also meet the diagnostic criteria for major
  depressive disorder"). Homosexuality removed as a diagnosis in 1973; the bereavement exclusion
  removed in DSM-5. Worldwide "the ICD is more frequently used for clinical diagnosis, whereas the
  DSM is more valued for research." Standing criticisms: the DSM "overpathologize[s] the human
  condition by turning common human problems into mental illnesses"; "nearly half of all Americans
  will meet the criteria for a DSM disorder at some point in their life." Anti-labeling stance:
  don't say "schizophrenics"/"depressives" — such labels "objectify people"; "a psychological
  disorder is not what a person is; it is something that a person has."

- **[S2] First, M. B., et al. (2022). *DSM-5-TR: overview of what's new and what's changed.* World Psychiatry, 21(2). PMC9077590.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9077590/
  _Peer-reviewed overview by the DSM-5-TR's own editors. Authority: highest available online for what the TR is._
  A text revision updates descriptive text from literature review, but DSM-5-TR went further:
  **prolonged grief disorder** added (intense yearning/preoccupation with the deceased ≥12 months
  after the death, with impairing grief symptoms — see the PFA module's grief pages), plus
  **unspecified mood disorder** and stimulant-induced mild neurocognitive disorder; free-standing
  **symptom codes for suicidal behavior and nonsuicidal self-injury** (recordable without any
  diagnosis); criteria/specifier changes to **70+ disorders**; terminology updates ("experienced
  gender," "individual assigned male/female at birth") and a full-text review by an **Ethnoracial
  Equity and Inclusion Work Group**.

- **[S3] Kress, V. E., et al. (2014). *The Removal of the Multiaxial System in the DSM-5: Implications and Practice Suggestions for Counselors.* The Professional Counselor (NBCC).**
  https://tpcjournal.nbcc.org/the-removal-of-the-multiaxial-system-in-the-dsm-5-implications-and-practice-suggestions-for-counselors/
  _Peer-reviewed counseling journal. Authority: high for the counseling-profession angle._
  DSM-IV's five axes: I clinical disorders; II personality disorders & intellectual disability;
  III relevant medical conditions; IV psychosocial/environmental stressors; V Global Assessment of
  Functioning (GAF, 0–100). DSM-5 dropped all five for **nonaxial documentation** (all disorders
  listed together by clinical priority), **WHODAS 2.0** replacing the GAF, and expanded **V/Z codes**
  for psychosocial context (housing, abuse, relational problems). Counselor implications: the shift
  reinforces a neurobiological/medical model while counselors emphasize "strength-based and
  developmentally, culturally and contextually sensitive approaches" — contextual factors must now
  be documented deliberately, without structural prompts. Catalogs the profession's standing
  critiques: the DSM has historically "marginalized, stigmatized and harmed those who are
  different," lacks cross-cultural validity, encourages reductionism, and reflects political as
  well as scientific processes.

- **[S4] SAMHSA (NCBI Bookshelf). *DSM-IV to DSM-5 Changes: Overview.* NBK519711.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK519711/
  _Government clinical reference. Authority: high for the mechanics of the DSM-IV→5 transition._
  Details the axis system and its removal; the GAF was eliminated for "conceptual lack of clarity
  (i.e., including symptoms, suicide risk, and disabilities in the descriptors) and questionable
  psychometric properties." DSM-5 eliminated the "disorders usually first diagnosed in infancy,
  childhood, or adolescence" chapter, redistributing those disorders through a **lifespan-ordered**
  manual (neurodevelopmental disorders first, neurocognitive disorders last).

## Tier: disorder-category clinical references (criteria-level ground truth)

- **[S5] Bains, N., & Abdijadid, S. (updated 2023). *Major Depressive Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK559078.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559078/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  MDD: **5 of 9 symptoms for ≥2 weeks, at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia**, causing
  functional impairment; the nine: depressed mood, anhedonia, guilt/worthlessness, fatigue, poor
  concentration, appetite/weight change, psychomotor retardation or agitation, sleep disturbance,
  suicidal thoughts. A manic/hypomanic episode must be excluded (that's bipolar). Children may show
  irritable rather than sad mood. Lifetime prevalence ~12% (range 5–17%), roughly **double in women**.
  Untreated episodes last 6–12 months; recurrence ~50% after one episode, 70% after two, 90% after
  three. Differentials include dysthymia, bereavement, adjustment disorder with depressed mood,
  bipolar disorders, substance/medical causes.

- **[S6] Marzani, G., & Neff, A. P. (updated 2023). *Bipolar Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK558998.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558998/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  **Manic episode:** elevated or irritable mood **with increased activity/energy ≥7 days** (or any
  duration if hospitalization required), with 3+ symptoms (4+ if mood only irritable): grandiosity,
  decreased need for sleep, pressured talkativeness, flight of ideas/racing thoughts,
  distractibility, increased goal-directed activity/agitation, high-risk behavior (spending sprees,
  sexual indiscretion). **Hypomania:** same symptom picture but **≥4 days, no marked impairment, no
  hospitalization, no psychosis**. **Bipolar I** = at least one manic episode (depression not
  required); **Bipolar II** = at least one hypomanic episode + at least one major depressive
  episode, never mania; **cyclothymia** = 2+ years of subthreshold hypomanic and depressive
  symptoms. Bipolar-spectrum lifetime prevalence ~2.4%; correct diagnosis typically lags first
  contact by ~6–10 years; **20–30% of "MDD" patients transition to bipolar within 3 years**;
  suicide rate ~20–30× the general population.

- **[S7] Munir, S., & Takov, V. (updated 2022). *Generalized Anxiety Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK441870.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441870/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  GAD: **excessive anxiety and worry, more days than not, ≥6 months**, about multiple domains;
  **difficulty controlling the worry**; **3+ of 6** associated symptoms (restlessness, easy
  fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance); distress/
  impairment; not attributable to a substance or medical cause (rule out hyperthyroidism, caffeine,
  stimulants). Women ~2× men; up to ~20% of adults have an anxiety disorder in a given year;
  course chronic and relapsing.

- **[S8] Cackovic, C., Nazir, S., & Marwaha, R. (updated 2023). *Panic Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK430973.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK430973/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  **Panic attack:** "an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort" peaking within minutes, with
  **4+ of 13** physical/cognitive symptoms (palpitations, sweating, trembling, dyspnea, chest pain,
  nausea, dizziness, chills/heat, paresthesias, derealization/depersonalization, fear of losing
  control or "going crazy," fear of dying). **Panic disorder:** recurrent **unexpected** attacks
  **plus ≥1 month** of worry about further attacks and/or maladaptive behavior change (avoidance).
  Key discrimination: in other anxiety disorders, attacks are cued/expected (e.g., panic at public
  speaking in social anxiety disorder); medical mimics (hyperthyroid, cardiac, vestibular) must be
  excluded.

- **[S9] Mann, S. K., & Marwaha, R. (updated 2023). *Posttraumatic Stress Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK559129.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559129/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  **Criterion A** gate: exposure to "actual or threatened death, injury, or sexual violence" —
  directly experienced, witnessed in person, learned of happening to a close relative/friend, or
  repeated indirect professional exposure (first responders). Then four clusters: **B intrusion
  (1+)** (intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks, cued distress/reactivity); **C avoidance
  (1+)** (of internal reminders and/or external cues); **D negative alterations in cognition/mood
  (2+)** (amnesia for aspects, "I am bad," blame, persistent fear/guilt/shame, detachment,
  diminished interest, no positive emotion); **E arousal/reactivity (2+)** (irritability,
  recklessness, hypervigilance, startle, concentration, sleep). Duration **>1 month** + impairment.
  Specifiers: **dissociative** (depersonalization/derealization) and **delayed expression** (full
  criteria ≥6 months post-trauma). Lifetime prevalence ~6.1–9.2% (US). Differentials: **acute
  stress disorder** (same family, symptoms 3 days–1 month), **adjustment disorder** (stressor of
  any severity, not necessarily Criterion-A trauma; onset within 3 months).

- **[S10] Hany, M., et al. (updated 2024). *Schizophrenia.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK539864.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539864/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  **2+ of 5** symptom domains for a significant portion of one month — delusions, hallucinations,
  disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, negative symptoms (at least one
  must be delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized speech) — with **continuous signs ≥6 months
  including ≥1 month of active-phase symptoms** and functional decline. Positive symptoms
  (hallucinations, delusions, disorganization) respond to antipsychotics better than negative
  symptoms (flat affect, avolition). **Spectrum by duration:** brief psychotic disorder (1 day–<1
  month), schizophreniform (<6 months), schizophrenia (≥6 months); **schizoaffective** = mood
  episodes concurrent with active-phase symptoms (plus ≥2 weeks of psychosis without mood
  symptoms); **delusional disorder** = delusions without the other characteristic symptoms.
  Lifetime risk ~1%; men ~1.7× incidence, earlier onset, more negative symptoms. Complete recovery
  in only ~13.5%; life expectancy ~15 years shorter; suicide the most common cause of premature
  death. Dopamine hypothesis: mesolimbic dysregulation → "misattribution of importance to
  irrelevant objects or events" (aberrant salience).

- **[S11] Fariba, K. A., et al. (updated 2023). *Personality Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK556058.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556058/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  General definition: "an enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates
  significantly from the norms and expectations of the surrounding culture" — pervasive,
  inflexible, onset by adolescence/early adulthood, stable, causing distress or impairment.
  **Cluster A** (odd/eccentric): paranoid, schizoid, schizotypal. **Cluster B**
  (dramatic/emotional/erratic): antisocial, borderline, histrionic, narcissistic. **Cluster C**
  (anxious/fearful): avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive (OCPD). Prevalence ~6.1% (WHO);
  much higher in clinical and incarcerated populations. "With the exception of borderline
  personality disorder, there are no high-quality, evidence-based psychotherapies for personality
  disorders" — DBT is the established borderline treatment. The DSM-5-TR also carries the
  **Alternative Model (AMPD)**: a hybrid dimensional-categorical system rating impairment in
  personality functioning plus pathological traits. Countertransference management is a core
  clinical challenge.

- **[S12] Brock, H., et al. (updated 2024). *Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK553162.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553162/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  **Obsessions:** "recurrent and persistent thoughts, urges, or images that are … intrusive and
  unwanted." **Compulsions:** "repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the person feels driven to
  perform in response to an obsession or according to rules that must be applied rigidly."
  Diagnosis: obsessions and/or compulsions that are **time-consuming (≥1 hr/day)** or cause marked
  distress/impairment. DSM-5 moved OCD out of the anxiety chapter into **Obsessive-Compulsive and
  Related Disorders** (with body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, trichotillomania,
  excoriation disorder) on shared phenomenology/neurobiology. Most patients have insight (only
  2–4% lack it) — symptoms are **ego-dystonic** (recognized as irrational, unwanted). Contrast
  **OCPD**: "a chronic pattern of perfectionism and rigid control" experienced as rational and
  ego-syntonic, with no true obsessions/compulsions.

- **[S13] Moore, C. A., & Bokor, B. R. (updated 2023). *Anorexia Nervosa.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK459148.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK459148/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high (AN); BN/BED facts corroborated in-page._
  AN: **restriction of intake → significantly low body weight** + **intense fear of gaining
  weight** + **distorted body image**; restricting vs. binge-eating/purging subtypes. The weight
  criterion is the line against **bulimia nervosa**, where weight is normal or above. Lifetime
  prevalence 0.3–1%, mostly female, onset late adolescence/early adulthood. Among the deadliest
  psychiatric conditions: elevated all-cause mortality via medical complications and **suicide
  (~25% of AN deaths)**; ~three-fourths of outpatients remit within 5 years.

- **[S14] Magnus, W., et al. (updated 2023). *Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK441838.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441838/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  ADHD: **6+ of 9** inattentive and/or **6+ of 9** hyperactive-impulsive symptoms in children
  (**5+ at 17+**), onset **before age 12**, present in **2+ settings**, impairing. Three
  presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive/impulsive, combined. Adult
  prevalence ~3–6%; roughly half of childhood cases attenuate substantially by adulthood.
  Overlapping inattention/concentration symptoms make ADHD, anxiety, and depression mutual
  misdiagnosis risks.

- **[S15] Mughal, S., et al. (updated 2022). *Autism Spectrum Disorder.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK525976.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK525976/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  ASD: persistent deficits in **all three** social-communication areas (social-emotional
  reciprocity; nonverbal communication; developing/maintaining relationships) **plus 2+ of 4**
  restricted/repetitive behavior types (stereotyped movements/speech; insistence on
  sameness/routines; highly restricted fixated interests; sensory hyper-/hyporeactivity), with
  onset in the early developmental period. DSM-5 merged autistic disorder, Asperger syndrome, and
  PDD-NOS into the single spectrum. CDC 2020: ~**1 in 36** 8-year-olds; 74% have ≥1 comorbidity.

- **[S16] DSM-5-TR Substance Use Disorder criteria, via SAMHSA / NCBI Bookshelf NBK565474 (table 3).**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK565474/table/table-3/
  _Diagnostic authority (same source anchors the aux-addiction module's [S5]). Authority: high._
  The **11 SUD criteria in four groups** — impaired control, social impairment, risky use,
  pharmacological (tolerance/withdrawal) — with severity **mild (2–3), moderate (4–5), severe
  (6+)**; one SUD per substance class; tolerance/withdrawal under prescribed medical care don't
  count toward a diagnosis. Full treatment in the elective module (`aux-addiction`).

## Tier: epidemiology, comorbidity & the dimensional debate

- **[S17] Kessler, R. C., et al. (2005). *Prevalence, Severity, and Comorbidity of 12-Month DSM-IV Disorders in the National Comorbidity Survey Replication.* Arch Gen Psychiatry, 62(6). PMC2847357.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2847357/
  _The canonical US psychiatric-epidemiology survey. Authority: high (DSM-IV-era numbers)._
  12-month prevalence: **any disorder 26.2%** — anxiety 18.1%, mood 9.5%, impulse-control 8.9%,
  substance 3.8%. Of 12-month cases, **55% carry one diagnosis, 22% two, 23% three or more** —
  comorbidity is routine, not exotic. Severity: 22.3% serious, 37.3% moderate, 40.4% mild — and
  seriousness concentrates in the highly comorbid few (classes covering ~7% of the sample hold
  43.6% of serious cases). (Companion lifetime paper: ~46% lifetime prevalence — the "nearly half"
  figure in [S1].)

- **[S18] Ruggero, C. J., Kotov, R., et al. (2019). *Integrating the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) into Clinical Practice.* J Consult Clin Psychol. PMC6859953.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6859953/
  _Peer-reviewed statement by the HiTOP consortium. Authority: high for the dimensional critique/model._
  Four problems with categorical diagnosis: **reliability** ("~40% of diagnoses examined in the
  DSM-5 field trials showed poor inter-rater agreement"); **comorbidity** (excessive co-occurrence
  questions the categories' distinctness); **heterogeneity** ("individuals with the same diagnosis
  can have distinct, even non-overlapping sets of symptoms"); **continuity** ("most psychopathology
  falls along a continuum with normality"). HiTOP: a six-level hierarchy — general (p) factor →
  six spectra (internalizing, antagonistic externalizing, disinhibited externalizing, thought
  disorder, detachment, somatoform) → subfactors → syndromes → symptom components/traits.
  Honest limit: "no direct evidence yet that implementation of HiTOP diagnoses in clinical
  settings will improve treatment outcomes."

- **[S19] Caspi, A., & Moffitt, T. E. (2014). *The p Factor: One General Psychopathology Factor in the Structure of Psychiatric Disorders?* Clin Psychol Sci, 2(2). PMC4209412.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4209412/
  _Primary paper, Dunedin cohort (n≈1,000, ages 18–38). Authority: high._
  Psychiatric disorders' co-occurrence fits a hierarchical model: three spectra (internalizing,
  externalizing, thought disorder) under one general **p factor**, analogous to *g* in
  intelligence. Higher p → "more life impairment, greater familiality, worse developmental
  histories, and more compromised early-life brain function." The p factor "explains why it is
  challenging to find causes, consequences, biomarkers, and treatments with specificity to
  individual mental disorders."

- **[S20] NIMH. *About RDoC* (Research Domain Criteria).**
  https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/about-rdoc
  _The funder's own framework page. Authority: high for what RDoC is and isn't._
  RDoC = "a set of research principles for investigating mental disorders" organized around six
  functional domains studied from genes to behavior across the normal-to-abnormal range — built
  because symptom-defined categories are heterogeneous and comorbid. Explicit limit: "RDoC is not
  meant to serve as a diagnostic guide, nor is it intended to replace current diagnostic systems."

- **[S21] Frances, A. (2013). *The past, present and future of psychiatric diagnosis.* World Psychiatry, 12(2). PMC3683254.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3683254/
  _Commentary by the chair of the DSM-IV task force — the loudest credentialed critic of DSM-5. Authority: high as a position piece, not as settled fact._
  "Diagnostic inflation": ADHD diagnoses tripled in twenty years, childhood bipolar up forty-fold,
  autism up twenty-fold; a prospective cohort (Great Smoky Mountains Study) found a cumulative 83%
  rate of mental disorder by age 21. Drivers include drug-company marketing that "sell[s]
  psychiatric diagnoses by convincing potential patients and prescribers that expectable life
  problems were really mental disorders." Diagnosis should "ensure reliable and accurate diagnosis
  and effective, safe, and necessary treatment" — not sweep in people likely to recover on their
  own.

- **[S22] Pies, R. W. (2014). *The Bereavement Exclusion and DSM-5: An Update and Commentary.* Innov Clin Neurosci, 11(7–8). PMC4204469.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4204469/
  _Peer-reviewed commentary by a participant in the debate (pro-removal side; counter-arguments summarized in-text). Authority: high for the shape of the debate._
  DSM-IV barred an MDD diagnosis in the first two months after bereavement absent override features
  (psychosis, suicidality). DSM-5 removed the exclusion because bereavement-triggered depression
  doesn't demonstrably "differ in nature, course, or outcome from depression of equal severity in
  any other context," and because depression is "a potentially lethal disorder" — while critics
  warned the change medicalizes ordinary grief. Clinically usable qualitative differences: grief
  preserves self-esteem, comes in waves that include positive memories, and is **consolable**;
  major depression trends toward pervasive misery, worthlessness/self-loathing, and
  inconsolability.

- **[S23] SAMHSA (NCBI Bookshelf). *Cultural Formulation in Diagnosis and Cultural Concepts of Distress.* In: Improving Cultural Competence (TIP 59), NBK248426.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK248426/
  _Government treatment-improvement protocol. Authority: high._
  DSM-5 retired "culture-bound syndrome" for three concepts: **cultural syndromes** (recognized
  symptom clusters, e.g., *ataque de nervios*, *susto*), **cultural idioms of distress** (culturally
  shared ways of expressing distress), and **cultural explanations** (causal attributions, e.g.,
  *susto* as soul loss after a fright). The Outline for Cultural Formulation covers five domains
  (identity; explanations of illness; psychosocial environment; the client–clinician relationship;
  overall assessment); the 16-question **Cultural Formulation Interview** operationalizes it.
  "Behavior that is aberrant in one culture can be standard in another" — e.g., apparent paranoia
  can be normative in migrants from oppressive contexts.

## Points of disagreement / uncertainty found (surfaced in readings)

1. **The categories themselves are the contested object.** DSM-5 field trials showed poor
   inter-rater agreement for a large share of tested diagnoses; comorbidity is the norm (45% of
   12-month cases carry 2+ diagnoses); same-diagnosis patients can share almost no symptoms; and
   no common disorder has been shown to be a true natural category. HiTOP, the p factor, and RDoC
   are serious empirical alternatives — but none is ready to replace the DSM in the clinic, and
   HiTOP's own authors concede there's no outcome evidence yet for using it clinically.
   [S17][S18][S19][S20]
2. **Diagnostic inflation is a real fight, not a fringe complaint.** Frances (who chaired DSM-IV)
   argues the boundaries of disorder have expanded into normal life, pushed partly by
   pharmaceutical marketing; defenders answer that lowered thresholds catch treatable suffering
   earlier. Both dynamics are documented. Hold diagnosis as a tool with false-positive *and*
   false-negative costs. [S21][S1]
3. **Grief vs. depression is the emblem case.** Removing the bereavement exclusion (DSM-5) and
   adding prolonged grief disorder (DSM-5-TR) are defensible on the evidence *and* legitimately
   feared as pathologizing love and loss — the PFA module's grief pages carry the same tension.
   The qualitative markers (self-esteem preserved, waves, consolability) are the working
   discrimination. [S22][S2]
4. **Criteria summarized secondhand.** The DSM-5-TR text itself is paywalled; criteria here come
   from peer-reviewed secondary references (StatPearls et al.). Counts and durations were
   cross-checked across sources, but **quote the manual before relying on a criterion clinically**.
   Prevalence figures vary by survey and era (NCS-R is DSM-IV-based); treat them as
   order-of-magnitude. [S5–S17]
5. **Culture cuts through everything.** The same behavior can be normative or symptomatic
   depending on context; the DSM's own cultural tools (CFI, cultural concepts of distress) exist
   because unexamined criteria misdiagnose — this is Unit 4's tripartite/humility material applied
   to diagnosis. [S23][S3]
