# Unit 12 sources — Research Methods & Program Evaluation

Evidence base for Unit 12 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-20. Tier 3 "rounding out" unit — the **final** unit of the 12-unit spine.
CACREP core area **Research & Program Evaluation** (§2.F.8). The syllabus objective is deliberately
practical: **read and critically evaluate counseling research**, understand **outcome measurement**,
and know **research ethics**. Per the course-map, the unit **quizzes choosing the method, not
executing it** — no hand-calculation of statistics; the skill is telling designs apart, reading an
effect size, ranking evidence, and spotting a threat to validity. Six substantive areas:
**experimental vs. correlational (vs. quasi-experimental) designs** and the requirements for a
causal claim; **threats to validity** (Cook & Campbell's four validities); **effect size vs.
statistical vs. clinical significance** (and what a p-value does and does not mean); **evidence-based
practice** (APA's three-legged definition, the evidence hierarchy, empirically-supported-treatment
tiers, and the **replication crisis** / allegiance & publication bias); **single-case designs and
progress monitoring**; **program evaluation** (formative/summative, process/outcome, needs
assessment); and **research ethics / the IRB** (the Belmont Report, the historical abuses behind it,
and ACA Section G). Anchored on **open psychology-methods textbooks** (Price et al., *Research
Methods in Psychology*), **primary/canonical methodology** (Cook & Campbell; Chambless & Hollon;
Jacobson & Truax; the Open Science Collaboration), **government/regulatory primaries** (the Belmont
Report, the Common Rule), and the **profession's own documents** (APA 2006 EBP policy; the ACA Code
of Ethics; CACREP 2016). Contested threads carried at evaluate level: the **replication crisis** and
questionable research practices; **allegiance/publication bias** (already met in Units 9–10); the
gap between a **group average and the individual client** (nomothetic vs. idiographic); and the
limits of the **evidence hierarchy** itself._

The unit's stance thread — the Unit-wide "resist the urge to fix" caution, translated for research —
is **"the number is not the person": evidence informs clinical judgment, it does not replace it**,
and being a discerning consumer means resisting both credulity and cynicism.

## Tier: research designs and causation

- **[S1] Price, P. C., Jhangiani, R., Chiang, I.-C. A., Leighton, D. C., & Cuttler, C. *Research Methods in Psychology* (open textbook; multiple editions — OpenTextBC / Saylor / WSU Pressbooks mirrors).**
  https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_research-methods-in-psychology/ · https://opentextbc.ca/researchmethods/
  _Widely adopted, peer-reviewed **open** psychology-methods textbook; the anchor for designs, single-subject research, and validity. Authority: high (educational primary)._
  Experiments are the design that supports **causal** claims; correlational studies show
  **association**, not causation. The **directionality problem** (which variable caused which?) and
  the **third-variable problem** (a lurking common cause) are why "correlation ≠ causation."
  **Random assignment** (equating groups at the outset — an internal-validity move) is distinct from
  **random sampling/selection** (drawing a representative sample — an external-validity move).

- **[S2] "What's the difference between correlational and experimental research?" Scribbr; David Schuster, *Research Methods Course Pack*, Ch. 4 "Causality" (San José State).**
  https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/correlational-vs-experimental-research/ · https://www.davidschuster.info/books/methods/causality.html
  _Orientation-tier teaching resources; used for the three requirements of a causal inference. Cross-checked against S1. Authority: moderate (corroborated)._
  A causal claim requires three things: (1) **covariation** (the variables are related), (2)
  **temporal precedence** (the cause precedes the effect), and (3) **no plausible alternative
  explanation** (confounds ruled out — the job random assignment does).

- **[S3] "Quasi-experimental designs." EBSCO Research Starters (Health and Medicine).**
  https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/health-and-medicine/quasi-experimental-designs
  _Encyclopedic research-reference entry; used for the quasi-experimental definition. Authority: moderate._
  A **quasi-experiment** has a manipulation (an intervention/treatment) **but no random
  assignment** — participants fall into groups by a pre-existing characteristic or by convenience.
  It sits **between** correlational studies and true experiments on internal validity: better than
  correlational (there is an intervention) but weaker than an RCT (selection confounds are not
  controlled by randomization). Common in real-world/field settings where randomizing is impossible
  or unethical.

## Tier: threats to validity (the Campbellian system)

- **[S4] Cook, T. D., & Campbell, D. T. (1979). *Quasi-Experimentation: Design & Analysis Issues for Field Settings*; extended in Shadish, W. R., Cook, T. D., & Campbell, D. T. (2002). *Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Generalized Causal Inference.***
  _The **canonical** taxonomy of the four validities and their threats. Books, offline — the framework is corroborated via the peer-reviewed and educational secondaries below. Authority: very high (foundational)._
  Four kinds of validity, each with characteristic threats: **statistical-conclusion validity** (is
  there a real covariation, analyzed correctly?), **internal validity** (did the treatment, not
  something else, cause the change?), **construct validity** (do the measures/operations match the
  intended constructs?), and **external validity** (do the findings generalize to other people,
  settings, times?). Classic **internal-validity threats**: **history, maturation, testing,
  instrumentation, statistical regression (regression to the mean), selection, attrition/mortality**,
  and their interactions.

- **[S5] García-Pérez, M. A. (2012). *Statistical Conclusion Validity: Some Common Threats and Simple Remedies.* Frontiers in Psychology, 3, 325 (PMC3429930).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3429930/
  _Peer-reviewed methods review. Authority: high (statistical-conclusion validity + the four-validity frame)._
  Statistical-conclusion validity concerns whether the data can reasonably be regarded as revealing
  a link (or its absence) between variables **on statistical grounds** — threats include low power,
  violated assumptions, unreliable measures, and fishing/error-rate inflation from multiple tests.

- **[S6] Trochim, *Research Methods Knowledge Base* (conjointly.com); "Threats to Validity of Research Design," Portland State (B. Stipak); "Threats to Internal / Statistical Conclusion Validity" (C. Rakes teaching slides).**
  https://web.pdx.edu/~stipakb/download/PA555/ResearchDesign.html
  _Widely cited educational treatments of the Campbellian threats; used to enumerate/define the internal-validity threats and the **internal↔external validity tradeoff**. Cross-checked against S4/S5. Authority: moderate (corroborated)._
  The **tradeoff**: tightly controlled lab experiments maximize internal validity but can sacrifice
  external validity (artificial setting); naturalistic field studies maximize external validity but
  admit confounds. No single study optimizes both — which is one reason **replication across
  contexts** matters.

## Tier: effect size, significance, and clinical significance

- **[S7] Cohen, J. (1988). *Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences* (2nd ed.); operationalized in Sullivan, G. M., & Feinn, R. (2012). *Using Effect Size—or Why the P Value Is Not Enough.* J. Grad. Med. Educ., 4(3), 279–282.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3444174/
  _Cohen is the canonical source for effect-size conventions; the JGME paper is the standard peer-reviewed "why effect size" teaching reference. Authority: high._
  **Effect size** = the **magnitude** of a difference or relationship, independent of sample size.
  **Cohen's *d*** (standardized mean difference) rule-of-thumb: **≈0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large**
  (a *d* below ~0.2 is trivial); **Pearson *r*** ≈ 0.1/0.3/0.5. The p-value depends on both effect
  size **and** sample size, so a large study can make a trivial effect "significant"; effect size
  answers the question a p-value cannot — **how big?**

- **[S8] Nahm, F. S. (2017). *What the P values really tell us.* / "Alternatives to P value: confidence interval and effect size" (PMC5133225); Andrade, C. (2019). *The P value and statistical significance: misunderstandings, explanations, challenges…* (PubMed 29337724).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5133225/ · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29337724/
  _Peer-reviewed statistics-education articles. Authority: high (p-value definition + its misinterpretations, CIs)._
  A **p-value** is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the observed one
  **if the null hypothesis were true**. It is **not** the probability the null (or the hypothesis) is
  true, **not** the probability the result is due to chance, and **not** a measure of effect size or
  importance. p < .05 is a conventional (arbitrary) threshold, not a law of nature. A **95%
  confidence interval** conveys the **precision** of the estimate (and if it excludes the null value,
  the result is "significant" at .05) — more informative than a bare p-value because it shows the
  plausible range and the magnitude.

- **[S9] Wasserstein, R. L., & Lazar, N. A. (2016). *The ASA Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose.* The American Statistician, 70(2), 129–133.**
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108
  _The American Statistical Association's formal consensus statement — the authoritative word on p-value (mis)use. Authority: very high._
  Six principles, in spirit: p-values can indicate incompatibility with a null model, but they **do
  not measure** the probability that the hypothesis is true or the size/importance of an effect;
  scientific conclusions should **not** be based only on whether p crosses a threshold; **proper
  inference requires full reporting and transparency**; and "statistical significance ≠ scientific,
  human, or economic significance."

- **[S10] Jacobson, N. S., & Truax, P. (1991). *Clinical significance: a statistical approach to defining meaningful change in psychotherapy research.* J. Consult. Clin. Psychol., 59(1), 12–19 (PubMed 1619094); overview at PSYCTC.org (RCSC).**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1619094/ · https://www.psyctc.org/psyctc/root/stats/rcsc/
  _The canonical operationalization of **clinical** (vs. statistical) significance in psychotherapy; the Reliable Change Index. Authority: high (heavily cited — the bridge to Unit 7 outcome monitoring)._
  **Clinically significant change** = movement from a score typical of the **dysfunctional**
  population to one typical of the **functional** population. The **Reliable Change Index (RCI)** =
  the pre-to-post change divided by the standard error of the difference — it asks whether an
  individual's change exceeds what measurement error alone would produce. Combining the two classifies
  clients as **recovered / improved / unchanged / deteriorated**. This is the individual-client meaning
  of "did it work," and the statistical backbone of measurement-based care (Unit 7).

## Tier: evidence-based practice, the evidence hierarchy, and empirically supported treatments

- **[S11] APA Presidential Task Force on Evidence-Based Practice (2006). *Evidence-based practice in psychology.* American Psychologist, 61(4), 271–285.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4832531/ (three-legged-stool corroboration) · https://www.apa.org/practice/resources/evidence
  _The profession's official EBP policy (primary); corroborated via peer-reviewed secondaries. Authority: very high._
  EBPP = **"the integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of
  patient characteristics, culture, and preferences"** (p. 273). Three co-equal legs — **best
  available research + clinical expertise + client characteristics/values** — that must be
  **integrated**; research evidence is one leg, not the master leg. (This is the same definition Unit
  9's `unit09-modalities` hub uses: "evidence-based" is not a horse race between brand-name therapies.)

- **[S12] Chambless, D. L., & Hollon, S. D. (1998). *Defining empirically supported therapies.* J. Consult. Clin. Psychol., 66(1), 7–18 (Div. 12 PDF).**
  https://www.div12.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Chambless-Hollon-1998-Defining-Empirically-Supported-Therapies.pdf
  _The standard criteria behind APA Division 12's list of empirically supported treatments. Authority: high._
  **Well-established:** efficacy shown in **≥2 good between-group design experiments by independent
  research teams** (or a large series of well-controlled single-case designs), using treatment
  manuals and specified samples. **Probably efficacious:** the lower tier — supported by fewer or
  non-independent studies. The load-bearing word is **efficacy**: it must be shown, ideally versus a
  placebo/rival treatment and by more than one lab (guarding against **allegiance** effects).

- **[S13] Evidence-based-practice library guides: UNM Health Sciences "Evidence Pyramid"; National University of Singapore "Hierarchy of Evidence & Study Types"; UC Davis "Levels of Evidence."**
  https://www.unm.edu/~unmvclib/cascade/handouts/PICOTpyramidofevidence.pdf · https://libguides.nus.edu.sg/sysreviews/evidence
  _Standard academic-library treatments of the evidence hierarchy. Authority: moderate–high (consensus teaching material)._
  The **evidence pyramid**, strongest at the top: **systematic reviews & meta-analyses** > **RCTs** >
  **cohort studies** > **case-control studies** > **cross-sectional/case series** > **case
  reports/expert opinion** > animal/lab studies. Higher = less susceptible to bias, but **rarer**.
  Caveats carried in the reading: a **bad** systematic review is not better than a **good** RCT (the
  pyramid ranks **design**, not execution); "GRADE"-style appraisal judges quality within a level.

- **[S14] Open Science Collaboration (2015). *Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science.* Science, 349(6251), aac4716; Simmons, Nelson, & Simonsohn (2011). *False-Positive Psychology.* Psychological Science, 22(11).**
  https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aac4716 · https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797611417632
  _The landmark reproducibility study + the paper that named researcher "degrees of freedom." Authority: very high._
  The OSC replicated **100** psychology studies; while ~97% of originals were "significant," only
  **~36%** of replications reached significance and effect sizes **halved** on average — the
  empirical core of the **replication crisis**. Simmons et al. showed that ordinary **researcher
  degrees of freedom** (flexible stopping rules, optional covariates, selective reporting) —
  **p-hacking** — can push the false-positive rate far above 5%. Fixes: **preregistration**,
  registered reports, larger samples, open data, and valuing replication and null results.
  **Publication bias** (the file-drawer problem) and **allegiance bias** (the developer's own trials
  report the biggest effects — met in Units 9–10) both inflate the apparent evidence base.

## Tier: single-case designs and progress monitoring

- **[S15] Price et al., *Research Methods in Psychology*, "Single-Subject Research Designs" (S1 anchor text, single-subject chapter); Kazdin, A. E. (2011). *Single-Case Research Designs* (2nd ed.).**
  https://saylordotorg.github.io/text_research-methods-in-psychology/s14-02-single-subject-research-design.html
  _Open-text chapter (fetched in full) + the canonical single-case methods reference (Kazdin, offline → corroborated). Authority: high._
  **Single-subject / single-case experimental designs** track one participant (or a few) with
  **repeated measurement** across phases. **Baseline (A)** = repeated measurement with no treatment;
  **treatment (B)** = the intervention. The **reversal / ABAB (withdrawal)** design introduces,
  removes, and re-introduces treatment: if the behavior tracks the phase changes, the treatment —
  not history/maturation — is the likely cause (the strongest single-case demonstration). The
  **multiple-baseline** design staggers treatment onset **across behaviors, settings, or people**,
  demonstrating causation **without** withdrawing an effective treatment (the ethical fix when
  reversal is inappropriate). Analysis is by **visual inspection** of **level, trend, and latency**,
  not (primarily) inferential statistics. Distinct from a **case study** (which is descriptive, no
  manipulation, no baseline logic). The clinical cousin — an **AB** design without the controls — is
  **progress monitoring**: good enough to guide **this client's** care, not to prove causation.

- **[S16] Fortney, J. C., et al. (2017). *A Tipping Point for Measurement-Based Care.* Psychiatric Services, 68(2), 179–188; Lambert's outcome-monitoring / feedback-informed treatment literature.**
  https://ps.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.201500439
  _Peer-reviewed; the measurement-based-care (MBC) evidence base. Authority: high (the Unit 7 ↔ Unit 12 bridge)._
  **Measurement-based care** = routine, systematic symptom measurement (e.g., PHQ-9/GAD-7 —
  Unit 7's `concept-screening-tools`) used to **inform and adjust** treatment. It is the
  practice-level, single-client application of outcome measurement and the reliable-change idea
  [S10]; feedback-informed treatment (routinely showing clinicians client progress, flagging
  "not-on-track" cases) improves outcomes, especially for clients who are deteriorating.

## Tier: program evaluation

- **[S17] "Types of Evaluation" (Centre for Innovation in Campus Mental Health toolkit; Penn State CECD engagement toolkit); Astramovich & Coker (2007), *Program Evaluation: The Accountability Bridge Model for Counselors,* J. Counseling & Development; national survey of counselors' program-evaluation use (CORE, 2019).**
  https://campusmentalhealth.ca/toolkits/evaluation/what-is-evaluation/types/ · https://aese.psu.edu/research/centers/cecd/engagement-toolbox/evaluating-engagement-efforts/evaluation-types
  _Practitioner-consensus toolkits + a counseling-specific peer-reviewed model. Authority: moderate–high._
  **Program evaluation** = judging the merit/worth of a **program or service** (vs. research, which
  aims for **generalizable** knowledge). Types: **needs assessment** (what's needed, before you
  build), **formative** (improve during development/early implementation), **process/implementation**
  (was it delivered as intended, to the intended people?), **summative** (final judgment of
  worth/outcomes), and **outcome/impact** (did the targeted conditions actually change?). Mnemonic
  contrast: **formative = steering the ship mid-voyage; summative = judging the whole trip on
  arrival.** Counselors most often do **single-case** and **outcome** evaluation.

## Tier: research ethics and the IRB

- **[S18] *The Belmont Report* (1979). National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research; U.S. DHHS Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP).**
  https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/index.html
  _The **primary** U.S. framework for human-subjects research ethics. Authority: very high._
  Three principles and their applications: **Respect for persons** → treat people as autonomous
  agents and protect those with diminished autonomy → **informed consent**. **Beneficence** →
  maximize benefits and minimize harms → **risk/benefit assessment**. **Justice** → distribute the
  burdens and benefits of research fairly → **fair subject selection** (don't exploit the vulnerable
  or convenient). IRBs enforce these.

- **[S19] The Nuremberg Code (1947); the World Medical Association *Declaration of Helsinki* (1964, rev.); the U.S. Public Health Service **Tuskegee** Syphilis Study (1932–1972); the **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46).**
  https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/regulations/45-cfr-46/index.html · https://nij.ojp.gov/funding/common-rule
  _Regulatory/government primaries + well-documented history (OHRP, CDC, NIJ). Authority: very high._
  The abuses that produced the rules: **Nazi experiments → the Nuremberg Code** (voluntary consent is
  "absolutely essential"); the **Declaration of Helsinki** (physician-research ethics); the
  **Tuskegee** study — Black men with syphilis were deceived and **denied available penicillin** for
  decades — the U.S. scandal that directly produced the **National Research Act (1974)**, the Belmont
  Report, and the **IRB/Common Rule** system requiring prospective review, informed consent, and
  protections for vulnerable populations.

- **[S20] American Counseling Association (2014). *ACA Code of Ethics,* **Section G: Research and Publication.***
  https://www.counseling.org/resources/aca-code-of-ethics.pdf
  _The counseling profession's own research-ethics standards (primary; the same document anchoring Unit 3). Authority: very high._
  Section G governs research: **informed consent to research** (voluntary; the right to decline; the
  purpose/procedures/risks disclosed), **protecting confidentiality** of research data, **avoiding
  harm**, honest **reporting of results** (no fabrication; report unfavorable results; correct
  errors), giving proper **publication credit**, and a boundary flag: participants who are also
  **students/supervisees/clients** must be free to decline without penalty to that relationship
  (ties to Unit 3 `concept-dual-relationships`).

## Tier: the CACREP standard

- **[S21] Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs (CACREP). *2016 Standards,* §2.F.8 "Research and Program Evaluation."**
  https://www.cacrep.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2016-CACREP-Standards-with-Glossary.pdf
  _The accreditation body's curriculum standard. Authority: high._
  §2.F.8 requires counselor training in: the **importance of research** and how to **critique
  research to inform practice**; **identification of evidence-based counseling practices**; **needs
  assessments**; **development of outcome measures** for counseling programs; **evaluation of
  counseling interventions and programs**; **qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods**;
  **research designs**; **statistical methods**; **analysis and use of data**; and **ethical and
  culturally relevant strategies** for conducting/interpreting/reporting research and program
  evaluation. The area's framing is explicitly **consumer + evaluator**, not "become a
  statistician."
