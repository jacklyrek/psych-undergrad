# Sources — Elective module: Understanding & Changing Addictive Behavior

Evidence base for [`../wiki/aux-addiction.md`](../wiki/aux-addiction.md) and its concept pages. Each
entry has a one-line authority note. This is an **elective/ad-hoc module** (off the 12-unit syllabus
spine; `aux-` namespace); it borders **Unit 6 (Psychopathology / substance use disorders)** and
**Unit 9 (Evidence-Based Modalities)**. Clinically loaded topic — claims are kept precise, tied to
federal health agencies (NIDA, SAMHSA, HHS), diagnostic authority (DSM-5-TR), and peer-reviewed /
Cochrane work, not recovery-industry marketing. "What works" in addiction is partly contested and
the *disease-vs-choice* framing is an active debate — both are flagged rather than smoothed over.

---

## What addiction is: definition, diagnosis, models

- **[S1] National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA). _Drugs, Brains, and Behavior: The Science of
  Addiction_ — "Drug Misuse and Addiction."**
  <https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/drug-misuse-addiction>
  — *The U.S. federal research agency's flagship public-science text. Source for the standard
  definition — addiction as a **"chronic, relapsing disorder characterized by compulsive drug
  seeking and use despite adverse consequences,"** a **brain disorder** because of functional changes
  to reward/stress/self-control circuits — and for **"no single factor determines whether a person
  will become addicted."***

- **[S2] NIDA. _Drugs, Brains, and Behavior_ — "Treatment and Recovery."**
  <https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/treatment-recovery>
  — *Source for the **40–60% relapse rate**, its comparison to relapse in other chronic diseases
  (diabetes 30–50%, hypertension & asthma 50–70%), the framing that **relapse signals treatment
  needs resuming/adjusting, not failure**, and the principle that addiction is treatable but not
  simply "curable."*

- **[S3] NIDA. _Understanding Drug Use and Addiction_ (DrugFacts).**
  <https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugfacts/understanding-drug-use-addiction>
  — *Concise `.gov` reference: drugs flood the reward circuit with **dopamine**; the brain adapts
  (tolerance), and cue-driven wanting persists; risk is multi-factorial (genes ~40–60% of risk,
  environment, development).*

- **[S5] DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) — Substance Use Disorders, via SAMHSA/NCBI
  Bookshelf (TIP / _Substance Use Screening, Risk Assessment, and Use Disorder Diagnosis in Adults_,
  Table 3).**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK565474/table/table-3/>
  — *Diagnostic authority. The **11 SUD criteria in four groups** — impaired control (1–4), social
  impairment (5–7), risky use (8–9), pharmacological (10–11: tolerance, withdrawal) — and the
  severity thresholds: **mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe 6+.** Also the rule that **tolerance and
  withdrawal don't count toward diagnosis when the drug is taken as prescribed under medical
  supervision.***

- **[S16] American Psychiatric Association — "What Is a Substance Use Disorder?" and "Internet
  Gaming."**
  <https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/addiction-substance-use-disorders/what-is-a-substance-use-disorder>
  · <https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/internet-gaming>
  — *Patient-facing but from the DSM's publisher. Confirms SUD as a treatable brain-affecting
  disorder and that **Internet Gaming Disorder sits in DSM-5 Section III ("conditions for further
  study"), not as a formal diagnosis.***

- **[S12] Yau YHC & Potenza MN (2015). "Gambling Disorder and Other Behavioral Addictions:
  Recognition and Treatment." _Harvard Review of Psychiatry_ (PMC4458066).**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4458066/>
  — *Peer-reviewed. **Gambling disorder is the only behavioral (non-substance) addiction recognized
  in DSM-5-TR**, moved beside substance disorders on the basis of shared symptoms, neurobiology
  (reward circuitry, "near-miss" effects), and treatment response; internet gaming remains
  provisional.*

## The neurobiology

- **[S4] Koob GF & Volkow ND (2016). "Neurobiology of addiction: a neurocircuitry analysis."
  _The Lancet Psychiatry_ 3(8):760–773.** (Framework also summarized in the open review "The
  neurobiology of addiction," PMC6767400: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6767400/>)
  — *The canonical **three-stage addiction cycle**: (1) **binge/intoxication** — reward &
  incentive-salience circuitry in the **basal ganglia**; (2) **withdrawal/negative affect** — loss of
  reward + recruited stress systems in the **extended amygdala**; (3) **preoccupation/anticipation**
  — impaired executive control & craving in the **prefrontal cortex.** Also the shift from
  **positive reinforcement** (early, chasing the high) to **negative reinforcement** (later,
  escaping the low).*

## How change happens: readiness, motivation, relapse

- **[S6] Prochaska JO & DiClemente CC — the Transtheoretical Model / Stages of Change.**
  Overview: Boston University School of Public Health, _Behavioral Change Models_
  <https://sphweb.bumc.bu.edu/otlt/mph-modules/sb/behavioralchangetheories/behavioralchangetheories6.html>
  · corroborated by Simply Psychology <https://www.simplypsychology.org/transtheoretical-model.html>
  — *Foundational stage model: **precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action →
  maintenance** (with **relapse/recycling** as an expected event). Change is **spiral, not linear**;
  matching the intervention to the person's stage is the point. Decisional balance shifts as the
  person moves.*

- **[S7] Larimer ME, Palmer RS & Marlatt GA (1999). "Relapse Prevention: An Overview of Marlatt's
  Cognitive-Behavioral Model." _Alcohol Research & Health_ (NIAAA), PMC6760427.**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6760427/>
  — *Peer-reviewed (federal alcohol institute) exposition of **Relapse Prevention (RP)**: immediate
  determinants (**high-risk situations**, coping skills, **outcome expectancies**, the **abstinence
  violation effect**) and covert antecedents (lifestyle, urges/cravings). Coping success raises
  **self-efficacy** and cuts relapse risk. Distinguishes a **lapse** (a single slip) from a
  **relapse** (return to prior pattern), and the **AVE** — the guilt/"I've blown it" spiral that
  turns one into the other.*

- **[S8] SAMHSA — TIP 35: _Enhancing Motivation for Change in Substance Use Disorder Treatment_
  (Ch. 3, Motivational Interviewing), NCBI Bookshelf NBK571068.**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571068/>
  — *Federal treatment-improvement protocol built on Miller & Rollnick's **Motivational Interviewing
  (MI)**. Source for **ambivalence** as the target, evoking **change talk** vs. reinforcing **sustain
  talk**, **"rolling with resistance,"** resisting the **righting reflex** (the urge to fix/argue),
  and the **RULE** heuristic (Resist the righting reflex, Understand motivations, Listen, Empower).*

## What treatment actually is (the evidence)

- **[S9] NIDA — "Medications for Opioid Use Disorder" (research topic) + SAMHSA MOUD guidance.**
  <https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/medications-opioid-use-disorder>
  — *Three FDA-approved medications for OUD — **methadone** (full agonist), **buprenorphine** (partial
  agonist), **naltrexone** (antagonist, ER injection). **Methadone and buprenorphine — not naltrexone
  — are proven to reduce overdose and all-cause mortality** (roughly halving fatal-overdose risk).
  MOUD stabilizes brain chemistry, cuts cravings, and enables engagement in the rest of treatment.
  Withholding it in favor of "medication-free" recovery is not evidence-based for OUD.*

- **[S10] Kelly JF, Humphreys K & Ferri M (2020). Cochrane systematic review — "Alcoholics Anonymous
  and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder"; clinician distillation in _Alcohol and
  Alcoholism_ (PMC8060988).**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8060988/> · review:
  <https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012880.pub2/full>
  — *Gold-standard review: **manualized AA/Twelve-Step Facilitation is more effective than other
  established treatments (e.g., CBT) for continuous abstinence** and at least as good on other
  outcomes, at **substantially lower cost** — largely by linking people to a free, durable recovery
  network. A meaningful reversal of the long-standing "no evidence for AA" claim.*

- **[S11] Contingency Management (CM) for stimulant use disorder — Society for the Advancement of
  Psychotherapy; Khazanov GK et al. (2024), _Addiction_.**
  <https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/contingency-management-for-stimulant-addiction-the-most-effective-evidence-based-treatment-youve-never-used/>
  · <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/add.16497>
  — *CM (tangible rewards/vouchers contingent on verified abstinence) is described as the **most
  effective, "gold-standard" treatment for stimulant use disorder** — the class (cocaine,
  methamphetamine) with **no FDA-approved medication.** Note the live tension between abstinence-only
  CM and harm-reduction (reduced-use) goals.*

- **[S14] SMART Recovery (Self-Management and Recovery Training) — organizational overview
  (SMART Recovery / American Addiction Centers).**
  <https://americanaddictioncenters.org/therapy-treatment/smart-recovery> ·
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART_Recovery>
  — *The main **secular, self-empowerment alternative to 12-step** mutual-help (founded 1994). A
  **4-Point Program** (build motivation; cope with urges; manage thoughts/feelings/behaviors;
  live a balanced life) built on **CBT/REBT and MI** — no higher power, no lifelong label. Orientation
  source; evidence base is thinner than AA's.*

## The framing debate (contested — surface, don't smooth)

- **[S13] Heilig M, Kalivas P, et al. / Lewis M — the brain-disease-vs.-learning debate.**
  "Q: Is Addiction a Brain Disease or a Moral Failing? A: Neither" (PMC5486515)
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5486515/> · Lewis critique via _Neuroethics_ (PMC5486510)
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5486510/>
  — *The active academic dispute. The dominant **brain-disease model (BDM)** (NIDA/Volkow) is
  challenged by **Marc Lewis** and others who argue addiction is a **learned, developmental pattern
  of a normally-functioning plastic brain**, not a disease — and by "**disorder of choice**"
  theorists. A middle position holds it is **"neither a brain disease nor a moral failing"** but a
  disorder shaped by biology, learning, and environment. The BDM's clinical value (reduces blame,
  supports treatment access) is real; so is the worry that it can imply helplessness. Keep the
  vocabulary of all three models; don't teach any single one as settled fact.*

- **[S15] U.S. Surgeon General (HHS, 2016). _Facing Addiction in America: The Surgeon General's
  Report on Alcohol, Drugs, and Health._**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK424857/>
  — *The federal consensus report. Corroborates the **chronic-condition framing**, the enormous
  **treatment gap** (most people with SUD never receive specialty care), and the shift toward a
  public-health, continuum-of-care model. Used here for orientation/consensus, not for specific
  statistics beyond that framing.*
