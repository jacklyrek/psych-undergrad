# Unit 9 sources — Evidence-Based Modalities (a practical toolkit)

Evidence base for Unit 9 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-19. This is a Tier 3 "rounding out" unit, built to the same in-depth standard as
Units 1–8. It covers the concrete, structured methods a counselor actually reaches for — **CBT**
(cognitive restructuring + behavioral activation), **Motivational Interviewing**, **Solution-Focused
Brief Therapy**, and the **third-wave** acceptance/mindfulness therapies **ACT** and **DBT**. Anchored
on **professional-body and government sources** (the Beck Institute; SAMHSA's TIP 35 MI chapter on the
NCBI Bookshelf; the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science for ACT), **primary trials and
meta-analyses** (Jacobson's 1996 dismantling study, Dimidjian 2006, the COBRA Lancet non-inferiority
trial, Linehan's 1991/2006 DBT RCTs, the Franklin 2024 SFBT meta-analysis), and **peer-reviewed
reviews**. Four anchor **books** (J. Beck; Miller & Rollnick; Hayes et al.; Linehan) are offline and
cited via reputable secondary sources + the primary publication record, flagged. Two contested threads
are carried deliberately and quizzed at evaluate level: (1) the **common-factors / dodo-bird** caution
— "evidence-based modality" does not mean the technique is the whole story; outcome rests heavily on
the alliance (Unit 1); and (2) **allegiance and publication bias** in the modality literatures,
especially the very large SFBT effect sizes reported by proponent sources._

## Tier: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy — practice, cognitive triangle, restructuring

- **[S1] Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy. *Understanding CBT.***
  https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/
  _The institute founded by Aaron & Judith Beck; the definitional authority for the Beckian cognitive model. Authority: high (with the caveat that it is an advocacy/training organization for its own method)._
  The **cognitive model**: "the way individuals perceive a situation is more closely connected to
  their reaction than the situation itself" (situation → **automatic thought** → emotional/behavioral
  reaction). CBT is described as "a time-sensitive, structured, present-oriented psychotherapy" that
  is collaborative, goal-oriented, uses **guided discovery** and homework, and teaches clients to
  evaluate and respond to their own dysfunctional cognitions so they become their own therapist.
  Claims "**more than 2,000 studies** have demonstrated the efficacy of CBT" across psychiatric,
  psychological, and medical conditions.

- **[S2] Beck, J. S. (2020). *Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond* (3rd ed.). Guilford Press.**
  https://www.guilford.com/books/Cognitive-Behavior-Therapy/Judith-Beck/9781462544196
  _The syllabus anchor text and the standard graduate CBT primer. Book, offline — corroborated by S1/S3/S4 for every claim used. Authority: high._
  Source for the **three levels of cognition** (automatic thoughts → intermediate beliefs [conditional
  rules] → core beliefs / schemas), the **structure of a CBT session** (mood check, agenda,
  bridge from last session, guided work, homework set collaboratively, summary + feedback), and the
  cognitive **conceptualization** that drives individualized treatment.

- **[S3] Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy. *Understanding the Core Principles and Techniques of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy: Part II.***
  https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/understanding-the-core-principles-and-techniques-of-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-part-ii/
  _APA Division 29 affiliate; peer-reviewed practitioner content. Authority: high for technique descriptions._
  Details the **thought record / dysfunctional thought record** (columns: situation → automatic
  thought → emotion → evidence for/against → balanced alternative), **Socratic questioning / guided
  discovery**, **cognitive distortions** (black-and-white/dichotomous thinking, catastrophizing,
  overgeneralization, personalizing, mind reading), **behavioral experiments**, exposure, and
  homework.

- **[S4] Chand, S. P., Kuckel, D. P., & Huecker, M. R. *Cognitive Behavior Therapy.* StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf).**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470241/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (also cited in Unit 2 sources). Authority: high._
  The cognitive triangle and the hallway example (a friend walking past → "he hates me" vs. "he's in a
  hurry" → different feeling from the same event); the founding claim that dysfunctional automatic
  thoughts play a significant role in psychopathology; CBT as structured, ~8–12 sessions, directive.

## Tier: Behavioral Activation

- **[S5] Jacobson, N. S., Dobson, K. S., Truax, P. A., et al. (1996). *A component analysis of cognitive-behavioral treatment for depression.* Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 64(2), 295–304.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8871414/
  _The landmark BA "dismantling" trial. Book/article primarily accessed via S6/S9 summaries + the abstract; flagged. Authority: high (primary trial)._
  **n = 150** adults with major depression randomized to three conditions: **behavioral activation
  alone**, BA **plus** modification of automatic thoughts, or the **full cognitive-behavioral package**.
  All three improved comparably, with **no significant differences** — evidence that the behavioral
  component alone was sufficient to drive change, and that adding explicit cognitive-restructuring did
  not add measurable benefit in this trial.

- **[S6] Jacobson, N. S., Martell, C. R., & Dimidjian, S. (2001). *Behavioral Activation Treatment for Depression: Returning to Contextual Roots.* Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice, 8(3), 255–270.**
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1093/clipsy.8.3.255 · PDF: https://i-cbt.org.ua/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Jacobson-BA-for-depression-2001.pdf
  _The theoretical statement of modern BA. Authority: high._
  BA's roots in **Lewinsohn & Libet** (pleasant-events scheduling) and **Ferster** (functional
  analysis); depression as reduced **response-contingent positive reinforcement** and/or excessive
  **avoidance**; activation targets **avoidance and withdrawal** that generate "secondary problems."
  The **outside-in** logic: change behavior first, and mood/motivation follow — you do not wait to
  feel like it.

- **[S7] PMC narrative review — *A Narrative Review of Empirical Literature of Behavioral Activation Treatment for Depression* (2022). Frontiers in Psychiatry / PMC9082162.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9082162/
  _Peer-reviewed review, read for theory + effect sizes. Authority: high._
  The **TRAP → TRAC** heuristic (Trigger–Response–**A**voidance **P**attern → Trigger–Response–
  **A**lternative **C**oping); core techniques (activity **monitoring**, activity **scheduling**,
  **graded task assignment**, **values-based** activation); effect sizes across populations (e.g.,
  community-sample MDD Cohen's *d* ≈ 0.70–1.02).

- **[S8] Dimidjian, S., Hollon, S. D., Dobson, K. S., et al. (2006). *Randomized trial of behavioral activation, cognitive therapy, and antidepressant medication in the acute treatment of adults with major depression.* Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 74(4), 658–670.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16881773/
  _Primary RCT. Authority: high._
  **N = 241** RCT comparing BA, cognitive therapy (CT), paroxetine, and placebo. Among **more
  severely depressed** patients, **BA was comparable to antidepressant medication and both
  outperformed CT** — a striking result for a "simpler" behavioral treatment.

- **[S9] Richards, D. A., Ekers, D., McMillan, D., et al. (2016). *Cost and Outcome of Behavioural Activation versus Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Depression (COBRA): a randomised, controlled, non-inferiority trial.* The Lancet, 388(10047), 871–880.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27461440/
  _Large pragmatic non-inferiority trial. Authority: high._
  BA was **non-inferior to CBT** for depression, could be delivered by **junior mental-health workers
  with less intensive training**, and was **less costly** — the practical case for BA as a scalable
  frontline treatment.

## Tier: Motivational Interviewing

- **[S10] SAMHSA. *Enhancing Motivation for Change in Substance Use Disorder Treatment.* Treatment Improvement Protocol (TIP) 35, Chapter 3 — "Motivational Interviewing as a Counseling Style." NCBI Bookshelf NBK571068.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571068/
  _U.S. government clinical-consensus document. Authority: very high._
  MI = "a person-centered counseling style for addressing the common problem of **ambivalence** about
  change." **Spirit = PACE**: **P**artnership, **A**cceptance (absolute worth, accurate empathy,
  autonomy support, affirmation), **C**ompassion, **E**vocation. **Four processes**: **Engaging →
  Focusing → Evoking → Planning**. **OARS**: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening,
  Summaries. The **righting reflex** = the counselor's urge to jump in and fix/direct, which provokes
  the client to defend the status quo. **Change talk** (DARN-CAT: **D**esire, **A**bility, **R**easons,
  **N**eed / **C**ommitment, **A**ctivation, **T**aking steps) vs. **sustain talk**. "Resistance" is
  reframed as **discord** — a two-way street, a signal to change direction, not a client trait. "A
  higher ratio of reflections to questions predicts positive outcomes." Efficacy: small-to-strong
  significant effects vs. no treatment; strong support for alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis.

- **[S11] Miller, W. R., & Rollnick, S. (2013). *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (3rd ed.). Guilford Press.** (1st ed. 1991, *…Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior.*)
  https://www.guilford.com/books/Motivational-Interviewing/Miller-Rollnick/9781609182274
  _The originating anchor text. Book, offline — corroborated by S10. Authority: high._
  The founders' own definitions of spirit, processes, and the distinction between MI and simple
  advice-giving; the evolution from "principles" (1991/2002) to the four-processes model (2013).

## Tier: Solution-Focused Brief Therapy

- **[S12] Solution-Focused Therapy Institute. *Research in Solution-Focused Therapy.***
  https://solutionfocused.net/research-in-solution-focused-therapy/
  _Proponent/professional-association source — **flagged**: use for technique descriptions and study counts, treat its largest effect sizes cautiously (allegiance/publication bias). Authority: moderate._
  Techniques: the **miracle question**, **exception questions**, **scaling questions (0–10)**, **coping
  questions**, **compliments**, and the **formula-first-session task**; SFBT began as **empirically
  derived** (de Shazer & Insoo Kim Berg observed real sessions at the Brief Family Therapy Center,
  Milwaukee, noting what led to progress). Reports SFBT "effective in **86.3%** of studies" globally
  across 251 outcome studies. (The very large couples *g* ≈ 3.02 and group *g* ≈ 1.64 figures are
  flagged as likely inflated.)

- **[S13] Franklin, C., Ding, X., Kim, J., Zhang, A., et al. (2024). *Solution-Focused Brief Therapy in Community-Based Services: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Studies.* Research on Social Work Practice.**
  https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10497315231162611
  _Peer-reviewed meta-analysis of RCTs — the more rigorous evidence anchor. Authority: high._
  SFBT showed a **large effect (g ≈ 1.17) on psychosocial functioning** in community-based RCTs, with
  no evidence of harm; comparable outcomes to established treatments in fewer sessions.

- **[S14] Neipp, M.-C., Beyebach, M., et al. (2021). *Solution-Focused versus Problem-Focused Questions: Differential Effects of Miracles, Exceptions and Scales.* Journal of Family Therapy, 43(4).**
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1467-6427.12345
  _Peer-reviewed experimental study. Authority: high for the differential-technique claim._
  **Miracle and exception questions** reduced negative affect more than problem-focused questions;
  **scaling questions** produced more concrete **action steps** — evidence that the specific SF
  questions do specific jobs.

## Tier: Third-wave — ACT & DBT

- **[S15] Association for Contextual Behavioral Science (ACBS). *The Six Core Processes of ACT.***
  https://contextualscience.org/six_core_processes_act
  _The professional/scientific society for ACT. Authority: high (in-tradition source)._
  **Psychological flexibility** = "the ability to contact the present moment more fully as a conscious
  human being, and to change or persist in behavior when doing so serves valued ends." The **hexaflex**
  six processes: **acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self as context,
  values, committed action**. Mindfulness/acceptance processes (acceptance, defusion, present moment,
  self-as-context) + commitment/behavior-change processes (present moment, self-as-context, values,
  committed action). **Psychological inflexibility** = experiential avoidance + cognitive fusion + the
  other four pathological poles.

- **[S16] Hayes, S. C., Strosahl, K. D., & Wilson, K. G. (2011). *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: The Process and Practice of Mindful Change* (2nd ed.). Guilford Press.** (Chapter 3 excerpt online.)
  https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/hayes_ch3.pdf
  _The originating anchor text; ACT's grounding in Relational Frame Theory and functional contextualism. Book, chapter excerpt online. Authority: high._
  ACT's aim is not first-line **symptom reduction** but reduced **suffering** and increased
  **valued living**; the therapeutic target is the client's *relationship to* thoughts/feelings, not
  their content.

- **[S17] Linehan, M. M., Armstrong, H. E., Suarez, A., et al. (1991). *Cognitive-behavioral treatment of chronically parasuicidal borderline patients.* Archives of General Psychiatry, 48(12), 1060–1064.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1845222/
  _The original DBT RCT. Authority: high._
  vs. treatment-as-usual, DBT patients were **less likely to attempt suicide**, had **fewer and less
  medically severe** self-harm episodes, and **stayed in treatment** longer.

- **[S18] Linehan, M. M., Comtois, K. A., Murray, A. M., et al. (2006). *Two-year randomized controlled trial and follow-up of dialectical behavior therapy vs therapy by experts for suicidal behaviors and borderline personality disorder.* Archives of General Psychiatry, 63(7), 757–766.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16818865/ · PDF: https://www.personal.kent.edu/~dfresco/CBT_Readings/Linehan%20et%20al.%20(2006)_Archives.pdf
  _The key controlled DBT trial vs. non-behavioral experts. Authority: high._
  DBT roughly **halved suicide-attempt rates**, and reduced ER visits and psychiatric hospitalizations,
  vs. treatment by community experts — ruling out "just any good therapist" as the explanation. The
  basis for DBT being called the **gold-standard** treatment for BPD.

- **[S19] Linehan, M. M. (2015). *DBT Skills Training Manual* (2nd ed.). Guilford Press;** corroborated by **Psychwire, *The Four DBT Skills Modules* (Linehan expert insight).**
  https://psychwire.com/free-resources/expert-insights/resource-1o224sc/the-four-dbt-skills-modules
  _Linehan's own skills manual + her recorded explanation. Book offline; the four modules are cross-confirmed. Authority: high._
  The **four skills modules**: **Mindfulness** (core/foundational) and **Distress Tolerance** are
  **acceptance-oriented**; **Emotion Regulation** and **Interpersonal Effectiveness** are
  **change-oriented**. Full DBT also has **four components**: individual therapy, skills group, phone
  coaching, and a therapist consultation team. The central **dialectic** = **acceptance AND change**.

## Tier: Framing — "third wave," evidence-based practice, common factors

- **[S20] Hayes, S. C., & Hofmann, S. G. (2017). *The third wave of cognitive behavioral therapy and the rise of process-based care.* World Psychiatry, 16(3), 245–246 (PMC).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429332/ · network meta-analysis: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1189970/full
  _Peer-reviewed. Authority: high for the "three waves" framing and the evidence caveat._
  The **three waves**: (1) **behavioral** (conditioning), (2) **cognitive** (Beck/Ellis; content of
  thought), (3) **contextual / third-wave** (ACT, DBT, mindfulness-based; the *function and context*
  of thought, acceptance over control). Caveat carried into the readings: **standard CBT clearly meets
  empirically-supported-treatment criteria; third-wave approaches are evidence-based and growing but
  have not been shown to be *superior* to standard CBT** for most conditions.

- **[S21] APA Presidential Task Force on Evidence-Based Practice (2006). *Evidence-Based Practice in Psychology.* American Psychologist, 61(4), 271–285.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719673/
  _The APA policy definition. Authority: high._
  **Evidence-based practice (EBP)** = the integration of **(1) best available research, (2) clinical
  expertise, and (3) patient characteristics, culture, and preferences** — not "manual on autopilot."
  Anchors why this unit is a *toolkit chosen for the client*, not a ranking of techniques.

- **[S22] Cross-reference — Unit 1, [`theory-common-factors`](../wiki/theory-common-factors.md) (Wampold & Imel; Lambert; the dodo-bird verdict).**
  _The common-factors caution: alliance and other shared factors account for a large share of outcome variance; specific-technique differences between bona fide therapies are typically small. Every "which modality?" item in this unit sits under that caveat — you choose a method, but it works **through** the relationship, not instead of it._
