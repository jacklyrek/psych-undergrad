# Sources — Elective module: Existentialism, In Depth

Evidence base for [`../wiki/aux-existentialism.md`](../wiki/aux-existentialism.md) and its pages. Each entry has a one-line authority note. This is an **elective/ad-hoc module** (off the 12-unit syllabus spine; `aux-` namespace). It deepens the one-page Unit 2 treatment ([`../wiki/theory-existential.md`](../wiki/theory-existential.md), whose sources S6–S8 live in [`unit02-theories-sources.md`](unit02-theories-sources.md)) and borders **Unit 2 (Theories)**, **Unit 8 (Crisis/grief)**, and **Unit 10 (Group — Yalom's existential factors)**.

Two kinds of evidence are mixed here and should not be confused. The **philosophy** (S1–S9) is sourced to the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, the discipline's peer-reviewed reference work. Its claims are claims about what thinkers *argued*, not empirical findings. The **psychology** (S17–S30) is sourced to peer-reviewed journals and is where "does it work / is it true" questions get answered. Where a source is secondary or practitioner-authored, the note says so.

Built 2026-09-23. Scope agreed with a sibling elective: Heidegger's Dasein/being-in-the-world, Binswanger & Boss's Daseinsanalysis, and the phenomenological stance (bracketing) are covered in depth by `aux-phenomenology` ([[concept-being-in-the-world]], [[theory-daseinsanalysis]], [[concept-phenomenological-stance]]). This module links to them instead of duplicating them.

## Philosophical roots

- **[S1] Aho, K. "Existentialism." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (first published 6 Jan 2023).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/existentialism/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference entry and the backbone of the philosophy page. Source for: existentialism as an "intellectual movement" rather than a system; the term coined by Gabriel Marcel (1943); **only Sartre and Beauvoir explicitly self-identified** as existentialists; "existence precedes essence"; the **facticity ↔ transcendence** tension; authenticity vs. conformity to "the They"; anxiety as structural rather than object-directed; bad faith as denying either pole; Sartre's later "freedom-in-situation"; Jaspers's "limit situations."*

- **[S2] "Søren Kierkegaard." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantive entry, 2023).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. Anxiety as **"the dizziness of freedom"** (The Concept of Anxiety, pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis) and learning "to be anxious in the right way"; despair (The Sickness unto Death) as failure to become a self, in two forms: despair of possibility and despair of necessity; the aesthetic / ethical / religious spheres; "truth is subjectivity"; the leap of faith.*

- **[S3] "Friedrich Nietzsche." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantively revised 25 Jun 2026).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. "God is dead" as the collapse of the Christian moral framework, which threatens **nihilism**; revaluation of values; eternal recurrence as a **test of life-affirmation**; amor fati; "become who you are." The entry does **not** classify Nietzsche as an existentialist. He is a precursor whose themes the movement adopted.*

- **[S4] Webber, J. "Jean-Paul Sartre." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantively revised 10 Jul 2026).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. **Bad faith** and the **café waiter** ("in the mode of being what I am not"); being-in-itself vs. being-for-itself; the look and shame (the keyhole); **"condemned to be free"**; anguish; the later shift toward freedom constrained by history and scarcity (the practico-inert).*

- **[S5] "Simone de Beauvoir." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantively revised 11 Jan 2023).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/beauvoir/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. The Ethics of Ambiguity: freedom requires willing others' freedom; typology of flights from freedom (sub-man, serious man, nihilist, adventurer, passionate man); oppression constrains the **exercise** of freedom through material conditions; The Second Sex: "one is not born but becomes a woman," woman as Other.*

- **[S6] Aronson, R. "Albert Camus." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantively revised 13 Dec 2021).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. **The absurd** as the confrontation between the human demand for meaning and the world's silence; "There is only one really serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide"; the three responses: physical suicide, **philosophical suicide** (the leap into hope or transcendence), and **revolt**; "one must imagine Sisyphus happy"; Camus's **rejection of the existentialist label**.*

- **[S7] Mendes-Flohr, P. "Martin Buber." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (substantively revised 12 May 2026).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buber/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference. **I–Thou vs. I–It**; "the between" (das Zwischenmenschliche); dialogue as the basic reality of relation; records the 1957 Buber–Rogers public dialogue.*

- **[S8] "Martin Heidegger." _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ (first published 31 Jan 2025).**
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/>
  — *Peer-reviewed reference, used **only** for the brief Heidegger paragraph here (thrownness, das Man, anxiety vs. fear, **being-towards-death**, authenticity, existential guilt). The entry also documents Heidegger's Nazi Party membership (1933–45) and antisemitism. Full treatment: `aux-phenomenology`.*

- **[S9] Tillich, P. _The Courage to Be_ (1952), via EBSCO _Research Starters_ summary and W. Wildman's (Boston University) review.**
  <https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/courage-be-paul-tillich> · <https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/tillich/resources/review_tillich-paul_couragetobe.htm>
  — *Secondary summaries (a reference-database summary plus a BU theologian's review) that agree with each other. Source for Tillich's **three anxieties**: ontic (fate and death), moral (guilt and condemnation), spiritual (emptiness and meaninglessness). The claim is structural, so two agreeing secondary summaries are adequate for it.*

## Existential psychotherapy — theory and schools

- **[S10] Yalom, I. D. _Existential Psychotherapy_ (Basic Books, 1980), via secondary summaries.**
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_Psychotherapy_(book)> (orientation only) · also Unit 2's [S6]
  — *The canonical clinical text; I did not read the primary. Its content here comes through secondary summaries that agree with each other: the **four ultimate concerns** (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness); the two primary **defenses against death anxiety**, **specialness** (personal inviolability) and the **ultimate rescuer**; and the argument that the concerns operate as "givens" beneath symptoms. The Wikipedia page is lower-tier and used only to confirm terms. **Spot-check flag:** several points attributed to this book (the **wish → will → decide** sequence, the **neurotic vs. existential guilt** distinction as Yalom uses it, the **three kinds of isolation**, which Unit 2's [S8] also supports; **fusion** as the defense against isolation; **cosmic vs. terrestrial** meaning; meaning as a **byproduct** of engagement; the **here-and-now** emphasis) come from general knowledge of the text and were **not** re-confirmed against a source during this build. They are standard readings of Yalom's Part II–III, but check them against the book before relying on them for an exam.*

- **[S11] Yalom, I. D. _Staring at the Sun: Overcoming the Terror of Death_ (Jossey-Bass, 2008), via yalom.com and Psychotherapy.net.**
  <https://www.yalom.com/staring-at-the-sun/> · <https://www.psychotherapy.net/perspectives/articles/staring-at-the-sun-overcoming-the-terror-of-death/>
  — *The author's own site plus a professional-training publisher. Source for the **awakening experience** (death awareness after loss, illness, a dream, aging or trauma) as a catalyst; **"rippling"** (leaving something of oneself that passes on to others); the thesis that "though the physicality of death destroys us, the idea of death saves us."*

- **[S12] Kelland, M. _Personality Theory in a Cultural Context_, Ch. 9 "Viktor Frankl, Rollo May, and Existential Psychology" (LibreTexts, open textbook), §9.3–9.5.**
  <https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Psychology/Culture_and_Community/Personality_Theory_in_a_Cultural_Context_(Kelland)/09%3A_Viktor_Frankl_Rollo_May_and_Existential_Psychology>
  — *Open peer-reviewed college textbook (same source as Unit 2's [S7]). Frankl's biography (camp survivor; parents, brother and wife died); the "Third Viennese School"; will-to-meaning vs. Freud's will-to-pleasure and Adler's will-to-power; three routes to meaning; existential vacuum; noogenic neurosis; paradoxical intention and dereflection; "the last of the human freedoms"; super-meaning. Rollo May's normal vs. neurotic anxiety, love and will, the daimonic, courage. Binswanger's Umwelt / Mitwelt / Eigenwelt as May imported them.*

- **[S13] May, R. _The Meaning of Anxiety_ (1950; rev. 1977), via Kelland [S12] and corroborating summaries.**
  <https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Psychology/Culture_and_Community/Personality_Theory_in_a_Cultural_Context_(Kelland)/09%3A_Viktor_Frankl_Rollo_May_and_Existential_Psychology/9.04%3A_Rollo_May_and_Existential_Psychology>
  — *Source for May's definition of anxiety, "the apprehension cued off by a threat to some value which the individual holds essential to his existence." **Normal anxiety** is proportionate to the threat, needs no repression, and can be used constructively. **Neurotic anxiety** is disproportionate, relies on repression and defenses, and produces symptoms. **Spot-check flag:** May's parallel **normal vs. neurotic *guilt*** distinction (in his 1958 *Existence* essays) is standard in textbooks, but the sources consulted for this build did not confirm it directly.*

- **[S14] Schneider, K. J., & Krug, O. T. _Existential–Humanistic Therapy_ (APA Theories of Psychotherapy Series; 2nd ed. 2017); plus Schneider, K. J. (2015). "The case for existential (spiritual) psychotherapy." _Journal of Contemporary Psychotherapy_, 45, 21–24. doi:10.1007/s10879-014-9278-8.**
  <https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/evidence-based-foundations-existential-humanistic-therapy>
  — *APA's own theory-series volume. Source for **existential-humanistic (E-H) therapy** as the American branch (May, **Bugental**, Schneider): the cultivation of **presence** as the core of the relationship; working with the client's "protections" (resistance) in the here and now; "life-enhancing anxiety." Content as described by APA and the author.*

- **[S15] van Deurzen, E. — Society for Existential Analysis (founded 1988) and the "four dimensions of existence," via emmyvandeurzen.com and her KU Leuven MOOC manuscript _A Map of Human Existence_.**
  <https://www.emmyvandeurzen.com/existential-therapy>
  — *Primary author's own materials (practitioner source, not independent). Source for the **British school** and the four dimensions: **physical** (Umwelt), **social** (Mitwelt), **personal/psychological** (Eigenwelt), and **spiritual** (Überwelt). The fourth extends Binswanger's three. Used only for what the model says, not for any claim that it works.*

- **[S16] Spinelli, E. _Practising Existential Psychotherapy: The Relational World_ (2nd ed., Sage, 2015), via Wiley _World Handbook of Existential Therapy_ (2019) chapter abstracts and summaries.**
  <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119167198.ch9>
  — *Leading British existential-phenomenological text. Source for the **three rules** of the phenomenological method in therapy: **epoché** (bracket assumptions, a stance of "un-knowing"), **description** (describe, don't explain), and **horizontalization** (don't pre-rank what matters). Full treatment in `aux-phenomenology`.*

## The empirical side — terror management, death anxiety, meaning, isolation

- **[S17] Burke, B. L., Martens, A., & Faucher, E. H. (2010). Two decades of terror management theory: A meta-analysis of mortality salience research. _Personality and Social Psychology Review_, 14(2), 155–195. doi:10.1177/1088868309352321.**
  — *Peer-reviewed meta-analysis. **164 articles / 277 experiments**; mortality salience had a **moderate effect (r = .35)** on worldview- and self-esteem-related outcomes. The effect was larger with American participants, college students, a **longer delay** between the death prime and the measure, and people-related attitudes as the outcome. This is the high-water mark of TMT evidence, before the replication crisis.*

- **[S18] Klein, R. A., Cook, C. L., Ebersole, C. R., et al. (2022). Many Labs 4: Failure to replicate mortality salience effect with and without original author involvement. _Collabra: Psychology_, 8(1), 35271. doi:10.1525/collabra.35271.**
  <https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/8/1/35271/168050/>
  — *Large preregistered multi-lab replication (≈2,200 recruited; ≈1,550 after exclusions across 17 labs). It found **no mortality salience effect** on the classic worldview-defense measure (preference for a pro-US vs. anti-US essay author), **whether or not the original authors were involved**. The authors conclude the original was either a false positive or depends on conditions no one currently understands. The result is disputed: Chatard et al. (2020) re-analyzed it and found a small effect, while Haaf et al. (2020, Bayesian multiverse) found fairly robust evidence **for the null**.*

- **[S19] Schindler, S., Hilgard, J., Fritsche, I., Burke, B., & Pfattheicher, S. (2023). Do salient social norms moderate mortality salience effects? A (challenging) meta-analysis of terror management studies. _Personality and Social Psychology Review_, 27(2), 195–225. doi:10.1177/10888683221107267.**
  — *Peer-reviewed meta-analysis, co-authored by Burke of [S17]. The uncorrected effect was g = 0.34; **bias-adjusted estimates fell to between about −0.36 and 0.15, mostly nonsignificant**, with "signs of publication bias and/or the exploitation of researcher degrees of freedom." It also reports that a 2018 bias-corrected re-analysis of the 2010 meta-analysis gave d ≈ 0.32–0.61, smaller than the original d ≈ 0.75–0.82. Here a meta-analyst reassesses his own earlier effect, which makes this source especially credible.*

- **[S20] Iverach, L., Menzies, R. G., & Menzies, R. E. (2014). Death anxiety and its role in psychopathology: Reviewing the status of a transdiagnostic construct. _Clinical Psychology Review_, 34(7), 580–593. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2014.09.002.**
  — *Peer-reviewed review in a top clinical journal. Proposes death anxiety as a **transdiagnostic** construct, "a basic fear at the core of a range of mental disorders," including **hypochondriasis (illness anxiety), panic disorder, and anxiety and depressive disorders**. Calls for large controlled trials. The claim is presented as promising and not yet settled.*

- **[S21] Menzies, R. E., Zuccala, M., Sharpe, L., & Dar-Nimrod, I. (2018). The effects of psychosocial interventions on death anxiety: A meta-analysis and systematic review of randomised controlled trials. _Journal of Anxiety Disorders_, 59, 64–73. doi:10.1016/j.janxdis.2018.09.004.**
  — *Peer-reviewed meta-analysis of **15 RCTs**. Overall **g = .45**. **CBT was markedly effective (g = 1.7)**, with exposure-based work showing the largest effects, while "other therapies did not" separate from control (g = .20). Studies were few and generally low in quality. This matters for the existential module: the best-supported treatment for **death anxiety specifically** is exposure-based CBT, not existential therapy.*

- **[S22] Steger, M. F., Frazier, P., Oishi, S., & Kaler, M. (2006). The Meaning in Life Questionnaire: Assessing the presence of and search for meaning in life. _Journal of Counseling Psychology_, 53(1), 80–93.**
  — *Peer-reviewed; the standard measure in the field. A 10-item **MLQ** with two factors, **presence of meaning** and **search for meaning**. Unlike older measures it has no item overlap with distress scales. The factor structure replicates across translations, e.g. a 2026 French validation in a psychiatric crisis sample.*

- **[S23] Park, C. L. (2010). Making sense of the meaning literature: An integrative review of meaning making and its effects on adjustment to stressful life events. _Psychological Bulletin_, 136(2), 257–301. doi:10.1037/a0018301.**
  — *Peer-reviewed review in psychology's top review journal. The **meaning-making model**: **global** meaning vs. **situational** meaning, and **meaning-making efforts** vs. **meaning made**. Conclusion: theory has outrun data; some parts of the model are well supported and others are not; the **quality** of meaning-making may matter more than the **quantity**.*

- **[S24] Pinel, E. C., Long, A. E., Murdoch, E. Q., & Helm, P. (2017). A prisoner of one's own mind: Identifying and understanding existential isolation. _Personality and Individual Differences_, 105, 54–63. doi:10.1016/j.paid.2016.09.024.**
  — *Peer-reviewed; introduces the **Existential Isolation Scale** (6 items, 1–9). Existential isolation is the felt sense that **others do not share or understand one's experience**. It is distinct from interpersonal isolation (few relationships) and from loneliness.*

- **[S25] Zhou, N., Zhao, Y., Smith, K. V., Killikelly, C., Stelzer, E., Maercker, A., Xi, J., & Helm, P. J. (2023). Existential isolation and prolonged grief in bereaved people: The moderating role of culture. _Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy_. doi:10.1002/cpp.2846.**
  — *Peer-reviewed cross-cultural study (267 Chinese and 158 German-speaking bereaved adults). Validated the scale in both languages. Found **no cultural or gender differences** in existential isolation, and **higher existential isolation went with more prolonged-grief symptoms**, with culture moderating the strength of that link. Also notes existential isolation is higher among people with **non-normative experiences** (e.g. racial or sexual minorities).*

## The empirical side — does existential therapy work?

- **[S26] Vos, J., Craig, M., & Cooper, M. (2015). Existential therapies: A meta-analysis of their effects on psychological outcomes. _Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology_, 83(1), 115–128. doi:10.1037/a0037167.**
  — *Peer-reviewed meta-analysis in APA's flagship clinical journal, by existential-therapy researchers (read the conclusions with that in mind). **21 eligible RCTs → 15 with unique data, N = 1,792.** **Meaning therapies (6 studies)** had large effects on meaning in life (d = 0.65 post, 0.57 follow-up) and moderate effects on psychopathology (d = 0.47) and self-efficacy (d = 0.48). **Supportive-expressive** therapy had small effects on psychopathology (d = 0.20 / 0.18). **Experiential-existential (2) and cognitive-existential (1) therapies showed no significant effects.** The analysis used a fixed-effects model and the authors call the studies "small [in] number and low [in] quality." Their conclusion: support is strongest for **structured interventions** with psychoeducation, exercises, and direct, positive discussion of meaning **with physically ill patients**.*

- **[S27] Breitbart, W., Rosenfeld, B., Pessin, H., Applebaum, A., Kulikowski, J., & Lichtenthal, W. G. (2015). Meaning-centered group psychotherapy: An effective intervention for improving psychological well-being in patients with advanced cancer. _Journal of Clinical Oncology_, 33(7), 749–754.**
  — *Peer-reviewed RCT in a top oncology journal. **N = 253**, 8-session **MCGP vs. supportive group psychotherapy (an active control)**. Among completers (≥3 sessions), MCGP improved spiritual well-being and quality of life more, and reduced depression, hopelessness, **desire for hastened death**, and symptom distress more. **Anxiety did not differ.** In the stricter intent-to-treat analysis, effects held only for quality of life, depression and hopelessness. The intervention is built on Frankl's sources of meaning.*

- **[S28] Rodin, G., Lo, C., Rydall, A., et al. (2018). Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully (CALM): A randomized controlled trial of a psychological intervention for patients with advanced cancer. _Journal of Clinical Oncology_, 36(23), 2422–2432.**
  — *Peer-reviewed, unblinded RCT, **N = 305**, CALM plus usual care vs. usual care, 3–6 sessions. Depressive symptoms were lower at 3 months (**d = 0.23**) and 6 months (**d = 0.29**), with better end-of-life preparation at 6 months. **Small effects** against a **passive** comparator; no adverse effects.*

- **[S29] Chochinov, H. M., Kristjanson, L. J., Breitbart, W., et al. (2011). Effect of dignity therapy on distress and end-of-life experience in terminally ill patients: A randomised controlled trial. _Lancet Oncology_, 12(8), 753–762.**
  — *Peer-reviewed three-arm RCT (N = 441). **No significant differences in distress** (depression, desire for death, suicidality). Patients nonetheless rated dignity therapy as more **helpful**, better for quality of life and sense of dignity, and valuable **to their families**. It is a clean example of an intervention that patients value but that did not move the primary symptom outcomes.*

- **[S30] Jansson-Fröjmark, M., Alfonsson, S., Bohman, B., Rozental, A., & Norell-Clarke, A. (2022). Paradoxical intention for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis. _Journal of Sleep Research_, 31(2), e13464. doi:10.1111/jsr.13464.**
  — *Preregistered (PROSPERO) peer-reviewed meta-analysis, **10 trials**. Frankl's paradoxical intention produced **large** gains against passive comparators and **moderate** gains against active comparators, and reduced **sleep-related performance anxiety**, the proposed mechanism. Calls for stronger trials. It is the logotherapy technique with the best modern evidence, and that evidence comes from sleep medicine, not from logotherapy research.*

## Critiques and cultural fit

- **[S31] Cleare-Hoffman, H. P., Hoffman, L., & Wilson, S. (2013). _Existential therapy, culture, and therapist factors in evidence-based practice_ (APA convention presentation; PsycEXTRA doi:10.1037/e610422013-001); Wong, P. T. P. (2016). The need for existential cross-cultural competency in therapy and supervision. _PsycCRITIQUES_, 61. doi:10.1037/a0040311.**
  — *Critiques from **inside** the existential camp, graded here as **orientation-tier**: one is a convention presentation and the other a book review. They make the critique that is standard in counseling-theory textbooks: existentialism's emphasis on **individual** freedom, choice and "authorship" reflects **Western individualist** assumptions that can clash with clients whose identity is **familial or communal**, or for whom **oppression** limits choice. Both argue for adapting the approach, not dropping it. [S25]'s finding of no cultural difference in existential isolation offers a partial empirical counterweight.*

## Added during drafting

- **[S32] "Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy." _StatPearls_ (NCBI Bookshelf, NBK622746).**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK622746/>
  — *Peer-reviewed clinical reference hosted by the U.S. National Library of Medicine. Source for MCP's **four sources of meaning**: **historical** (life narrative, roles, legacy), **creative**, **experiential**, and **attitudinal**. Historical meaning is MCP's addition to Frankl's three. Also the MCGP format: 8 weekly 2-hour sessions, groups of 6–8, with sessions on identity before and after diagnosis, the sources of meaning in turn, and hope. Corroborated by the Breitbart & Poppito treatment manual (Oxford University Press).*

- **[S33] Frankl, V. E. _Man's Search for Meaning_ (1946; 1984 edition with the postscript "The Case for a Tragic Optimism"), primary text, corroborated by a peer-reviewed book review (PMC5111395) and standard study guides (orientation only).**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5111395/>
  — *Primary source for: the **tragic triad** (pain, guilt, death) and **tragic optimism**, meaning saying "yes to life" in spite of it, with suffering turned into achievement, guilt into change, and transience into responsible action (1984 postscript, from a 1983 lecture); **Sunday neurosis**; the existential vacuum as a "private and personal form of nihilism" showing up mainly as boredom; "**to suffer unnecessarily is masochistic rather than heroic**"; and concern over life's worthwhileness as "a spiritual distress but by no means a mental disease." The quotations were checked for consistency across multiple reproductions of the text.*

- **[S34] "The History of Daseinsanalysis," in _The Wiley World Handbook of Existential Therapy_ (van Deurzen et al., eds., 2019), ch. 1; and "Daseinsanalysis," _Encyclopedia of Psychology and Religion_ (Springer).**
  <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119167198.ch1> · <https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-1-4614-6086-2_154>
  — *Academic handbook chapter and reference-encyclopedia entry (abstract-level access). Source for Daseinsanalysis being developed in the 1940s by Swiss psychiatrists **Binswanger** (the first systematic existential approach to psychiatry and psychopathology) and **Boss** (the first systematic existential **psychotherapy**); Heidegger's endorsement of Boss and later disowning of Binswanger; the **Zollikon Seminars** at Boss's home, 1959–1969. Used only for the one-paragraph mention here. The sibling elective `aux-phenomenology` covers this in full.*
