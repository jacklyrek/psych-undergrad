# Unit 3 sources — Ethics, Law & Professional Identity

Evidence base for Unit 3 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-06. Anchored on the **primary document itself** — the 2014 ACA Code of Ethics
(full text extracted and quoted verbatim) — triangulated with peer-reviewed clinical references
(NCBI/StatPearls, PMC), primary case law (Cornell LII), professional bodies (APA, ACA), a federal
clearinghouse (.gov), and university/.edu pages. Lower-tier practice-blog sources used for
orientation/wording only and labeled as such. **Anchor text (not online):** Remley & Herlihy,

## Tier: primary documents & case law (the ground truth)

- **[S1] American Counseling Association — *2014 ACA Code of Ethics* (official PDF, counseling.org).**
  https://www.counseling.org/docs/default-source/default-document-library/ethics/2014-aca-code-of-ethics.pdf
  _The primary document — the profession's own code, approved by the ACA Governing Council. Authority: highest (it IS the object of study). Full text extracted; all standard numbers and quotes below are verbatim._
  Key content used: **Preamble** — five core professional values (human development across the
  lifespan; diversity/multiculturalism; social justice; integrity of the counselor–client
  relationship; competent and ethical practice) and **six ethical principles**: *autonomy*
  ("fostering the right to control the direction of one's life"), *nonmaleficence* ("avoiding
  actions that cause harm"), *beneficence* ("working for the good of the individual and society"),
  *justice* ("treating individuals equitably and fostering fairness and equality"), *fidelity*
  ("honoring commitments and keeping promises"), *veracity* ("dealing truthfully"). **Purpose
  section:** nine sections A–I; "No specific ethical decision-making model is always most
  effective"; "A breach of the standards and principles provided herein does not necessarily
  constitute legal liability or violation of the law." **A.2.a** informed consent as ongoing
  process, reviewed "in writing and verbally"; **A.2.b** types of information needed (purposes,
  goals, techniques, risks/benefits, qualifications, continuation of services, role of technology).
  **A.4.a** avoiding harm. **A.5.a** sexual/romantic relationships with current clients, their
  partners, or family members **prohibited** (in-person and electronic); **A.5.c** former clients —
  prohibited for **5 years** after last professional contact, and even then requires documented
  forethought that it isn't exploitive; **A.5.d** friends/family prohibited where objectivity is
  impaired; **A.5.e** personal virtual relationships with current clients prohibited. **A.6.a–e**
  managing boundaries: previous relationships, extending boundaries (weddings, hospital visits,
  purchasing a client's product), documenting boundary extensions *prior to* the interaction when
  feasible, role changes require fresh informed consent, avoiding harmful nonprofessional
  relationships. **B.1.b** respect for privacy; **B.1.c** "Counselors disclose information only with
  appropriate consent or with sound legal or ethical justification"; **B.1.d** explanation of
  limitations "at initiation and throughout." **B.2.a** the general confidentiality requirement
  "does not apply when disclosure is required to protect clients or identified others from serious
  and foreseeable harm or when legal requirements demand"; consult when in doubt. **B.2.b**
  end-of-life exception (option to maintain confidentiality); **B.2.c** contagious life-threatening
  diseases (may disclose to identifiable third parties at serious/foreseeable risk, after assessing
  client's intent to inform; follow state law); **B.2.d** court-ordered disclosure (seek client
  consent or seek to limit/prohibit disclosure); **B.2.e** minimal disclosure ("only essential
  information is revealed"). **C.2.a** boundaries of competence ("education, training, supervised
  experience, state and national professional credentials, and appropriate professional
  experience"; multicultural competency required across all specialties); **C.2.b** new specialty
  areas only after education/training/supervised experience; **C.2.d** monitor effectiveness;
  **C.2.f** continuing education; **C.2.g** impairment (refrain from providing services when
  impaired). **I.1.c** conflicts between ethics and laws: make known commitment to the Code, try to
  resolve; if unresolvable, "may adhere to the requirements of the law."

- **[S2] Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1 (1996) — opinion text, Cornell Law School LII.**
  https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/95-266.ZO.html
  _Primary case law (U.S. Supreme Court), hosted by Cornell's Legal Information Institute. Authority: highest for what the case held._
  7–2 decision (Stevens): recognized a **psychotherapist–patient privilege** under Federal Rule of
  Evidence 501 — protecting confidential communications from **compelled disclosure in federal
  court**. Reasoning: effective psychotherapy "depends upon an atmosphere of confidence and trust";
  "the mere possibility of disclosure may impede development of the confidential relationship
  necessary for successful treatment." Privilege explicitly extended to **licensed social workers**
  (the therapist in the case was a clinical social worker). Used for the
  privacy/confidentiality/privilege distinction.

## Tier: peer-reviewed / professional reference (lean on these)

- **[S3] StatPearls — *Duty to Warn*, NCBI Bookshelf (NBK542236).**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK542236/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (NCBI). Authority: high._
  Tarasoff case facts (Poddar/Tarasoff, 1968–69; therapist Dr. Lawrence Moore alerted campus
  police; Poddar released; clinical notes ordered destroyed; Tarasoff killed October 1969).
  **Tarasoff I (1974)** = duty to **warn** the potential victim; **Tarasoff II (1976)** = broader
  duty to **protect** ("take reasonable precautions to protect these third parties"). Justice
  Tobriner: *"The protective privilege ends where the public peril begins."* Reasonable protective
  steps menu: increase appointment frequency, arrange removal of weapons, adjust/initiate
  medication, voluntary/involuntary hospitalization, involve family, notify victim or police.
  State variation (as of the article's 2012-based count): **23 states** mandatory by statute,
  **10** common-law duty, **11** permissive, **6** no guidance (incl. e.g. Arkansas, Kansas, Maine,
  Nevada, New Mexico, North Dakota). Post-Tarasoff cases: *Jablonski v. United States* (must review
  prior records), *Ewing v. Goldstein* (threats communicated by family members count, in CA).
  APA (1987) threat criteria: specific identifiable victim + intent and ability + imminence.
  Candid about limits: "guidelines on accurately predicting and assessing dangerousness are
  lacking."

- **[S4] Darby, W. C., & Weinstock, R. (2018). *The Limits of Confidentiality: Informed Consent and Psychotherapy.* Focus (Am Psychiatr Publ), via PMC (PMC6493245).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6493245/
  _Peer-reviewed journal article (PMC open access). Authority: high._
  Standard mandated exceptions (danger to self/others, child and elder abuse; state-specific
  additions). Notes there is often "no specific legal obligation to inform patients of the limits
  of confidentiality," but ethical duties require it; exhaustive up-front warnings would be "time
  prohibitive and unnecessarily frightening for rare scenarios"; patients show poor retention of
  consent information weeks later — supports treating informed consent as an **ongoing process**,
  not a one-time signature.

- **[S5] APA Services — *Mandatory reporting* (patient confidentiality series).**
  https://www.apaservices.org/practice/legal/patient-confidentiality/mandatory-reporting
  _Professional body (American Psychological Association practice arm). Authority: high._
  Categories: child abuse/neglect, elder & vulnerable-adult abuse/neglect, duty to warn/protect.
  Trigger = **suspicion**, not proof; timing "typically require[s] immediate action." "All states
  and the District of Columbia provide some form of immunity from liability for good faith
  reporting." Failure to report: criminal charges (misdemeanor→felony), fines/jail, plus civil
  liability. Emphasizes knowing your jurisdiction's specific law.

- **[S6] Child Welfare Information Gateway — *Mandatory Reporters of Child Abuse and Neglect* (State Statutes Series).**
  https://www.childwelfare.gov/topics/systemwide/laws-policies/statutes/manda/
  _Federal clearinghouse (.gov, U.S. Children's Bureau). Authority: high. The page indexes the full state-by-state statute compilation._
  Covers which professional groups are mandated reporters, standards for making a report,
  privileged-communication carve-outs, reporter-identity confidentiality. Used as the pointer for
  "check your state's statute."

- **[S7] APA — *What Is the Difference Between Psychologists, Psychiatrists and Social Workers?* (apa.org, PTSD guideline patient pages).**
  https://www.apa.org/ptsd-guideline/patients-and-families/psychotherapy-professionals
  _Professional body (APA). Authority: high._
  Training paths: psychiatrists = MD/DO + psychiatry residency (3–4 yrs), prescribe, biologically
  focused; psychologists = doctoral degree (PhD/PsyD/EdD), 4–6 yrs + supervised practice +
  licensure; social workers = MSW → LCSW after supervised clinical work; counselors = master's →
  licensure (LPC).

- **[S8] APA Services — *About prescribing psychologists* (RxP).**
  https://www.apaservices.org/advocacy/issues/prescriptive-authority/prescribing-psychologists
  _Professional body (APA practice arm). Authority: high — but **dated**: lists the first five RxP states._
  New Mexico (2002, first), Louisiana (2004), Illinois (2014), Iowa (2016), Idaho (2017); also DoD,
  U.S. Public Health Service, Indian Health Service. Requires postdoctoral MS in clinical
  psychopharmacology + supervised hours + national psychopharmacology exam. **Note:** later
  reporting (Cato Institute 2024; Behavioral Health Business 2025) adds **Colorado (2023)** and
  **Utah (2024)** — treat "how many states" as a moving target (~7 as of 2024–25).

## Tier: practitioner / orientation only (wording and examples; not load-bearing)

- **[S11] Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy — *Boundaries and Multiple Relationships in Psychotherapy: Recommendations for Ethical Practice*.**
  https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/boundaries-and-multiple-relationships-in-psychotherapy-recommendations-for-ethical-practice/
  _APA Division 29's practitioner outlet. Authority: moderate._
  **Boundary crossing** = nonpejorative departure from common practice that may even help
  (shaking an extended hand; extending a session in crisis) vs. **boundary violation** = departure
  that is harmful/exploitative and places the client or the work at serious risk (e.g., romantic
  relationship). Multiple relationships become problems when they impair judgment, exploit, or
  raise risk of harm.

- **[S12] Zur Institute (Ofer Zur) — *Dual Relationships, Multiple Relationships, Boundaries, Boundary Crossings & Boundary Violations*.**
  https://www.zurinstitute.com/boundaries-dual-relationships/
  _Practitioner CE site by a boundaries specialist. Authority: moderate/orientation — used for the rural/small-community unavoidability point and terminology mapping (ACA says "nonprofessional relationships"; APA says "multiple relationships"), cross-checked against [S1]/[S11]._

## Points of disagreement / uncertainty found (surfaced in readings)

1. **"Duty to warn" is a misnomer in most places.** The 1976 ruling replaced *warn* with the
   broader *protect*, and state law varies enormously (mandatory / permissive / silent). Any flat
   statement "therapists must warn" is wrong as law in many states. [S3]
2. **Prediction is shaky.** StatPearls is explicit that validated tools for predicting
   dangerousness are lacking — the legal duty assumes a predictive ability the science doesn't
   cleanly support. This is the field's own critique of Tarasoff. [S3]
3. **How much to disclose up front** is genuinely debated: exhaustive confidentiality warnings may
   chill disclosure and aren't retained anyway; the ethical consensus is a clear, plain-language
   summary at intake plus revisiting as situations arise (consent as process). [S4][S1]
4. **RxP state count is a moving target** — sources disagree (5 vs 7) because the APA page is
   dated; flagged in the reading. [S8]
5. **State variation pervades everything legal** in this unit (reporting triggers, privilege
   statutes, duty-to-protect specifics). The readings teach the *pattern* and repeatedly point to
   "check your state's statute." [S3][S5][S6]
