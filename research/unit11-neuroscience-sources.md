# Unit 11 sources — Neuroscience & Biological Bases of Behavior

Evidence base for Unit 11 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-20. Tier 3 "rounding out" unit, built to the same in-depth standard as Units
1–10. Its job is a **working mental model** — not a neuroscience degree — of the brain and body in
mental health, so a counselor can answer client questions responsibly ("what's wrong with my
brain?", "should I take medication?"), coordinate with prescribers, and refer appropriately. Four
core areas: the **neuron and neurotransmission** (the substrate), the **autonomic nervous system**
(the body's fast balance), the **stress response** (SAM + HPA, the full version of Unit 8's
working-level trauma preview), the **psychotropic medication classes** (what they do and the
counselor's non-prescribing role), and **how psychotherapy changes the brain** (neuroplasticity;
the anchor Cozolino text). Anchored where possible on **peer-reviewed clinical references**
(StatPearls/NCBI Bookshelf), **government/professional bodies** (NIMH, CACREP), and the **primary
literature** for the contested threads. The unit deliberately carries several **contested threads**
quizzed at evaluate level: the **serotonin/"chemical-imbalance" theory of depression is not
supported** (Moncrieff 2022) even though antidepressants have real (if **modest**) effects
(Cipriani 2018); **neuroplasticity is real but overhyped**; and much clinical neuro-talk (polyvagal,
"amygdala hijack," left/right brain) runs ahead of the evidence. The anchor book (Cozolino) is
offline and cited via reputable secondary reviews + the primary publication record, flagged._

## Tier: the neuron, neurotransmitters, and neurotransmission

- **[S1] Sheffler, Z. M., Reddy, V., & Pillarisetty, L. S. *Physiology, Neurotransmitters.* StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf), 2023.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539894/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (same series anchoring Units 2/6/9/10). Authority: high._
  **Glutamate** = "the principal excitatory neurotransmitter" and "primary mediator of nervous
  system plasticity" (memory; excitotoxicity in Alzheimer's/Parkinson's). **GABA** = "the major
  inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system" (~40% of inhibitory processing;
  therapeutic target for anxiety/insomnia/epilepsy). **Glycine** = inhibitory, spinal-cord.
  **Dopamine** = learning, motor control, reward, emotion, executive function; dysregulation in
  schizophrenia, depression, Parkinson's, ADHD. **Serotonin** = mood + GI function; low levels
  correlate with depression; the target of many psychiatric drugs. **Norepinephrine** = stress
  response, sleep, attention, ANS; dysfunction in anxiety, mood disorders, ADHD, PTSD.
  **Acetylcholine** = muscle movement, memory, learning. **Histamine** = wakefulness. Release =
  calcium-evoked exocytosis of presynaptic vesicles via SNARE proteins; neurotransmitters bind
  postsynaptic receptors. (Reuptake/receptor-pharmacology detail corroborated by S6.)

## Tier: the autonomic nervous system

- **[S2] Waxenbaum, J. A., Reddy, V., & Varacallo, M. *Physiology, Autonomic Nervous System.* StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf), 2023.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538516/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  The ANS regulates internal organs **without conscious control** to maintain **homeostasis**,
  via two antagonistic divisions. **Sympathetic ("fight or flight"), thoracolumbar:** cell bodies
  T1–L2; short preganglionic → ganglia near the cord → long postganglionic releasing
  **norepinephrine** onto **adrenergic** receptors (α1/α2/β1/β2/β3); the **adrenal medulla**
  releases **epinephrine** into the bloodstream on sympathetic stimulation. Effects: ↑heart rate &
  contractility, pupil dilation, bronchodilation, glucose mobilization, blood to muscles, ↓digestion.
  **Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"), craniosacral:** cranial nerves III, VII, IX, **X (vagus)**
  + pelvic splanchnics S2–S4; long preganglionic → ganglia near/in the organ → short postganglionic.
  Effects: ↓heart rate, pupil constriction, ↑salivation & digestion. **All preganglionic neurons and
  parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine** (nicotinic on ganglia; muscarinic on
  parasympathetic targets); only sympathetic postganglionic neurons switch to norepinephrine. The two
  branches achieve equilibrium through continuous reciprocal (antagonistic) control.

- **[S3] Tindle, J., & Tadi, P. *Neuroanatomy, Parasympathetic Nervous System.* StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf), 2022; and Alshak, M. N., & Das, J. M. *Neuroanatomy, Sympathetic Nervous System.* StatPearls, 2023.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK553141/ · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK542195/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical references; used to corroborate S2's branch anatomy and the vagus nerve's dominance of parasympathetic outflow. Authority: high._

## Tier: the stress response (SAM + HPA; cross-links Unit 8)

- **[S4] Godoy, L. D., et al. *A Comprehensive Overview on Stress Neurobiology* / Gunnar, M., & Quevedo, K. (2007). *The Neurobiology of Stress and Development.* Annual Review of Psychology, 58, 145–173.**
  https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/literature_general_unsorted/gunnar_quevedo07_annu_rev_psychol.pdf
  _Peer-reviewed review (Annu. Rev. Psychol.) + overview literature on the two stress axes. Authority: high._
  Threat first triggers the fast **sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis** — catecholamines
  (adrenaline/noradrenaline) in **seconds** — then the slower **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)
  axis** produces **glucocorticoids (cortisol)** over **minutes**; the two "synergistically mobilize
  energy necessary for coping behaviors." The HPA cascade: hypothalamus **CRH** → pituitary **ACTH**
  → adrenal cortex **cortisol**, which feeds back (with hippocampal output) to shut off CRH
  (**negative feedback**). The awakening cortisol response is a sensitive marker of HPA integrity.

- **[S5] "Dysregulated HPA Axis Function Contributes to Altered Endocrine and Neurobehavioral Responses to Acute Stress." Frontiers in Psychiatry / PMC4358064, 2015; McEwen allostatic-load literature (ScienceDirect S0306453023002676).**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4358064/
  _Peer-reviewed. Authority: high (used for HPA dysregulation + the allostatic-load model)._
  Bruce **McEwen's allostatic-load model**: **allostasis** = stability through change (the body
  shifts its set-points to meet demand); **allostatic load** = the multi-system "wear and tear" the
  brain and body accrue when the emergency systems (SAM + HPA) are activated **too often or fail to
  shut off** — "how chronic stress gets under our skin and skull." Acute stress is **adaptive**;
  **chronic** activation is what damages. (The amygdala/hippocampus/PFC circuit that gates this is
  detailed in Unit 8's [`concept-neurobiology-of-trauma`](../wiki/concept-neurobiology-of-trauma.md),
  `source_units: [8, 11]`; this unit is its full neuroscience home. Cortisol findings in trauma are
  **inconsistent** — no clean biomarker — per that page's S14 [PMC9120425].)

## Tier: psychotropic medication classes (the counselor's non-prescribing map)

- **[S6] National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). *Mental Health Medications.* 2023.**
  https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/mental-health-medications
  _U.S. government mental-health authority; the class-by-class anchor. Authority: very high._
  Five classes. **Antidepressants** (SSRIs, SNRIs, NDRIs): affect serotonin/norepinephrine (±dopamine)
  reuptake; treat depression + anxiety, pain, insomnia; take **4–8 weeks**; **black-box warning** for
  ↑suicidal thoughts in people **under 25**; serotonin-syndrome risk; **do not stop abruptly**.
  **Anti-anxiety**: **benzodiazepines** ↑GABA (calming) but risk **tolerance/dependence**, must be
  **tapered**; SSRIs/SNRIs are first-line for chronic anxiety; buspirone (daily, 3–4 wks); beta-blockers
  (physical symptoms). **Stimulants** (ADHD, narcolepsy): ↑alertness/attention; ↑HR/BP; low misuse risk
  when used as prescribed. **Antipsychotics** (act on **dopamine**): **typical/first-generation** →
  risk of **tardive dyskinesia**; **atypical/second-generation** → metabolic monitoring (weight,
  glucose, lipids); ↑stroke/death in older adults with dementia (black-box); clozapine needs blood
  monitoring. **Mood stabilizers**: **lithium** (mania/bipolar; may **reduce suicide risk**; needs
  blood-level + kidney/thyroid monitoring — narrow therapeutic index); **anticonvulsants** (mixed/
  rapid-cycling). **General guidance, verbatim in spirit:** "People should **not stop** taking a
  prescribed medication, even if they are feeling better, without the help of a health care provider";
  and NIMH "does not provide medical advice, endorse or recommend specific medications, or offer
  treatment referrals" — the same non-prescribing posture the counselor holds.

- **[S7] Moncrieff, J., Cooper, R. E., Stockmann, T., Amendola, S., Hengartner, M. P., & Horowitz, M. A. (2022). *The serotonin theory of depression: a systematic umbrella review of the evidence.* Molecular Psychiatry.**
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01661-0 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35854107/
  _Peer-reviewed umbrella review; the key evidence against the "chemical imbalance" story. Authority: high (with the caveat that it is contested — see the reply below)._
  Across the main areas — **5-HIAA** metabolites (no association), plasma serotonin (lowered serotonin
  *associated with antidepressant use*, not depression), **5-HT1A** receptors and **SERT** binding
  (weak/inconsistent, confounded by prior drug use), **tryptophan depletion** (no effect in most
  healthy volunteers), and **SLC6A4** gene studies (largest/highest-quality found no association) —
  the review concluded: **"no consistent evidence of…an association between serotonin and depression,
  and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or
  concentrations."** Context: the "chemical imbalance" explanation is believed by a large majority of
  the public but is not endorsed as literal by most researchers. **Contested:** a formal reply
  (Jauhar et al., *Mol. Psychiatry* 2023) argues the umbrella method was inappropriate and that the
  serotonin theory was never meant as a single-chemical-deficiency claim — used here **both-sides**.

- **[S8] "Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRI) Pathway" (PMC2896866); "From Serotonin to Neuroplasticity: Evolvement of Theories for Major Depressive Disorder" (PMC5624993).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2896866/ · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5624993/
  _Peer-reviewed. Authority: high (SSRI mechanism + the delayed-onset/neuroplasticity puzzle)._
  SSRIs **block the serotonin transporter (SLC6A4/SERT)** → ↑serotonin in the synaptic cleft
  **immediately** — yet the **clinical effect takes 2–6 weeks**. That lag is the crux: it implies the
  benefit comes from **downstream, adaptive changes** (autoreceptor desensitization, ↑**BDNF**,
  synaptic plasticity/neurogenesis), **not** from simply topping up a low chemical. Antidepressant
  action is now theorized as **neuroplastic**, which dovetails with the "therapy changes the brain"
  literature (S12–S14).

- **[S9] Ghiasi, N., Bhansali, R. K., & Marwaha, R. *GABA Receptor Positive Allosteric Modulators / Benzodiazepines.* StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf), 2024.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK554443/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference. Authority: high._
  Benzodiazepines are **positive allosteric modulators of the GABA-A receptor**: they bind a site
  **distinct from the GABA site** and increase the probability the chloride channel opens **in
  response to GABA** (they don't open it themselves), amplifying inhibition → sedation/anxiolysis.
  Recommended for **short-term** use because prolonged use produces **tolerance** (GABA-A subunit
  downregulation + glutamatergic sensitization) and **dependence**; **withdrawal** can include
  rebound anxiety, insomnia, tremor, perceptual changes, and — dangerously — **seizures**, so they
  must be **tapered**, never stopped abruptly.

- **[S10] Howes, O. D., & Kapur, S. (2009). *The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia: version III — the final common pathway.* Schizophrenia Bulletin; Howes et al. (2016), *Dopamine and the aberrant salience hypothesis of schizophrenia.* World Psychiatry.**
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.20276
  _Peer-reviewed; the modern, revised dopamine account. Authority: high._
  The dopamine hypothesis, **revised**: not "too much dopamine everywhere" but **elevated presynaptic
  striatal (mesolimbic) dopamine** as a **final common pathway** funneling many risk factors (genes,
  obstetric complications, stress/trauma, drug use). **Aberrant salience:** dysregulated dopamine
  release **over-attributes meaning/significance** to irrelevant stimuli → the raw material of
  delusions/hallucinations. Antipsychotics work by **blocking D2 receptors**, damping salience —
  which explains why they target **positive** symptoms and less so negative/cognitive ones.
  **Glutamate, GABA, ACh, and serotonin** are also implicated; dopamine alone **does not fully
  explain** schizophrenia.

## Tier: neuroplasticity and how psychotherapy changes the brain (anchor: Cozolino)

- **[S11] Hebb, D. O. (1949). *The Organization of Behavior* (Hebbian plasticity); "Neuroplasticity," *Biological Psychology* (Pressbooks/ROTEL open text), 2023.**
  https://rotel.pressbooks.pub/biologicalpsychology/chapter/neuroplasticity/
  _Open educational text + the primary Hebb reference. Authority: high (definitional/uncontested)._
  **Neuroplasticity** = the brain's lifelong ability to reorganize structure and function in
  response to experience, learning, and injury (new connections; modified/pruned ones; in a few
  regions, new neurons). **Hebbian learning** ("**neurons that fire together wire together**," Hebb
  1949): repeated co-activation **strengthens** a synapse (long-term potentiation, LTP); **disuse
  weakens** it (long-term depression / **synaptic pruning**). Development shows exuberant synapse
  formation then **experience-dependent pruning** ("use it or lose it"). This is the cellular basis
  of *all* learning — including what happens in therapy.

- **[S12] Kandel, E. R. (1998). *A New Intellectual Framework for Psychiatry.* American Journal of Psychiatry, 155(4), 457–469.**
  https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.155.4.457
  _Nobel laureate's landmark framework; corroborated via secondary reviews (S13, Nature 4001816). Authority: very high._
  Kandel's thesis: **learning changes the brain** by altering **gene expression** and **synaptic
  strength/structure**; therefore **psychotherapy, when it works, is a biological treatment** that
  produces detectable, enduring changes in the brain — dissolving the false mind/brain, "real vs.
  psychological" split. The intellectual backbone of the whole "therapy changes the brain" idea.

- **[S13] Baxter, L. R., et al. (1992). *Caudate glucose metabolic rate changes with…drug and behavior therapy for obsessive-compulsive disorder.* Archives of General Psychiatry; reviewed in "How psychotherapy changes the brain — the contribution of functional neuroimaging," Molecular Psychiatry (4001816).**
  https://www.nature.com/articles/4001816
  _Primary study + peer-reviewed imaging review. Authority: high._
  The classic demonstration: in OCD, **successful behavior/cognitive-behavior therapy changed
  caudate-nucleus metabolism on PET** — and the change **resembled** that produced by SSRI medication.
  So "talking" and "chemistry" converge on the **same circuit**: two routes to one neuroplastic
  endpoint. Imaging reviews confirm psychotherapy produces measurable brain-function change across
  disorders (OCD's CSTC loop is the cleanest because it's a single identifiable circuit).

- **[S14] Cozolino, L. (2017). *The Neuroscience of Psychotherapy: Healing the Social Brain* (3rd ed.). W. W. Norton.**
  https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/neuroscience-psychotherapy-healing-social-brain-second-edition
  _The syllabus anchor text. Book, offline — thesis corroborated via reputable secondary reviews (Psychiatric Times; Psychiatric Services 54:10) + the publication record. Authority: high (clinically framed; much is inference — flagged)._
  Core theses: (1) psychotherapy — **regardless of modality** — works by promoting **neural
  integration** (connecting networks: cortical/subcortical, left/right, top-down/bottom-up), via
  combinations of empathy, affect regulation, narrative construction, and moderate arousal. (2) The
  brain is a **social organ** ("the social brain"): relationships **build and rebuild** the brain
  across the lifespan; a warm, attuned, safe therapeutic relationship functions like an **enriched
  environment** that stimulates plasticity. (3) Learning happens best in a **moderate-arousal**
  window (echoing Unit 8's window of tolerance). **Caveat carried in the reading:** the framework is
  compelling and clinically useful, but the link from a specific therapy move to a specific neural
  change is largely **inferential** — neural correlates are not mechanism.

## Tier: the counselor's scope + medication-withdrawal reality

- **[S15] Davies, J., & Read, J. (2019). *A systematic review into the incidence, severity and duration of antidepressant withdrawal effects.* Addictive Behaviors; recalibrated by Henssler et al. / Kalfas et al. (2024), *Incidence of antidepressant discontinuation symptoms,* Lancet Psychiatry.**
  https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(24)00133-0/fulltext
  _Peer-reviewed systematic reviews/meta-analyses. Authority: high (numbers are debated — both given)._
  Antidepressant **discontinuation/withdrawal is real**. Davies & Read (2019) estimated ~**half**
  (56%) of people who stop experience withdrawal, ~half of those rating it **severe**; the more
  conservative 2024 Lancet Psychiatry meta-analysis, subtracting placebo/nocebo effects, put the
  **excess incidence at ~15%** (about **1 in 6–7**) with **severe ~3%**. Either way: withdrawal is
  more likely after longer use, higher doses, and with **SNRIs/paroxetine**, is **reduced by slow
  tapering**, and is **easily mistaken for relapse**. The clinical takeaway for a counselor:
  antidepressants are **not "addictive" like benzos**, but stopping is **not nothing** — this is
  a prescriber conversation, and a counselor never advises stopping.

- **[S16] Council for Accreditation of Counseling and Related Educational Programs (CACREP). *2016 Standards* (§2.F.5.h / clinical-mental-health §5.C); The Professional Counselor (NBCC), Vol. 12(2).**
  https://www.cacrep.org/section-3-foundational-counseling-curriculum/
  _The accreditation body's curriculum standard + the counseling profession's peer-reviewed journal. Authority: high._
  CACREP requires counselor training in the **"classifications, indications, and contraindications of
  commonly prescribed psychopharmacological medications for appropriate medical referrals and
  consultation."** The scope line: counselors **do not prescribe** (and shouldn't even recommend OTC
  or herbal remedies — outside scope); the competency is to **recognize** medications, **educate**
  clients enough to have an informed conversation with their prescriber, **coordinate/consult** with
  prescribers, and **refer** — never to start, stop, or change a medication. (Ties to Unit 3
  [`concept-scope-of-practice`](../wiki/concept-scope-of-practice.md) and
  [`concept-helping-professions-compared`](../wiki/concept-helping-professions-compared.md).)
