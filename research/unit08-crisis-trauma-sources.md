## Tier: suicide risk assessment (instruments, interviewing, frameworks)

- **[S1] Columbia Lighthouse Project. *Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS).* Columbia University Department of Psychiatry / The Columbia Lighthouse Project. (Validation: Posner, K., et al. (2011). *The Columbia–Suicide Severity Rating Scale: Initial Validity and Internal Consistency Findings From Three Multisite Studies With Adolescents and Adults.* American Journal of Psychiatry, 168(12), 1266–1277.)**
  https://www.columbiapsychiatry.org/research-labs/columbia-suicide-severity-rating-scale-c-ssrs · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22193671/
  _Widely adopted, validated risk-screening instrument. Authority: high for the ideation/behavior distinction and the severity ladder._
  The C-SSRS separates **suicidal ideation** from **suicidal behavior** and grades each. **Ideation**
  is rated on a five-point severity ladder of increasing seriousness: (1) **wish to be dead**;
  (2) **nonspecific active suicidal thoughts**; (3) **active ideation with methods** considered (no
  plan); (4) **active ideation with some intent** to act; (5) **active ideation with a specific plan
  and intent**. **Intensity** items capture frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, and
  reasons. The **behavior** subscale records actual attempts, **interrupted** attempts (stopped by an
  outside circumstance), **aborted/self-interrupted** attempts (stopped by the person), **preparatory
  acts** (assembling means, giving things away, writing a note), and non-suicidal self-injury. Plain
  language; requires no mental-health training to administer; endorsed across clinical, research, and
  institutional settings.

- **[S2] Shea, S. C. *The Chronological Assessment of Suicide Events (the CASE Approach).* Training Institute for Suicide Assessment and Clinical Interviewing (TISA); SPRC Best Practices Registry. (Shea, S. C. (2009). *Suicide Assessment Part 2: Uncovering Suicidal Intent Using the CASE Approach.* Psychiatric Times.)**
  https://suicideassessment.com/the-case-approach/ · https://www.psychiatrist.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/11584_chronological-assessment-suicide-events-practical.pdf
  _Widely taught interviewing strategy (SPRC-registered). Authority: high for the interviewing method and the stated/reflected/withheld-intent distinction._
  The **CASE Approach** is an interviewing strategy for eliciting suicidal ideation, planning, behavior,
  and **intent** with higher validity. It sequences the interview through **four chronological
  regions**: (1) **presenting** ideation/events (the crisis that brought them in), (2) **recent**
  ideation/events (past ~8 weeks), (3) **past** ideation/events (lifetime), and (4) **immediate**
  ideation (how they feel about safety right now and their disposition). The delineation keeps the
  clinician "on task" and reduces **errors of omission**. Shea distinguishes **stated intent** (what
  the client reports), **reflected intent** (the extent of thinking/planning/action the client
  describes, which may reveal more than the stated intent), and **withheld intent** — the interview's
  job is to maximize reflected intent and minimize what's withheld. Validity-enhancing techniques
  (e.g., **behavioral incident** — asking for fact/sequence rather than opinion; **gentle assumption**;
  **normalization**; **denial of the specific**) reduce the tendency to under-report.

- **[S3] U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs / VA National Center for Patient Safety. *Suicide Risk Assessment Guide — Reference Manual.* mentalhealth.va.gov.**
  https://www.mentalhealth.va.gov/docs/suicide_risk_assessment_reference_guide.pdf
  _Government clinical reference. Authority: high for the warning-signs mnemonic, the acute/chronic distinction, and risk stratification._
  Distinguishes **chronic/distal** risk factors (long-standing background vulnerabilities: prior
  attempts — the single strongest predictor — psychiatric disorder, substance use, chronic pain/
  illness, trauma/abuse history, family history, access to means, male sex, social isolation) from
  **acute/proximal** risk factors and **warning signs** (recent events and states that raise risk in
  the near term). Warning signs via the **IS PATH WARM** mnemonic (American Association of
  Suicidology): **I**deation, **S**ubstance abuse, **P**urposelessness, **A**nxiety/agitation,
  **T**rapped, **H**opelessness, **W**ithdrawal, **A**nger, **R**ecklessness, **M**ood change.
  **Protective factors**: reasons for living, social connectedness, responsibility to family/children,
  engaged treatment, coping skills, cultural/religious beliefs discouraging suicide. **Direct inquiry
  does not increase risk.** Ask about ideation → **plan** → **intent** → **means/access** →
  **preparatory behavior**. Risk stratification guides disposition: **low** (outpatient + safety plan
  + follow-up), **moderate** (intensified outpatient / crisis services / increased monitoring / remove
  means), **high** (hospitalization / do not leave alone / immediate means removal).

- **[S4] Dazzi, T., Gribble, R., Wessely, S., & Fear, N. T. (2014). *Does Asking About Suicide and Related Behaviours Induce Suicidal Ideation? What Is the Evidence?* Psychological Medicine, 44(16), 3361–3363.**
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/does-asking-about-suicide-and-related-behaviours-induce-suicidal-ideation-what-is-the-evidence/
  _Peer-reviewed evidence synthesis. Authority: high for the "asking doesn't plant the idea" claim._
  Reviewing studies across community, adult, adolescent, and at-risk samples, found **no evidence that
  asking about suicide induces or increases suicidal ideation**; several studies found acknowledging
  and talking about suicide may **reduce** rather than increase ideation. Refutes the common clinician
  fear that direct questioning is dangerous — the far greater risk is **not** asking.

- **[S5] Klonsky, E. D., & May, A. M. (2015). *The Three-Step Theory (3ST): A New Theory of Suicide Rooted in the "Ideation-to-Action" Framework.* International Journal of Cognitive Therapy, 8(2), 114–129. (Framework origin: Joiner, T. (2005). *Why People Die by Suicide*, the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide.)**
  https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~klonsky/publications/3ST.pdf
  _Peer-reviewed theory. Authority: high for the ideation-to-action framework and its clinical implications._
  The **ideation-to-action framework** holds that the development of suicidal **ideation** and the
  **progression from ideation to an attempt** are **distinct processes with distinct causes** — so
  factors that explain who thinks about suicide differ from factors that explain who acts. **Joiner's
  Interpersonal Theory (IPTS):** ideation arises from **thwarted belongingness + perceived
  burdensomeness**; the leap to an attempt requires **acquired capability** (habituation to pain,
  fear, and death through exposure — prior self-injury, abuse, others' suicide, etc.). **Klonsky &
  May's 3ST:** ideation = **pain (usually psychological) + hopelessness**; **connectedness** is the key
  factor that keeps moderate ideation from escalating; the move to attempt is governed by **capacity**
  for suicide (dispositional, acquired, practical). Clinical upshot: **a person with strong ideation
  but low capability, or a client whose connectedness is intact, is at a different kind of risk than
  one who has acquired capability** — and means access is a *practical* contributor to capacity.

## Tier: safety planning and means reduction

- **[S6] Stanley, B., & Brown, G. K. (2012). *Safety Planning Intervention: A Brief Intervention to Mitigate Suicide Risk.* Cognitive and Behavioral Practice, 19(2), 256–264. (Template + clinician guidance: Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention, suicidesafetyplan.com; National Jewish Health clinician guide.)**
  https://suicidesafetyplan.com/ · https://www.nationaljewish.org/suicide-awareness/suicidal-patients-next-steps/safety-plan-with-a-patient
  _The intervention's primary source + official template. Authority: high for the six steps and the collaborative stance._
  The **Safety Planning Intervention (SPI)** is a brief, **collaborative, clinician-assisted** tool: a
  prioritized, written list of coping strategies and supports for a suicidal crisis. **Six steps, in
  order of escalating external reliance:** (1) **Warning signs** (personal thoughts/images/moods/
  behaviors that a crisis is building) → (2) **Internal coping strategies** the client can do **alone**
  to distract without contacting anyone (walk, music, exercise) → (3) **Social contacts and settings**
  that provide distraction (people/places, without disclosing the crisis) → (4) **People to ask for
  help** (family/friends the client *will* tell) → (5) **Professionals and agencies** to contact in
  crisis (clinician, local ED, **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline**) → (6) **Making the environment safe**
  (reducing access to lethal means — the means-restriction step). The ordering matters: it moves from
  self-reliance toward external help so the earliest steps are things the client can always do. It is
  **built with the client, not handed to them**, and is a living document to revisit. Explicitly
  **not** a "no-suicide contract" (see S7).

- **[S7] Stanley, B., et al. (2018). *Comparison of the Safety Planning Intervention With Follow-up vs Usual Care of Suicidal Patients Treated in the Emergency Department.* JAMA Psychiatry, 75(9), 894–900. (No-suicide-contract critique: Rudd, M. D., Mandrusiak, M., & Joiner, T. E. (2006). *The Case Against No-Suicide Contracts.* Journal of Clinical Psychology, 62(2), 243–251.)**
  https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2687370
  _Peer-reviewed effectiveness study + peer-reviewed critique. Authority: high for the evidence base and the contract contrast._
  In a cohort study of ED patients, the **SPI plus structured telephone follow-up** was associated with
  **~45% fewer suicidal behaviors** over six months versus usual care, and with greater treatment
  engagement. Safety planning is endorsed by the **Joint Commission, the VA/DoD guideline, and the Zero
  Suicide framework**. By contrast, the **"no-suicide contract" / "no-harm contract"** (a client's
  promise not to self-harm) is **not evidence-based**: no study shows it reduces suicide or attempts,
  it can create **false reassurance** for the clinician, it shifts toward defensive rather than
  therapeutic practice, and it offers the client nothing to *do* in a crisis. A **commitment-to-safety**
  or safety plan (what the client *will* do, and how to get help) has replaced it.

- **[S8] Harvard T. H. Chan School of Public Health, Means Matter program. *Lethal Means Counseling* and *Means Matter* research summaries.**
  https://hsph.harvard.edu/research/means-matter/lethal-means-counseling/ · https://publichealth.jhu.edu/center-for-gun-violence-solutions/lethal-means-safety-counseling
  _Public-health program at a leading school. Authority: high for means-restriction rationale and the counseling method._
  **Means reduction / lethal-means safety counseling** = collaboratively reducing a suicidal person's
  access to highly lethal methods until the crisis passes. Rationale: (1) **case fatality varies
  enormously by method** — roughly **85–90% of firearm attempts are fatal**, versus a **few percent**
  for overdose/cutting — so the method a person reaches for in a crisis heavily determines whether they
  live; (2) **suicidal crises are often brief and impulsive** — the interval between deciding and acting
  is frequently **minutes to an hour**; (3) **method substitution is incomplete** — many people do not
  simply switch to an equally lethal method, and (4) **most attempt survivors do not go on to die by
  suicide** (roughly **9 in 10**). The counseling targets **firearms** (best: temporary **off-site**
  storage with a trusted person or facility; otherwise locked, unloaded, ammunition stored separately)
  and **medications** (limit quantities, lock up, dispose of excess). It is **collaborative and
  time-limited** ("while things are hard"), not confiscation. Despite the evidence it is **underused**
  — one survey found ~78% of EDs assess access to means but only ~35% provide means counseling.

## Tier: crisis intervention and de-escalation models

- **[S9] James, R. K., & Gilliland, B. E. *Crisis Intervention Strategies* (anchor text; latest eds.). Six-step model summarized via clinician references (ICANotes; university crisis-intervention course materials).**
  https://www.icanotes.com/2022/04/19/6-step-crisis-intervention-model-explained/
  _The unit's anchor text (offline); six-step model corroborated via reputable secondary references. Authority: high for the model's content, secondary for exact wording._
  Defines a **crisis** as a perception of an event or situation as an **intolerable difficulty that
  exceeds the person's current resources and coping** (Caplan's tradition) — crisis is in the
  *perception/response*, not the event. The **six-step model** blends **listening** (steps 1–3) and
  **acting** (steps 4–6): (1) **Define the problem** from the client's viewpoint; (2) **Ensure client
  safety** — the continuous first priority; (3) **Provide support** (unconditional, present); (4)
  **Examine alternatives** (mobilize situational supports, coping mechanisms, positive thinking); (5)
  **Make plans** (concrete, collaborative, doable steps that restore some control); (6) **Obtain
  commitment** to the plan. Safety is not one step but the thread running through all six.

- **[S10] Roberts, A. R. (2005). *Bridging the Past and Present to the Future of Crisis Intervention and Crisis Management.* In *Crisis Intervention Handbook: Assessment, Treatment, and Research* (3rd ed.). Roberts' Seven-Stage Crisis Intervention Model (R-SSCIM). (Summarized via crisis-intervention teaching references.)**
  https://umsystem.pressbooks.pub/aswbprep/chapter/crisis-intervention-theories/
  _Established crisis-intervention model. Authority: high for the seven stages (secondary summary)._
  Roberts' **seven stages**: (1) **plan and conduct a biopsychosocial and lethality/risk assessment**;
  (2) **establish rapport and a collaborative relationship**; (3) **identify the major problems**
  (including the "last straw"/precipitant); (4) **deal with feelings and emotions** (active listening,
  validation); (5) **generate and explore alternatives**; (6) **develop and formulate an action plan**;
  (7) **follow-up plan and agreement**. Shares the assessment-first, rapport, alternatives, plan, and
  follow-up spine with the James & Gilliland model — different granularity, same logic.

- **[S11] Richmond, J. S., Berlin, J. S., Fishkind, A. B., et al. (2012). *Verbal De-escalation of the Agitated Patient: Consensus Statement of the American Association for Emergency Psychiatry Project BETA De-escalation Workgroup.* Western Journal of Emergency Medicine, 13(1), 17–25. PMC3298202.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3298202/
  _Professional consensus statement. Authority: high for verbal de-escalation technique._
  **Project BETA** (Best practices in Evaluation and Treatment of Agitation). De-escalation goal in
  three moves: verbally **engage** the patient → establish a **collaborative** relationship →
  verbally **de-escalate** them out of agitation. The **ten domains**: (1) **respect personal space**
  (≥ two arms' lengths; keep exits clear for both); (2) **do not be provocative** (calm posture, hands
  visible, no challenge); (3) **establish verbal contact** — **only one person speaks** to the patient;
  (4) **be concise** (short sentences, simple words — agitated people process language poorly); (5)
  **identify wants and feelings**; (6) **listen closely** (active listening; "Miller's law" — assume
  what they say is true from their view; restate it back); (7) **agree or agree to disagree** (find
  common ground); (8) **set clear limits** matter-of-factly, without threats; (9) **offer choices and
  optimism** (alternatives to violence, realistic hope); (10) **debrief** the patient and staff
  afterward. **Coercion/restraint is a last resort** — it can reinforce that violence resolves conflict
  and is associated with worse outcomes; successful verbal de-escalation preserves dignity and the
  therapeutic relationship.

## Tier: trauma neurobiology, trauma-informed care, and trauma treatment

- **[S12] Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA). (2014). *SAMHSA's Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach.* HHS Publication No. (SMA) 14-4884. Rockville, MD.**
  https://library.samhsa.gov/product/samhsas-concept-trauma-and-guidance-trauma-informed-approach/sma14-4884 (definition/4Rs/6 principles confirmed via the National Coalition for Homeless Veterans research brief and OpenTextBC peer-support curriculum)
  _The field's foundational trauma-informed-care framework. Authority: high (primary framework document; specifics corroborated across two secondary summaries)._
  **Definition — the "three E's":** trauma results from **an Event (or series of events) that is
  Experienced** by the person **as harmful or life-threatening** and that has lasting adverse
  **Effects** on functioning and mental, physical, social, emotional, or spiritual well-being. The
  subjective **experience** is what makes an event traumatic — the same event traumatizes one person
  and not another. **A trauma-informed *approach* — the "four R's":** an organization/program (1)
  **Realizes** the widespread impact of trauma and paths to recovery; (2) **Recognizes** the signs and
  symptoms of trauma in clients, staff, and others; (3) **Responds** by integrating knowledge of trauma
  into policies, procedures, and practices; and (4) actively **resists re-traumatization**. **Six key
  principles:** (i) **Safety**; (ii) **Trustworthiness and transparency**; (iii) **Peer support**;
  (iv) **Collaboration and mutuality**; (v) **Empowerment, voice, and choice**; (vi) **Cultural,
  historical, and gender issues**. Crucially, SAMHSA **distinguishes a trauma-informed *approach***
  (a universal, organization-wide stance that assumes any client *may* be a trauma survivor and aims
  above all to **avoid re-traumatizing**) **from trauma-specific *interventions/services*** (treatments
  that directly address trauma symptoms and the traumatic event). The primary goal of the approach is
  to **avoid re-traumatization**; it does not require the client to disclose or process the trauma.

- **[S13] American Psychological Association (2017), *Clinical Practice Guideline for the Treatment of PTSD*, and U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs / Department of Defense (2023), *VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of PTSD.* (First-line trauma-focused psychotherapies.)**
  https://www.apa.org/ptsd-guideline · https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/MH/ptsd/
  _Professional-body evidence guidelines. Authority: high for what counts as trauma treatment._
  Both guidelines identify **trauma-focused psychotherapies** as first-line for PTSD: **Prolonged
  Exposure (PE)**, **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)**, **Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT** — the
  standard for children/adolescents**)**, and **Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)**.
  These are **trauma treatments** — structured, symptom-reducing, delivered by clinicians trained in the
  specific protocol — and are categorically different from **trauma-*informed* care**, which is a stance
  any counselor adopts to create safety and avoid harm regardless of setting or specialty. (EMDR's
  mechanism — whether the bilateral eye movements add anything beyond the exposure component — remains
  debated even though the package works; flagged in the reading.)

- **[S14] Sheffler, J. L., et al. / peer-reviewed synthesis: *Childhood Trauma, the HPA Axis and Psychiatric Illnesses: A Targeted Literature Synthesis.* Frontiers in Psychiatry / PMC9120425 (2022).**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9120425/
  _Peer-reviewed review (read in full via pypdf). Authority: high for the HPA axis and limbic/prefrontal stress circuitry._
  The **HPA axis**: the hypothalamus releases **CRH** → the pituitary releases **ACTH** → the adrenal
  cortex releases **cortisol**; the system is normally reined in by **negative feedback** (cortisol and
  hippocampal output inhibit further CRH). Trauma/early-life stress **dysregulates** it — findings are
  **inconsistent** (some show elevated baseline cortisol, others reduced morning cortisol/blunted
  reactivity), and "dysregulated responses are **not consistently associated with psychopathology**,"
  a caution against over-reading cortisol as a trauma biomarker. Circuitry: the **amygdala** assigns
  emotional/threat valence and is **excitatory** to the HPA axis (enlarged/hyperactive after early
  stress); the **hippocampus** contextualizes memory and is **inhibitory** to the axis (reduced volume
  is a common trauma/MDD finding); the **medial prefrontal cortex** provides top-down regulation
  (reduced volume after childhood maltreatment). McEwen's **allostatic load** frames chronic stress as
  a lasting shift in the body's set-point.

- **[S15] Guy-Evans, O. / Simply Psychology. *The Neurobiology of Trauma: How Traumatic Experiences Change the Brain.* simplypsychology.com. (Orientation-tier synthesis; corroborated against S14.)**
  https://www.simplypsychology.com/articles/neurobiology-of-trauma
  _Educational secondary source. Authority: moderate (orientation-level; used for the working-model framing and corroborated by peer-reviewed S14)._
  Working model: in trauma/PTSD the **amygdala** is hyperactive (exaggerated, over-generalized fear),
  the **hippocampus** shows reduced volume (8–26% in some combat-veteran samples) and **fails to
  time-stamp** the memory as *past* (so it intrudes as if present), and the **prefrontal cortex** shows
  reduced regulatory activity — a "regulatory failure" where fear can't be dampened. Distinguishes
  **explicit memory** (hippocampus-dependent, verbal, narrative, contextualized) from **implicit
  memory** (amygdala-dependent, non-verbal, sensory/emotional/bodily) — trauma disrupts explicit
  encoding while strengthening implicit, so a survivor may have somatic/emotional reactions and
  fragmentary sensory images without a coherent narrative ("the body remembers before the mind"). The
  **fight/flight/freeze** cascade includes **freeze/tonic immobility** — an involuntary shutdown, not a
  choice, which matters for how survivors judge their own "why didn't I fight back" reactions.

- **[S16] Siegel, D. J. (1999). *The Developing Mind.* — the "window of tolerance." (Summarized via clinical trauma-education references; PA DHS trauma-informed-care tip sheet.)**
  https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/dhs/documents/trauma-informed-care/tip-sheets/2024-09-september-final-tts.pdf
  _Widely used clinical heuristic. Authority: moderate (a useful teaching model, not a validated neurobiological construct — flagged as such)._
  The **window of tolerance** is the arousal band within which a person can process experience and stay
  regulated (think clearly, stay present, feel without being overwhelmed). Above it = **hyperarousal**
  (fight/flight: panic, rage, racing thoughts, hypervigilance); below it = **hypoarousal** (freeze/
  shutdown: numbness, dissociation, flatness, collapse). Trauma **narrows and destabilizes** the window,
  so small triggers push a survivor out of it. Clinical use: notice which side the client is on and help
  them back into the window (grounding for hyperarousal; re-engagement/orienting for hypoarousal) before
  doing any content work — a concrete operationalization of trauma-informed pacing. A **heuristic**, not
  a measured brain state.

- **[S17] van der Kolk, B. (2014). *The Body Keeps the Score: Brain, Mind, and Body in the Healing of Trauma.* Viking. (Popular-press — flagged per course-map.)**
  _Best-selling trade book; clinically influential but **not peer-reviewed**. Authority: low-moderate — used only for framing already corroborated by S14/S15; contested claims labeled._
  Popularized the ideas that trauma is registered in the body and nervous system (not just as narrative
  memory), that traumatic memory is stored differently from ordinary memory, and that body-based and
  regulation-focused approaches matter. **Corroborated**: the explicit/implicit memory split and limbic
  hyperarousal (S14, S15). **Flagged as contested/overstated**: the strong "the body *stores* trauma"
  metaphor, sweeping claims about repressed/recovered traumatic memory (the memory-as-videotape view is
  rejected by memory science — trauma memory is reconstructive and suggestible), and reliance on
  **polyvagal theory** (Porges), whose specific neurophysiological claims are **scientifically
  contested**. Use the book for orientation and metaphor, corroborate the mechanisms elsewhere.

## Tier: counselor cost of the work (vicarious trauma, self-care)

- **[S18] McCann, I. L., & Pearlman, L. A. (1990). *Vicarious Traumatization: A Framework for Understanding the Psychological Effects of Working With Victims.* Journal of Traumatic Stress, 3(1), 131–149.**
  https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00975140
  _The construct's origin paper. Authority: high for the definition and the Constructivist Self-Development Theory (CSDT) frame._
  **Coined "vicarious traumatization" (VT).** VT is the **transformation of the counselor's inner
  experience** — disruptions to their **cognitive schemas** (beliefs about self and world in the areas
  of **safety, trust, esteem, intimacy, and control**) — resulting from **empathic engagement** with
  clients' trauma material. It is framed by **Constructivist Self-Development Theory (CSDT)**: people
  construct reality through schemas, and repeated exposure to others' trauma reshapes the helper's
  schemas. VT is **cumulative** (each traumatized client reinforces the change) and potentially
  **permanent** if unaddressed — and it is a normal, expectable *cost of caring*, not a weakness.

- **[S19] Trippany, R. L., Kress, V. E. W., & Wilcoxon, S. A. (2004). *Preventing Vicarious Trauma: What Counselors Should Know When Working With Trauma Survivors.* Journal of Counseling & Development, 82(1), 31–37.**
  https://ovc.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh226/files/media/document/vt_summary_and_a_new_theory-508.pdf
  _Peer-reviewed **counseling** journal (read in full via pypdf). Authority: high for the VT/burnout/countertransference distinctions and prevention._
  Draws the key **distinctions**: **VT** is a reaction to **specific client trauma material**, occurs
  **only** in those working with trauma survivors, has a **sudden/abrupt onset**, involves **schema
  disruptions** (safety/trust/esteem/intimacy/control) and **intrusive imagery**, and is **pervasive**
  (spills into all of life) and **cumulative**. **Burnout** is general **emotional exhaustion/overload**
  from difficult or high-volume caseloads, occurs in **any** profession, has a **gradual** onset, and
  **lacks** the schema/imagery changes. **Countertransference** is the counselor's reaction rooted in
  **their own** past experiences and is **session-bound**, whereas VT transcends the session. The
  paper stresses that **unaddressed VT is an ethical problem** (boundary lapses, clinical error,
  misdiagnosis, "rescuing," reduced quality of care) and lays out **prevention as shared responsibility**:
  **caseload management** (limit trauma clients per week), **peer supervision/consultation** (85% of
  trauma counselors' most-used coping method; also an *ethical* way to debrief given confidentiality
  limits), **agency responsibility** (supervision, training, benefits, caseload limits — VT prevention
  is **organizational, not just personal**), **traumatology training**, **work/play/rest balance**,
  social support, and **meaning/spirituality**.

- **[S20] Figley, C. R. (1995). *Compassion Fatigue: Coping With Secondary Traumatic Stress Disorder in Those Who Treat the Traumatized.* + Stamm, B. H. *Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL).* proqol.org.**
  https://proqol.org/
  _Foundational construct + the standard measure. Authority: high for the STS/compassion-fatigue terms and the ProQOL._
  **Secondary traumatic stress (STS)** = **PTSD-like symptoms** (intrusion, avoidance, arousal) arising
  from **indirect** exposure to another's trauma; onset can be **rapid** (even a single exposure).
  **Compassion fatigue** is Figley's umbrella term often taken to combine **STS + burnout** — the
  cumulative *cost of caring*. The **ProQOL** (Stamm) measures **professional quality of life** on three
  scales: **compassion satisfaction** (the positive — the reward of helping), **burnout**, and
  **secondary traumatic stress** — capturing that the work has both a fulfilling and a depleting side.
  (Terms overlap in the literature; the readings treat VT, STS, compassion fatigue, burnout, and
  countertransference as a **confusable cluster** and quiz the discriminations.)

- **[S21] American Counseling Association. (2014). *ACA Code of Ethics.* — C.2.g (Impairment) and the professional-responsibility sections. (Also cited in Unit 3.)**
  https://www.counseling.org/resources/aca-code-of-ethics.pdf
  _The profession's binding ethics code. Authority: high for the self-care-as-ethical-duty claim._
  Standard **C.2.g (Impairment)**: counselors monitor themselves for signs of impairment from their own
  physical, mental, or emotional problems, **refrain from offering or providing services when impaired**,
  seek assistance, and — if necessary — **limit, suspend, or terminate** their professional
  responsibilities until it is safe to resume. This is the code hook that makes **self-care an ethical
  competency, not a personal luxury**: an impaired counselor (including one impaired by unaddressed VT/
  STS) who keeps seeing clients is committing an ethical breach. (See [[concept-scope-of-practice]] from
  Unit 3.)

## Points of disagreement / uncertainty found (surfaced in readings)

1. **We cannot reliably *predict* individual suicide.** Risk factors and instruments (C-SSRS, IS PATH
   WARM) identify **populations** at elevated risk and **guide intervention and disposition**; they do
   **not** forecast who will die and when — actuarial prediction of individual suicide is poor. This is
   the same **prediction critique** raised against Tarasoff-style dangerousness prediction in Unit 3
   ([[study-tarasoff]]). Assess to **intervene and manage risk**, not to prophesy. [S1][S3][S5]
2. **Asking about suicide does not plant the idea.** The clinician's fear of "putting the thought in
   their head" is not supported; direct, plain questioning is safe and may reduce distress. The real
   danger is **avoidance**. [S4]
3. **No-suicide contracts are not evidence-based** and can create false reassurance and defensive
   practice; **collaborative safety planning** (with means reduction) has replaced them. [S6][S7]
4. **Means restriction works at the population level despite the substitution objection.** Critics argue
   people substitute methods; the evidence is that substitution is **incomplete**, crises are often
   **transient/impulsive**, and case fatality differs so much by method that reducing access to the most
   lethal means saves lives. [S8]
5. **Trauma neurobiology: read the direction, not the details.** The amygdala-up / hippocampus-and-PFC-
   down picture and the explicit/implicit memory split are well supported in direction, but **cortisol
   findings are inconsistent** and not a clean biomarker, and the **window of tolerance** and
   **polyvagal theory** are clinically useful **heuristics**, not validated brain states. **van der
   Kolk's *Body Keeps the Score* is popular-press** — corroborate its mechanisms (done here against S14/
   S15) and treat the strong "trauma stored in the body" and recovered-memory claims as contested.
   [S14][S15][S16][S17]
6. **Trauma-informed ≠ trauma treatment.** A trauma-informed *approach* (SAMHSA's 4 Rs / 6 principles)
   is a **universal stance** to avoid re-traumatization that **any** counselor adopts and that **does
   not require the client to disclose or process** the trauma; **trauma treatment** (PE, CPT, TF-CBT,
   EMDR) is a **specific, trained modality** that reduces symptoms. Conflating them leads beginners to
   "dig for the trauma story," which is exactly what trauma-informed care restrains (ties to the
   don't-probe stance from [[aux-psychological-first-aid]] and [[concept-intake-interview]]). [S12][S13]
7. **The cost-of-the-work terms overlap.** VT (schema disruption from empathic exposure), STS (PTSD-like
   symptoms from indirect exposure), compassion fatigue (STS + burnout), burnout (general overload), and
   countertransference (from the counselor's own history) are **distinct constructs with fuzzy edges**
   in the literature; the unit teaches the discriminations while noting the overlap. Self-care is an
   **ethical duty (ACA C.2.g)**, and prevention is **organizational, not only personal**. [S18][S19][S20][S21]
8. **Provenance caveats.** James & Gilliland and Herman are offline (books) and cited via reputable
   secondary summaries; the SAMHSA framework's exact wording was confirmed across two secondary
   summaries plus the primary publication record; the HPA-axis review (S14) and the Trippany/Kress
   counseling article (S19) were **read in full** via pypdf. Firearm case-fatality and crisis-duration
   figures are order-of-magnitude public-health estimates from Means Matter, not precise constants.
