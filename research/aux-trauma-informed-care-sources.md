# Sources — Elective module: Trauma-Informed Care

Evidence base for [`../wiki/aux-trauma-informed-care.md`](../wiki/aux-trauma-informed-care.md) and its
concept pages. Each entry has a one-line authority note. This is an **elective/ad-hoc module** (off the
12-unit syllabus spine; `aux-` namespace); it expands **[`concept-trauma-informed-care`](../wiki/concept-trauma-informed-care.md)**,
which is **retained in Unit 8** as the spine's overview page for a syllabus-named core concept (the
`concept-core-conditions` precedent). It borders **Unit 8**, **Unit 6** (PTSD), and **Unit 4**
(cultural/historical).

**The sourcing problem specific to this topic, stated up front.** Trauma-informed care is *policy* in
much of U.S. health care, education, and justice, and the literature around it is overwhelmingly
**advocacy and implementation guidance rather than outcome evidence**. Searching it returns training
vendors, state initiatives, and enthusiastic practice descriptions almost exclusively. That imbalance
is not incidental to the topic — it *is* one of the topic's findings [S28], and this file is
structured to keep the two apart:

- **Framework sources** (SAMHSA and successors) define what TIC *is*. They are authoritative for the
  framework's content and carry **no weight** as evidence that it works. SAMHSA's own documents are
  guidance, not trials, and do not claim otherwise.
- **Evidence sources** (AHRQ's systematic review, peer-reviewed critiques) establish what is actually
  known about effects. These are cited wherever the module makes a claim about outcomes.
- **Critique sources** are included deliberately and at the same tier as the framework sources, because
  a module that taught SAMHSA's six principles without the AHRQ finding would be teaching a consensus
  that the evidence does not support.

Two specific exclusions, noted so their absence is legible. A widely-repeated figure that **~70% of
psychiatric inpatients subjected to seclusion or restraint had childhood abuse histories** appears in
secondary sources tracing to a 2010 study; **the primary source could not be retrieved** (PubMed
returned a cookie wall), so the module states the association qualitatively and cites the retrievable
reviews [S4][S5] instead of quoting the number. Robert Anda's much-quoted line about his own
instrument is sourced to [S10], **consulted at abstract/summary level** — the quotation is
well-attested across independent secondary sources, and is flagged rather than presented as a
verified full-text read.

## The framework itself (authoritative for content, not for efficacy)

- **[S1] SAMHSA (2014). _SAMHSA's Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach_
  (HHS Pub. No. SMA 14-4884).**
  <https://library.samhsa.gov/product/samhsas-concept-trauma-and-guidance-trauma-informed-approach/sma14-4884>
  · <https://www.health.ny.gov/health_care/medicaid/program/medicaid_health_homes/docs/samhsa_trauma_concept_paper.pdf>
  — *The foundational U.S. document and the source of everything people mean by "trauma-informed."
  Produced by SAMHSA's Trauma and Justice Strategic Initiative. Source for the **three E's** (Event,
  Experience, Effects), the **four R's** (Realize, Recognize, Respond, Resist re-traumatization), the
  **six principles**, and — the part Unit 8's overview page does not carry — the **ten implementation
  domains**. Guidance document: it defines and recommends, and makes no efficacy claim.*

- **[S2] SAMHSA (2014). _TIP 57: Trauma-Informed Care in Behavioral Health Services_ (Treatment
  Improvement Protocol Series 57). Full text on NCBI Bookshelf.**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK207201/> ·
  <https://library.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/sma14-4816.pdf>
  — *The long-form clinical companion to [S1], and the most detailed U.S. practice guidance that
  exists for behavioral health specifically. Source for the **retraumatization** material, the
  screening-and-assessment chapter, trauma-informed treatment objectives, and the workforce-development
  material. Where this module makes a claim about what a clinician should *do*, TIP 57 is usually the
  authority. Note its age — 2014 — relative to the evidence reviews in [S28].*

- **[S3] SAMHSA (2023). _Practical Guide for Implementing a Trauma-Informed Approach_
  (PEP23-06-05-005).**
  <https://library.samhsa.gov/product/practical-guide-implementing-trauma-informed-approach/pep23-06-05-005>
  — *SAMHSA's own update, nine years on, aimed at operationalizing [S1]'s domains. Useful as evidence
  that the framework's authors themselves judged the original guidance insufficiently actionable.*

## Retraumatization

- **[S4] Cusack P, Cusack FP, McAndrew S, McKeown M, Duxbury J (2018). "An integrative review exploring
  the physical and psychological harm inherent in using restraint in mental health inpatient settings."
  _International Journal of Mental Health Nursing_ 27(3):1162–1176.**
  <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/inm.12432>
  — *Peer-reviewed integrative review. Source for the finding that restraint produces physical and
  psychological harm including humiliation and retraumatization, and that coercive practice is
  associated with patients feeling powerless and disbelieved. The empirical spine of the claim that
  re-traumatization is a real, documented harm rather than a theoretical risk.*

- **[S5] Qualitative synthesis of patients' experiences of re-traumatization in acute mental health
  inpatient settings (2022).**
  <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366322367>
  — *Peer-reviewed qualitative synthesis of patient accounts. Corroborates [S4] from the service-user
  perspective. **Abstract-level consultation; full text not retrieved.***

- **[S6] Psychiatric Services — "Toward the Cessation of Seclusion and Mechanical Restraint Use in
  Psychiatric Hospitals: A Call for Regulatory Action."**
  <https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.202100538>
  — *APA's health-services journal. Cited for the professional-consensus direction of travel on
  seclusion and restraint, and as the clearest example of a trauma-informed principle (avoid
  re-enacting powerlessness) translating into a concrete, contested policy demand.*

## The ACE study and its misuse

- **[S7] Felitti VJ, Anda RF, Nordenberg D, Williamson DF, Spitz AM, Edwards V, Koss MP, Marks JS
  (1998). "Relationship of childhood abuse and household dysfunction to many of the leading causes of
  death in adults: The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Study." _American Journal of Preventive
  Medicine_ 14(4):245–258.**
  <https://juvenilecouncil.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh301/files/media/document/ace_studygold_intolead_dr_felitti.pdf>
  — *The origin, and one of the most-cited papers in public health. The landmark finding is the
  **graded dose-response relationship** between the number of adversity categories and adult health
  risk — not any single association.*

- **[S8] CDC — _About the CDC-Kaiser ACE Study._**
  <https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/aces/about.html>
  — *The federal agency's own account of the study it co-ran. Source for the design (**over 17,000**
  Kaiser Permanente HMO members in Southern California, two waves, **1995–1997**), the **ten ACE
  categories** in three groups — abuse (emotional, physical, sexual); household challenges (mother
  treated violently, household substance abuse, household mental illness, parental separation/divorce,
  incarcerated household member); neglect (emotional, physical) — and the score distribution:
  **36.1% zero ACEs, 26% one, 15.9% two, 9.5% three, 12.5% four or more**, with nearly two-thirds
  reporting at least one.*

- **[S9] Anda RF, Porter LE, Brown DW (2020). "Inside the Adverse Childhood Experience Score:
  Strengths, Limitations, and Misapplications." _American Journal of Preventive Medicine_
  59(2):293–295.**
  <https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797(20)30058-1/fulltext>
  — *The single most important source in this module, because it is **the original investigator
  disowning the instrument's clinical use**. Anda writes that the ACE questionnaire was designed to
  **research — not screen** — and that the ACE score **"is neither a diagnostic tool nor is it
  predictive at the individual level."** Also the reason: the score is an unweighted count that
  ignores frequency, severity, duration, and developmental timing, so two people scoring 4 may have
  had entirely different exposures. **Consulted at abstract/summary level; the quoted language is
  well-attested across independent secondary sources but was not read in full text.***

- **[S10] Baldwin JR, Caspi A, Meehan AJ, et al. (2021). "Population vs Individual Prediction of Poor
  Health From Results of Adverse Childhood Experiences Screening." _JAMA Pediatrics_; and "Poor
  Individual Risk Classification From Adverse Childhood Experiences Screening," _AJPM_ (2021).**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7835926/> ·
  <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749379721004566>
  — *The empirical test of [S9]'s warning, and the reason this module treats individual ACE screening
  as a measurement error rather than a preference. ACE scores predict **mean group differences** in
  health outcomes but have **low accuracy in identifying which individuals** will have poor health —
  a population-level instrument being read as a personal risk score.*

- **[S11] National Child Traumatic Stress Network — _Beyond the ACE Score: Perspectives from the NCTSN
  on Child Trauma and Adversity Screening and Impact._**
  <https://www.nctsn.org/sites/default/files/resources/special-resource/beyond-the-ace-score-perspectives-from-the-nctsn-on-child-tauma-and-adversity-screening-and-impact.pdf>
  — *The SAMHSA-funded national child-trauma network's position. Source for what the ACE score
  **omits**: frequency, severity, duration, and developmental timing of exposure, plus whole
  categories of adversity — **traumatic bereavement, medical trauma, natural disasters, racial trauma,
  community violence**. And the operative clinical conclusion: an ACE score gives **insufficient
  information for treatment decision-making**; comprehensive assessment is required to determine
  whether a child has traumatic stress.*

- **[S12] American College of Preventive Medicine — "Recommendations for Population-Based Applications
  of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Study: Position Statement."**
  <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2773065422000372>
  — *A medical specialty society's formal position, included because it converges independently with
  [S9]–[S11]: ACEs belong in **population-level** prevention work. Corroborates that the
  research-instrument-vs-screening-tool distinction is a professional consensus, not one critic's view.*

- **[S13] Critiques of ACEs as deterministic labelling — sector debate summary; California's universal
  ACEs screening program and its critics (_The Imprint_).**
  <https://www.plinth.org.uk/debates/adverse-childhood-experiences> ·
  <https://imprintnews.org/child-welfare-2/painful-questions-critics-question-california-universal-aces-screening/46670>
  — *The practice-level objection, distinct from the psychometric one: that ACE framing **oversimplifies
  complex lives, creates deterministic narratives about children's futures, pathologizes poverty and
  structural disadvantage,** and can embed a deficit-based stance. The Imprint reporting covers
  California's ACEs Aware initiative — the largest real-world universal ACE screening program — and
  the clinician objections to it. **Journalism and sector-commentary tier**: used for the shape of the
  objection and the existence of the controversy, not for empirical claims.*

## Screening and assessment

- **[S14] U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs / ISTSS — _Primary Care PTSD Screen for DSM-5
  (PC-PTSD-5)._**
  <https://istss.org/clinical-resources/adult-trauma-assessments/primary-care-ptsd-screen-for-dsm-5/>
  — *A validated brief screen, and the contrast case to the ACE score: it has a **defined purpose,
  validation, and cut points.** Structure — an initial trauma-exposure item, then five yes/no symptom
  items if endorsed; **3+ is the optimally sensitive cut point** (minimizing false negatives), with
  **4+ suggesting probable PTSD warranting further evaluation**, and evidence that a lower cut point
  may suit women in VA primary care. A screen, not a diagnosis.*

- **[S15] VA National Center for PTSD — _Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5)._**
  <https://novopsych.com/assessments/diagnosis/life-events-checklist-5-lec-5/>
  — *Trauma-**exposure** inventory, deliberately distinguished from symptom measures. Source for the
  design caveat that matters clinically: the LEC-5 screens for potentially traumatic events and has
  **no formal scoring protocol** — it does not establish that exposure met DSM-5 Criterion A, and
  identifying exposure says nothing on its own about whether the person has traumatic stress.*

## Implementation, and the continuum

- **[S16] SAMHSA's ten implementation domains (from [S1]); operationalized by Trauma Informed Oregon's
  implementation tool.**
  <https://traumainformedoregon.org/implementation/tic-implementation-tool/> ·
  <https://traumainformedcaretraining.com/tic-in-organizations-ten-implementation-domains/>
  — *The ten domains: **governance and leadership; policy; physical environment; engagement and
  involvement; cross-sector collaboration; screening, assessment and treatment services; training and
  workforce development; progress monitoring and quality assurance; financing; evaluation.** The
  reason this module insists TIC is an organizational-change framework and not a clinical technique:
  eight of the ten are things an individual counselor cannot do alone.*

- **[S17] Australian Institute of Family Studies — _Trauma-informed care in child/family welfare
  services_; Wisconsin Continuum of Trauma-Informed Care (2017).**
  <https://aifs.gov.au/resources/policy-and-practice-papers/trauma-informed-care-childfamily-welfare-services>
  · <https://children.wi.gov/Documents/TICWisconsinModel2017.pdf>
  — *AIFS is Australia's federal family-research agency. Source for the **continuum** —
  trauma-**aware** → trauma-**sensitive** → trauma-**responsive** → trauma-**informed** /
  trauma-**specific** — and for the distinction this module leans on: trauma-informed and
  trauma-responsive describe **organizational philosophy and practice**, while **trauma-specific**
  denotes clinical treatments delivered within them. Note that the terminology is **not standardized
  across sources** — which is itself a finding, and one [S28] flags as a research problem.*

- **[S18] American Institutes for Research (2014). _Trauma-Informed Care and Trauma-Specific Services:
  A Comprehensive Approach._**
  <https://www.air.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Trauma-Informed%20Care%20White%20Paper_October%202014.pdf>
  — *Independent research-organization white paper on the informed-vs-specific distinction at system
  level. Corroborates [S17] from a U.S. vantage.*

## Historical, intergenerational, and racial trauma

- **[S19] Brave Heart MYH — historical trauma and historical unresolved grief; Indian Health Service
  training materials.**
  <https://www.ihs.gov/sites/telebehavioral/themes/responsive2017/display_objects/documents/slides/historicaltrauma/htreturnsacredpath0513.pdf>
  · <http://materials.ndrn.org/virtual20/session28/Building%20Relationships/Historical%20Trauma,%20Brave%20Heart.pdf>
  — *Maria Yellow Horse Brave Heart (Hunkpapa/Oglala Lakota) introduced **historical trauma** to the
  mental health literature in the mid-1990s, defining it as **cumulative emotional and psychological
  wounding across the lifespan and across generations**, emerging from massive group trauma. Source
  also for **historical unresolved grief** and the "**soul wound**" framing, and for the clinically
  important claim that locating suffering in collective history **reduces stigma and isolation** for
  the individual. IHS is the federal health agency for tribal nations.*

- **[S20] Carter RT (2007). "Racism and Psychological and Emotional Injury: Recognizing and Assessing
  Race-Based Traumatic Stress." _The Counseling Psychologist_ 35(1):13–105.**
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0011000006292033>
  — *Published in **counseling psychology's** flagship journal, which is why it belongs in this
  curriculum specifically. Carter's model treats racial discrimination experienced as overwhelming or
  beyond one's coping as producing responses resembling posttraumatic stress — and frames it as an
  **emotional injury rather than a pathology**, operating at interpersonal, institutional, and
  cultural levels. The content that fills in SAMHSA's otherwise thin sixth principle.*

- **[S21] VA National Center for PTSD — _PTSD Research Quarterly_: "Posttraumatic Stress Disorder and
  Racial Trauma" (Vol. 32, No. 1).**
  <https://www.ptsd.va.gov/publications/rq_docs/V32N1.pdf>
  — *Federal clinical-research review. Used as the corroborating, agency-level treatment of racial
  trauma alongside [S20], and for the open question of how race-based stress relates to the PTSD
  construct — which is unresolved, since DSM-5-TR Criterion A does not straightforwardly encompass
  discrimination.*

- **[S22] Yehuda R, Lehrner A (2018). "Intergenerational transmission of trauma effects: putative role
  of epigenetic mechanisms." _World Psychiatry_ 17(3):243–257; with the contrary appraisal in the
  2023 scoping review, "Biological Embedding of Early-Life Adversity and a Scoping Review of the
  Evidence for Intergenerational Epigenetic Transmission of Stress and Trauma in Humans."**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6127768/> ·
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10454883/>
  — *Both sides, cited together deliberately. Note the word **"putative"** in Yehuda's own title. The
  contested point is **mechanism**, not whether trauma effects appear across generations: whether
  *epigenetic* transmission in humans is established remains under debate, and the reviews stress how
  hard it is to separate **biological** transmission from **social** transmission (parenting,
  narrative, ongoing structural conditions) — which in most real cases is the more parsimonious
  explanation. Popular claims that ancestral trauma memory is "validated by scientists" outrun the
  evidence.*

- **[S23] Gone JP, et al. — "Indigenous Historical Trauma: Alter-Native Explanations for Mental Health
  Inequities." _Daedalus_.**
  <https://www.amacad.org/publication/daedalus/indigenous-historical-trauma-alter-native-explanations-mental-health-inequities>
  — *Critical scholarship on historical trauma **from within Indigenous mental health research** —
  included so the concept is taught with its own field's internal debate rather than as settled
  doctrine. Raises whether the construct is empirically tractable and whether framing collective
  suffering as inherited pathology can itself become a deficit narrative, competing with structural
  and political explanations of health inequity.*

## What is actually known about whether it works

- **[S24] Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (2025). _Trauma-Informed Care: A Systematic
  Review._ AHRQ Comparative Effectiveness Review; full text on NCBI Bookshelf.**
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK614502/>
  — ***The most important evidence source in this module, and the reason it is framed as it is.** AHRQ
  is the U.S. federal agency for health-services evidence synthesis and runs the most rigorous review
  process available. From **1,326 publications screened**, only **12 unique eligible studies** (across
  16 publications) were found. The verdict, in the review's own words: **"insufficient evidence on the
  effectiveness of current TIC approaches for reducing future or repeat trauma exposure; improving
  healthcare processes and utilization as well as policies and procedures; improving patient/client-
  related behavioral and psychosocial wellness as well as physical health; or effecting changes in
  patient/client harm."** Also the definitional finding: **"When looking deeper into the specific
  content of TIC domains…we found little consistency,"** and the review's Key Informants
  **"acknowledged debate around what TIC contributes beyond what many generally recognize as 'good
  care.'"** Finally, the implementation gap: **"TIC is being widely implemented; yet research on TIC's
  effectiveness and potential harms does not appear to be informing nor keeping pace with related
  areas."** Note "insufficient evidence" means **not established either way** — it is not a finding
  that TIC does not work.*

- **[S25] _American Family Physician_ (2025) — editorial: "Trauma-Informed Care: Evidence and Pragmatic
  Approaches."**
  <https://www.aafp.org/afp/2025/1100/editorials-trauma-informed-care>
  — *A clinical-specialty response to [S24], and the model for how this module holds the tension:
  acknowledging thin outcome evidence while arguing TIC remains defensible on grounds of low risk,
  ethical coherence, and consistency with what is independently known about trauma. The
  **"implementation outpacing evidence"** framing comes from here.*

- **[S26] Mixed-methods systematic review of trauma-informed organizational change interventions in
  primary and community mental health care (protocol and preprint).**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7896604/> ·
  <https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.07.09.22277443.full.pdf>
  — *Independent review effort converging with [S24] on the core problem: **no universally consistent
  operationalization** of TIC in the literature, which makes the body of work resistant to synthesis.
  **Preprint and protocol tier** — cited for the methodological point, not for outcome findings.*
