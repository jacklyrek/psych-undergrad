# Unit 5 sources — Human Development Across the Lifespan

Evidence base for Unit 5 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S1]`.

_Researched 2026-07-17. Anchored on **peer-reviewed clinical references** (NCBI Bookshelf StatPearls
for Erikson and Piaget; a *Development and Psychopathology* framework review for attachment; the
open-access *Biopsychosocial Model of Health and Disease* volume for Engel), triangulated with the
**primary methodological papers** (van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg's cross-cultural meta-analysis;
Main & Solomon's disorganized category; Bartholomew & Horowitz / Hazan & Shaver / the AAI for adult
attachment) and professional-body guidance (AAP developmental surveillance). Orientation-tier
teaching summaries (Simply Psychology, LibreTexts) are used only to corroborate the standard reading
of the primaries, and flagged as such. **Anchor text (not online):** Broderick & Blewitt, *The Life
Span* (written for helping professions) — used as the structural model for how this material is
taught to counselors; every substantive claim here is corroborated online._

## Tier: peer-reviewed clinical references & primary theory (ground truth)

- **[S1] Orenstein, G. A., & Lewis, L. (updated 2022). *Erikson's Stages of Psychosocial Development.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK556096.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556096/ (table: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556096/table/article-21284.table0/)
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (NCBI Bookshelf). Authority: high for the model as taught clinically._
  Eight sequential psychosocial stages across the lifespan, each a tension between a positive
  (**syntonic**) and negative (**dystonic**) tendency; navigating the two yields an **ego
  virtue/strength**. Built on Freud's psychosexual stages but adds social dynamics and extends into
  adulthood. Table (stage → conflict → virtue): Infancy **Trust vs. Mistrust → Hope**; Early
  Childhood **Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt → Will**; Play Age **Initiative vs. Guilt → Purpose**; School
  Age **Industry vs. Inferiority → Competence**; Adolescence **Identity vs. Role/Identity Confusion →
  Fidelity**; Young Adulthood **Intimacy vs. Isolation → Love**; Adulthood **Generativity vs.
  Stagnation → Care**; Old Age **Integrity vs. Despair → Wisdom**. Resolution of a stage is **not
  required to move to the next**; the later stages partly repeat earlier ones (development is
  "ever-evolving," not rigid). Joan Erikson later added a tentative **ninth stage** (very old age).

- **[S2] Malik, F., & Marwaha, R. (updated 2022). *Cognitive Development.* StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf, NBK537095.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537095/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (NCBI Bookshelf). Authority: high for Piaget as taught clinically._
  Piaget's engine: **schema** (mental structure), **assimilation** (fitting new information into an
  existing schema) and **accommodation** (revising the schema when it doesn't fit), driven toward
  **equilibration**. Four stages: **Sensorimotor** (0–2; knowledge through sensory/motor action;
  **object permanence** emerges ~6 months = objects exist when out of sight); **Pre-operational**
  (2–7; symbols and language, but **egocentrism** — inability to perceive that others think
  differently — and no conservation); **Concrete operational** (7–11; logical operations on concrete
  material, **conservation**, reversibility, inductive logic); **Formal operational** (12+; abstract
  and hypothetical-deductive reasoning — theories, "love," "justice"). Stated limitations:
  underestimates infants and **overestimates adolescents**; **theory of mind is mastered by ~4–5
  years**, earlier than Piaget's egocentrism timeline; stages are "poorly generalizable" and
  conservation overlaps stages; Piaget neglected **social and cultural** contributions (the Vygotsky
  critique).

- **[S3] Jones-Mason, Cassidy, et al. (2015). *Contributions of Attachment Theory and Research: A Framework for Future Research, Translation, and Policy.* Development and Psychopathology, 25(4pt2). PMC4085672.**
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4085672/
  _Peer-reviewed review by senior attachment researchers. Authority: high — the load-bearing source for the clinical/developmental claims._
  Attachment = "an organized system of behaviors that has a predictable outcome (i.e., proximity)
  and serves an identifiable biological function (i.e., protection)." The **secure base** (a base to
  explore from) and **safe haven** (a haven to return to in distress) are "the heart of attachment
  theory." Early experience builds **internal working models** / a **secure base script** ("when I
  am hurt, I go to my mother and receive comfort"); secure people hold coherent narratives, insecure
  people show gaps/distortions. **Risk, not destiny:** following Waddington's developmental-pathways
  model, early attachment is "not expected to be perfectly predictive of later outcomes"; insecurity
  "is thought to be a risk factor for later psychopathology **if** subsequent development occurs in
  the context of other risk factors." **Intergenerational transmission:** a parent's AAI state of
  mind predicts the infant's attachment pattern, but the **"transmission gap"** — maternal sensitive
  behavior does not fully explain that link — is one of the field's central unsolved problems.
  Bowlby characterized the **therapist as an attachment figure** in long-term psychotherapy.

- **[S4] Bolton, D., & Gillett, G. (2019). *The Biopsychosocial Model 40 Years On.* In* The Biopsychosocial Model of Health and Disease *(open access). NCBI Bookshelf, NBK552030.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK552030/
  _Open-access scholarly monograph chapter. Authority: high — a careful, criticism-aware treatment._
  Engel's model rejected the biomedical model's **reductionism** (complex illness reduced to one
  physical primary principle), **physicalism**, and **mind–body dualism**; it drew on **general
  systems theory** — nested, interacting biological/psychological/social domains that reciprocally
  influence health. Legitimized psychosocial intervention alongside medication and multidisciplinary
  care. **Criticisms:** Ghaemi — it "gives mental health professionals permission to do everything
  but no specific guidance," ideology masquerading as theory; Kendler — "never … a scientific model
  … It was a slogan," though useful as a teaching tool; the "equal in all cases" reading contradicts
  cases where biology alone explains outcomes. **Revival:** locate the model in *specific conditions
  and stages* (social factors may dominate aetiology, biology the acute treatment) and treat its job
  as theorizing the *interactions* between domains, not asserting equal weight everywhere.

- **[S5] Engel, G. L. (1977). *The Need for a New Medical Model: A Challenge for Biomedicine.* Science, 196(4286), 129–136.**
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/847460/
  _The primary paper that introduced the term (paywalled full text; content corroborated via [S4]). Authority: highest for what Engel actually proposed._
  Argued the dominant biomedical model was reductionist and dualist and left "no room within its
  framework for the social, psychological, and behavioral dimensions of illness"; proposed a
  systems-based model spanning biological, psychological, and social levels.

## Tier: primary attachment-methodology papers (lean on these)

- **[S6] van IJzendoorn, M. H., & Kroonenberg, P. M. (1988). *Cross-Cultural Patterns of Attachment: A Meta-Analysis of the Strange Situation.* Child Development, 59(1), 147–156.**
  https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=3437369 (corroborated: https://www.simplypsychology.org/culture-attachment.html)
  _The canonical cross-cultural meta-analysis (32 studies, 8 countries, ~2,000 classifications). Authority: high._
  **Secure was the most common classification in every country sampled** (roughly 50–75%);
  **intra-cultural variation was ~1.5× the inter-cultural variation** — differences *within* a
  country exceeded differences *between* countries. Caveats that puncture over-strong universality
  claims: **18 of 32 studies were US samples** and only a handful were in collectivist cultures; the
  Strange Situation may be an **"imposed etic"** — behaviors can look identical across cultures yet
  mean different things (e.g., German independence read as avoidance; Japanese distress at
  separation read as resistance because infants are rarely left alone).

- **[S7] Main, M., & Solomon, J. (1990). *Procedures for identifying infants as disorganized/disoriented during the Ainsworth Strange Situation.* In Greenberg et al. (Eds.), Attachment in the Preschool Years. (+ disorganized-as-risk meta-analyses, Development and Psychopathology.)**
  https://www.child-encyclopedia.com/attachment/according-experts/disorganization-attachment-strategies-infancy-and-childhood (corroboration/orientation)
  _The primary source for the fourth (D) category (1980s conference work, 1990 chapter); disorganized-risk claims corroborated via peer-reviewed meta-analyses. Authority: high for the D category; risk claims held probabilistically._
  Added the **disorganized/disoriented (D)** classification for infants who show **contradictory,
  incomplete, or fearful behavior** on reunion (freezing, approaching then backing away, dazed/
  trance-like states) — a **breakdown of an organized strategy**, typically where the caregiver is
  both the source of and the solution to alarm ("fright without solution"; associated with
  frightening/frightened parenting, abuse, or unresolved parental loss/trauma). Disorganization is
  the classification most robustly linked to later problems — **a broad, transdiagnostic relational
  risk factor** for internalizing and (especially) externalizing problems, dissociation, and later
  borderline features — but the link is **probabilistic and moderated by later context**, not
  deterministic.

- **[S8] Adult attachment — Hazan & Shaver (1987); Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991); George, Kaplan & Main (Adult Attachment Interview).**
  https://openbooks.library.baylor.edu/lifespanhumandevelopment/chapter/chapter-17-2-attachment-in-adulthood/ · https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/foc.8.1.foc127
  _Open textbook + a peer-reviewed clinical review (Focus/APA) on adult attachment and psychotherapy. Authority: high for the adult framework._
  **Hazan & Shaver (1987):** first self-report of adult *romantic* attachment, translating Ainsworth's
  three infant patterns into three adult styles. **Bartholomew & Horowitz (1991):** four-category
  model built on two dimensions — **anxiety** (about abandonment/self-worth) and **avoidance** (of
  intimacy/dependence): **secure** (low/low), **preoccupied** (high anxiety/low avoidance),
  **dismissing** (low anxiety/high avoidance), **fearful** (high/high). **AAI** (George, Kaplan &
  Main): assesses an adult's *state of mind* with respect to attachment — secure/autonomous,
  dismissing, preoccupied, unresolved/disorganized — from narrative coherence, not romantic
  self-report. Clinical: a client's pattern shows up in the alliance (preoccupied clients may seek
  reassurance and merge; dismissing clients may minimize and keep distance); the relationship can be
  a corrective attachment experience.

## Tier: professional-body & orientation-tier corroboration (context)

- **[S9] Simply Psychology — *Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation* (and *Cultural Variations in Attachment*).**
  https://www.simplypsychology.org/mary-ainsworth.html
  _Orientation-tier teaching summary; every load-bearing claim anchored to [S3][S6][S7]. Authority: orientation only._
  Standard presentation of the **eight 3-minute episodes**, the five scored behaviors (proximity/
  contact seeking, contact maintaining, avoidance, resistance, search), and the **original-sample
  proportions** (~**70% secure, 15% avoidant, 15% resistant**, from a small US middle-class Baltimore
  sample). **Caregiver correlates:** sensitive, consistent responsiveness → secure; consistently
  unresponsive/rejecting-of-distress → avoidant; **inconsistent** (sometimes responsive, sometimes
  not) → resistant/ambivalent. **Criticisms of the sensitivity hypothesis:** De Wolff & van
  IJzendoorn (1997) found only a **modest sensitivity–security correlation (r ≈ .24)**; **temperament**
  accounts (Kagan) argue inborn temperament drives the classification; **interactionist** accounts
  (Belsky & Rovine) say temperament × caregiving; plus the cross-cultural bias in [S6].

- **[S10] Simply Psychology / LibreTexts — *John Bowlby* (four phases; monotropy; internal working model; maternal deprivation).**
  https://www.simplypsychology.org/stages-of-attachment-identified-by-john-bowlby-and-schaffer-emerson-1964.html · https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Courses/Pittsburg_State_University/The_Developing_Child/13:_Social_and_Emotional_Development_in_Infancy_and_Toddlerhood/13.05:_Attachment
  _Orientation-tier; anchored to [S3]. Authority: orientation only._
  Bowlby's **four phases of attachment development**: **pre-attachment** (birth–~6 wks; signals to
  any adult) → **attachment-in-the-making** (~6 wks–6–8 mo; prefers familiar people) → **clear-cut
  attachment** (~7–24 mo; specific primary figure, **separation anxiety** peaks) → **reciprocal /
  goal-corrected partnership** (24 mo+; understands the caregiver has separate goals). Attachment as
  an evolved survival system; **monotropy** (a bias toward one principal attachment figure), a
  **sensitive/critical period**, and the **internal working model** as the template early attachment
  leaves for later relationships.

- **[S11] American Academy of Pediatrics — *Developmental Surveillance and Screening* + Zubler et al. (2022), *Evidence-Informed Milestones for Developmental Surveillance Tools,* Pediatrics.**
  https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/developmental-surveillance-and-screening-patient-care/ · https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/149/3/e2021052138/184748/
  _Professional-body guidance + peer-reviewed milestone update. Authority: high for the typical-vs-clinical framing._
  **Developmental surveillance** is the longitudinal process of eliciting concerns, taking a
  milestone history, observing the child, and applying clinical judgment across well-child visits;
  milestones define **age-dependent population norms** so that failure to attain one calibrates the
  *level of concern*. Basis for distinguishing **normal-range variation** from a **red flag** that
  warrants screening/referral — the counselor's version of "you can't tell what's wrong if you don't
  know what's normal for the age."

## Points of disagreement / uncertainty found (surfaced in readings)

1. **Maternal sensitivity is real but not the whole story.** Ainsworth's sensitivity hypothesis is
   supported only at a **modest** effect size (De Wolff & van IJzendoorn r ≈ .24), and the
   **"transmission gap"** means parental state of mind (AAI) predicts infant attachment beyond what
   observed sensitivity explains. Temperament (Kagan) and interactionist accounts (Belsky & Rovine)
   are live alternatives. Don't teach "sensitive parenting → secure baby" as a clean causal law.
   [S3][S9]
2. **The Strange Situation may be an imposed etic.** Secure is modal everywhere and within-culture
   variation exceeds between-culture variation [S6], but the procedure was built on US norms and its
   behaviors can *mean* different things across cultures (German "avoidance," Japanese "resistance").
   Attachment security may be universal in function while its behavioral markers are culture-bound.
   [S6][S9]
3. **Attachment is a risk factor, not a diagnosis or destiny.** Insecure and even disorganized
   attachment raise the probability of later problems only in the context of continuing risk; many
   insecurely attached children do fine, and later relationships (including therapy) can revise the
   internal working model. Resist typing a client by an attachment "category." [S3][S7]
4. **Erikson's stages are a clinical heuristic, not measured fact.** Ages are approximate and
   culture-bound (adolescent "identity" and the very existence of an extended adolescence are partly
   Western/modern), stages need not be resolved in order, and the theory is hard to falsify. Useful
   as a "where is this person stuck?" lens, not as a developmental law. [S1]
5. **Piaget under-credited infants and over-credited adolescents, and skipped culture.** Object
   permanence and theory of mind arrive earlier than he claimed; many adults don't reliably use
   formal-operational reasoning; and Vygotsky's sociocultural critique (development is scaffolded by
   social interaction and language, the "zone of proximal development") names what Piaget's
   solo-scientist child leaves out. Stages describe a real *sequence* better than they fix real
   *ages*. [S2]
6. **The biopsychosocial model is widely used and widely criticized at once.** It is the field's
   default case-formulation frame, yet critics (Ghaemi, Kendler) call it a vague "slogan" that
   licenses anything and specifies nothing. Hold it as an organizing checklist that must be made
   specific to *this* client — not as an explanation by itself. [S4][S5]
