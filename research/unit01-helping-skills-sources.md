# Unit 1 sources — Helping Skills & the Therapeutic Relationship

Evidence base for Unit 1 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S3]`.

_Researched 2026-06-27. Triangulated across professional bodies, peer-reviewed/open-access work,
NCBI/StatPearls, and a university course page. Lower-tier counselling-training sites used for
orientation/wording only and labeled as such._

## Tier: peer-reviewed / professional reference (lean on these)

- **[S1] StatPearls — *Person-Centered Therapy (Rogerian Therapy)*, NCBI Bookshelf.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK589708/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (NCBI). Authority: high._
  Key points: Three core conditions — **accurate empathy** ("the therapist conveys an accurate
  understanding of the patient's private world"), **congruence** (therapist transparently conveys
  feelings/thoughts to genuinely relate), **unconditional positive regard** (a warm environment
  conveying clients are accepted unconditionally). Rogers proposed **six** necessary-and-sufficient
  conditions for change (incl. psychological contact, client incongruence, and the client's
  *perception* of the therapist's empathy/UPR). Pioneered early 1940s; tied to the Human Potential
  Movement. Client problem = **incongruence** between self-image and experienced reality.
  *Limitations noted:* critics say the principles are "too vague," there's "a lack of controlled
  research on the efficacy," and it may be less suited to clients who struggle to talk about
  themselves. Evidence mixed: not inferior to CBT at 6 months for depression, possibly inferior at
  12 months; comparable for trauma with fewer dropouts.

- **[S2] Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold & Horvath (2018), *The Alliance in Adult Psychotherapy: A
  Meta-Analytic Synthesis*, Psychotherapy.** (Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy copy.)
  https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Fluckiger-et-al-2018.pdf
  _Definitive alliance meta-analysis. Authority: high (peer-reviewed)._
  Headline: alliance–outcome **r = .278** (95% CI [.256, .299]), **k = 295** independent studies,
  **>30,000 patients** (1978–2017), face-to-face and internet-based. One of the most robust and
  replicated predictors in psychotherapy research. (Association is correlational; direction/causation
  debated — alliance and improvement likely reinforce each other.)
  Cross-check: PMC summary confirms d ≈ 0.57 / r ≈ .28 figure across >30,000 clients.

- **[S3] Wampold (2015), *How important are the common factors in psychotherapy? An update*, World
  Psychiatry 14(3).** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.20238/full
  _Leading common-factors statement. Authority: high (peer-reviewed). NOTE: full text was paywalled
  on fetch (HTTP 402); claims below corroborated via search abstracts + secondary summaries — verify
  exact effect sizes against the primary before quoting numerically._
  Contextual Model: a treatment works when it has (a) a cogent rationale the client accepts, (b) is
  delivered by a clinician with whom the client has a therapeutic relationship, and (c) involves
  actions the client expects to help. Argues common factors (alliance, empathy, expectations,
  goal consensus) carry much of psychotherapy's effect — the relationship "seemingly outstripping"
  technique, though contemporary view is relationship and technique work together, not in opposition.

- **[S4] Therapeutic Alliance and Outcome of Psychotherapy: Historical Excursus, Measurements, and
  Prospects, PMC3198542.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198542/
  _Open-access review. Authority: high._
  Bordin (1979) reformulated alliance as **transtheoretical**, three components: **bond** (the
  affective relationship), **task** (agreement on the in-session activities/techniques), **goal**
  (agreement on the desired outcomes). Bordin held the alliance influences outcome not by being
  curative itself but as the ingredient that lets the client accept/follow/believe in the treatment.
  Led to measures: Working Alliance Inventory (WAI), Penn Helping Alliance Questionnaire.
  Since 2002 APA recognizes alliance as an empirically validated common factor.

## Tier: university / institutional (good for structure)

- **[S5] Penn State World Campus, RHS 301 — *Counseling Microskills*.**
  https://courses.worldcampus.psu.edu/welcome/rhs301/001/content/01_lesson/06_page.html
  _University course page. Authority: medium-high (.edu, teaching material)._
  Ivey **microskills hierarchy** (pyramid, base → top): foundation = Rogerian conditions
  (genuineness, empathy, UPR) + ethics + multicultural competence → **attending behavior** (eye
  contact, vocal quality, verbal tracking, body language) → **observation skills** (verbal/nonverbal
  mismatches) → **open & closed questions** → **reflecting/encouraging/paraphrasing/summarizing &
  reflection of feeling** → (higher) the five-stage interview → **influencing skills**
  (self-disclosure, confrontation, interpretation). Master lower skills before higher.

- **[S6] iResearchNet — *Allen Ivey* (History of Counseling).**
  https://psychology.iresearchnet.com/counseling-psychology/history-of-counseling/allen-ivey/
  _Reference encyclopedia. Authority: medium._
  Ivey developed microcounseling/microskills (~1967–1971) to isolate teachable units of helping
  behavior. Approach validated by 450+/600+ data-based studies per the publisher's claims (treat the
  exact count as marketing — directionally well-supported).

- **[S7] counseling.education — *Reflecting & Listening Skills*.**
  https://counseling.education/counseling/skills/reflecting.html
  _Counselor-educator teaching site. Authority: medium (orientation/wording)._
  **Reflecting content (paraphrase):** repeating back a version of what the client said (shows
  listening; weaker alone). **Reflecting feeling:** names the client's emotion, requires a wide
  feeling vocabulary matched to intensity. **Summarizing:** broadest — condenses several
  paraphrases/reflections, used at session start/end. **Interpretation:** going *beyond* what the
  client said — adding the counselor's read of meaning/needs (an influencing skill, higher up the
  hierarchy, more risk).

- **[S10] Duan, Wei & others / *A Comparison of Empathy and Sympathy Between Counselors-in-Training
  and Their Non-Counseling Academic Peers*, The Professional Counselor (NBCC journal).**
  https://tpcjournal.nbcc.org/a-comparison-of-empathy-and-sympathy-between-counselors-in-training-and-their-non-counseling-academic-peers/
  _Peer-reviewed counseling journal (National Board for Certified Counselors). Authority: high for the
  empathy/sympathy distinction specifically._
  **Empathy** has affective + cognitive components — experiencing *and* comprehending another's
  emotional state (Vossen, Piotrowski & Valkenburg, 2015). **Sympathy** "like empathy, involves an
  understanding of another's emotion and includes an emotional response, but it consists of feelings
  of **sorrow or concern** for the distressed or needy other rather than merely feeling the same
  emotion" (Eisenberg et al., 2010). Clinical stakes: Clark (2010) warns that **"the misapplication
  of sympathetic responses as empathic responses can interfere in the counseling relationship"** —
  clients need empathic, not sympathetic, responses.

## In-depth upgrade additions (researched 2026-07-19)

Sources added when Unit 1 was upgraded concise → in-depth. Same triangulation/authority rules.
Two anchor **books** (Carkhuff 1969; Egan 1975) are offline — corroborated via reputable secondary

### Tier: peer-reviewed / professional reference

- **[S15] Eubanks, Muran & Safran (2018), *Alliance Rupture Repair: A Meta-Analysis*, Psychotherapy
  55(4), 508–519.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335462/
  _Peer-reviewed meta-analysis (Div. 29 task force). Authority: high._
  A **rupture** = a deterioration in the alliance — disagreement on **goals**, lack of collaboration on
  **tasks**, or a strain in the **bond** (note: maps onto Bordin's three components). Two markers:
  **withdrawal** ruptures (client moves *away* — placating, denying, going silent, changing subject)
  and **confrontation** ruptures (client moves *against* — anger, complaints, pressure/control).
  Meta-analysis of rupture-repair vs. outcome: **r = .29** (k = 11, N = 1,314), a moderate positive
  relation; rupture-**resolution training** also improved outcomes. Foundational work: **Safran & Muran
  (2000), *Negotiating the Therapeutic Alliance*** — repairing a rupture may benefit the client *more*
  than an alliance with no ruptures.

- **[S16] Common-factors history — primary/secondary composite.**
  Rosenzweig (1936), *Some Implicit Common Factors in Diverse Methods of Psychotherapy*, Am. J.
  Orthopsychiatry — **coined "common factors"** and invoked the **Dodo bird** ("Everybody has won, and
  all must have prizes"). Frank & Frank (1961/1991), *Persuasion and Healing* — clients arrive
  **demoralized**; four shared healing components (an emotionally charged confiding relationship, a
  healing setting, a **rationale/myth**, and a **ritual/procedure**) + the **expectation of help**.
  Assay & Lambert (1999) — the widely-reproduced "pie": ~40% client/extratherapeutic, ~30%
  relationship, ~15% expectancy/placebo, ~15% technique. Wampold & Imel (2015), *The Great
  Psychotherapy Debate* (2nd ed.) — the modern Contextual-Model statement.
  Corroboration: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_factors_theory; simplypsychology.org dodo-bird page.
  _Authority: high for the primary claims (classic, heavily cited). **Caveat:** the Lambert
  percentages are an illustrative heuristic, **not** a formal variance decomposition — cite as
  "roughly," not as measured partition._

- **[S17] Montgomery et al. (2024), *The shifting sound of silence: A constructivist grounded theory*,
  Counselling and Psychotherapy Research (Wiley).**
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/capr.12654
  _Peer-reviewed. Authority: high (for silence typology)._
  **Therapeutic silence** falls into **productive, neutral, and obstructive** categories; productive
  silences give the client space to **access emotion, find expression, or reflect.** Silence is part of
  **attending**; it is most useful *after* a strong alliance and when a client is processing intense
  feeling. Cross-check: positivepsychology.com/silence-in-therapy; counselingschools.com. Beginners
  over-fill silence — holding it is a trained skill.

### Tier: institutional / teaching (structure & wording; classics corroborated across ≥2)

- **[S11] Carkhuff's five-level empathy scale (Carkhuff, 1969, *Helping and Human Relations*; from
  Truax & Carkhuff, 1967).** Offline book → corroborated across:
  counsellingcentral.com/understanding-empathy-a-deep-dive-into-robert-carkhuffs-empathy-scale/;
  CounsellingTutor "Carkhuff Rating Scales Explained" (PDF); sites.google.com empathy-training-lit-review
  (Carkhuff 1969). _Authority: medium-high — a **classic, widely-published training rubric** in
  counselor education._
  Revised from an earlier 9-point to a **5-point** scale, the **first to operationalize empathy into
  observable helper behaviors** for training. **L1** non-empathic (detached/dismissive; ignores or
  misreads feeling). **L2** subtractive (partial/superficial; subtracts noticeably from affect).
  **L3 interchangeable** — accurately reflects the client's expressed content *and* feeling, no more,
  no less; the **minimally facilitative baseline** for effective helping. **L4 additive/skilled** —
  adds to and clarifies feelings the client was reaching for. **L5 additive/highly skilled** — surfaces
  emotion not yet articulated; deepens self-awareness. (Levels 1–2 subtract, 3 is interchangeable, 4–5
  add.)

- **[S12] Carl Rogers — biography & self-theory. Encyclopedia.com, *Carl Ransom Rogers*** (Gale
  reference). https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/medicine/psychology-and-psychiatry-biographies/carl-ransom-rogers
  _Reference-encyclopedia biography. Authority: medium-high._
  **1902–1987.** Method arc: **non-directive** (1942) → **client-centered** (1951, *Client-Centered
  Therapy* — the **19 propositions** / self-theory of personality; the phenomenological field) →
  **person-centered** (extended to education, groups, conflict). **Actualizing tendency** (innate growth
  drive — "violets through concrete"). **1957** "necessary and sufficient conditions" paper. **1982**
  APA survey: ranked **#1 most influential psychotherapist** in America. Later **peace/cross-cultural**
  work (Belfast, South Africa, Soviet Union; Rust workshop); widely reported to have been **nominated
  for the Nobel Peace Prize (1987)**. Criticisms: **vagueness/oversimplification**; the "organismic
  trusting" / "if it feels right it is right" objection; reflection reduced to parody; Western
  individualism. **Wisconsin schizophrenia project** (Rogers, Gendlin, Kiesler & Truax; NIMH-funded;
  Mendota State Hospital / Univ. of Wisconsin) published as **Rogers et al. (1967), *The Therapeutic
  Relationship and Its Impact: A Study of Psychotherapy with Schizophrenics*** — long read as a
  **disappointment** (the conditions did **not** produce dramatic gains with hospitalized patients, and
  the project was marred by an internal data dispute), though it showed **modest** positive outcomes,
  especially when patients were more "in contact." Wisconsin corroboration: focusing.org/gendlin;
  Univ. of Wisconsin Press 1967 record.

- **[S13] Conditions of worth & the organismic valuing process.** explorepsychology.com,
  *What Is the Organismic Valuing Process?*; counsellingtutor.com *Organismic Self*;
  positivepsychology.com *Rogers' Actualizing Tendency*. _Authority: medium (orientation); the concepts
  trace to **Rogers (1959), "A Theory of Therapy, Personality, and Interpersonal Relationships."**_
  **Organismic valuing process** = an innate internal compass rating experience by whether it serves
  actualization. **Conditions of worth** = when significant others give **conditional** positive regard,
  the child internalizes their standards and starts valuing experience by *others'* criteria — creating
  **incongruence** between the self-concept and organismic experience (the engine of distress that UPR
  is meant to reverse).

- **[S14] Egan's Skilled Helper & SOLER.** Gerard Egan, *The Skilled Helper* (1975), Loyola Univ.
  Chicago — offline book → corroborated across counsellingtutor.com (Egan/SOLER + Skilled-Helper
  approach) and counsellingcentral.com/the-egan-model-and-soler/ and the Life Charity SOLER handout.
  _Authority: medium-high (a standard counselor-training text)._
  **SOLER** operationalizes attending as observable behaviors: **S**quarely face the client, **O**pen
  posture, **L**ean slightly in, **E**ye contact (without staring), **R**elax. The **Skilled Helper**
  three-stage map: **Stage 1 Current Picture** (what's going on?), **Stage 2 Preferred Picture** (what do
  I want instead?), **Stage 3 The Way Forward** (how do I get there?). **Caveat:** SOLER's norms (direct
  eye contact, square facing) are **culturally specific** — to be used flexibly, not as a rigid rule
  (see [[unit04-multicultural]]); Egan himself framed it as a guide.

## Open threads / where sources disagree or hedge
- **"Necessary and sufficient"** (Rogers, 1957): the strong claim that the six conditions are *all*
  that's needed is **contested** — most integrative clinicians treat the conditions as necessary but
  not sufficient. Flag in the reading.
- **Alliance causation:** the r≈.28 association is robust but **correlational**; whether a strong
  alliance *causes* improvement or partly reflects early improvement is debated. Don't overclaim.
- **Person-centered efficacy:** evidence is mixed vs. CBT (S1); good rapport ≠ proven superiority.
- **Microskills study counts** (450–600 studies): publisher figures; cite as "extensively studied,"
  not a hard number.
- **Lambert's percentages** (40/30/15/15): a memorable heuristic, **not** a formal variance
  decomposition — repeatedly reproduced but methodologically contested. Present as "roughly," and note
  the debate about whether common factors can even be cleanly separated from technique (S16).
- **Carkhuff / Egan anchors offline:** the empathy-scale (S11) and SOLER/Skilled-Helper (S14) primary
  books weren't fetchable; both are classics corroborated across ≥2 independent teaching sources, but
  exact level-wording and stage-wording should be verified against the primary before clinical use.
- **SOLER is culturally loaded:** direct eye contact / square facing read as respectful in dominant
  US-white norms but intrusive elsewhere — the Unit 4 material complicates it; the reading flags it.
- **Rogers Nobel nomination (1987):** widely reported but nomination records are sealed 50 years —
  stated as "reportedly nominated," not as verified fact.
- **Wisconsin project:** honest read is "landmark that tempered the *sufficient* claim" — modest, not
  null, results; don't caricature it as a clean failure or inflate it as a success (S12).
