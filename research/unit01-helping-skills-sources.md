# Unit 1 sources — Helping Skills & the Therapeutic Relationship

Evidence base for Unit 1 readings. Each source has a one-line authority note. Inline citations in
wiki pages reference these numbers, e.g. `[S3]`.

_Researched 2026-06-27. Triangulated across professional bodies, peer-reviewed/open-access work,

## Tier: peer-reviewed / professional reference (lean on these)

- **[S1] StatPearls — *Person-Centered Therapy (Rogerian Therapy)*, NCBI Bookshelf.**
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK589708/
  _Peer-reviewed clinical reference (NCBI). Authority: high._
  Key points: Three core conditions — **accurate empathy** ("the therapist conveys an accurate
  understanding of the patient's private world"), **congruence** (therapist transparently conveys
  feelings/thoughts to genuinely relate), **unconditional positive regard** (a warm environment
  conveying clients are accepted unconditionally). Rogers proposed **six** necessary-and-sufficient
  conditions for change (incl. psychological contact, client incongruence, and the client's
  *perception* of the therapist's empathy/UPR). Pioneered early 1940s; tied to the Human Potential
  Movement. Client problem = **incongruence** between self-image and experienced reality.
  *Limitations noted:* critics say the principles are "too vague," there's "a lack of controlled
  research on the efficacy," and it may be less suited to clients who struggle to talk about
  themselves. Evidence mixed: not inferior to CBT at 6 months for depression, possibly inferior at
  12 months; comparable for trauma with fewer dropouts.

- **[S2] Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold & Horvath (2018), *The Alliance in Adult Psychotherapy: A
  Meta-Analytic Synthesis*, Psychotherapy.** (Society for the Advancement of Psychotherapy copy.)
  https://societyforpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Fluckiger-et-al-2018.pdf
  _Definitive alliance meta-analysis. Authority: high (peer-reviewed)._
  Headline: alliance–outcome **r = .278** (95% CI [.256, .299]), **k = 295** independent studies,
  **>30,000 patients** (1978–2017), face-to-face and internet-based. One of the most robust and
  replicated predictors in psychotherapy research. (Association is correlational; direction/causation
  debated — alliance and improvement likely reinforce each other.)
  Cross-check: PMC summary confirms d ≈ 0.57 / r ≈ .28 figure across >30,000 clients.

- **[S3] Wampold (2015), *How important are the common factors in psychotherapy? An update*, World
  Psychiatry 14(3).** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wps.20238/full
  _Leading common-factors statement. Authority: high (peer-reviewed). NOTE: full text was paywalled
  on fetch (HTTP 402); claims below corroborated via search abstracts + secondary summaries — verify
  exact effect sizes against the primary before quoting numerically._
  Contextual Model: a treatment works when it has (a) a cogent rationale the client accepts, (b) is
  delivered by a clinician with whom the client has a therapeutic relationship, and (c) involves
  actions the client expects to help. Argues common factors (alliance, empathy, expectations,
  goal consensus) carry much of psychotherapy's effect — the relationship "seemingly outstripping"
  technique, though contemporary view is relationship and technique work together, not in opposition.

- **[S4] Therapeutic Alliance and Outcome of Psychotherapy: Historical Excursus, Measurements, and
  Prospects, PMC3198542.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198542/
  _Open-access review. Authority: high._
  Bordin (1979) reformulated alliance as **transtheoretical**, three components: **bond** (the
  affective relationship), **task** (agreement on the in-session activities/techniques), **goal**
  (agreement on the desired outcomes). Bordin held the alliance influences outcome not by being
  curative itself but as the ingredient that lets the client accept/follow/believe in the treatment.
  Led to measures: Working Alliance Inventory (WAI), Penn Helping Alliance Questionnaire.
  Since 2002 APA recognizes alliance as an empirically validated common factor.

## Tier: university / institutional (good for structure)

- **[S5] Penn State World Campus, RHS 301 — *Counseling Microskills*.**
  https://courses.worldcampus.psu.edu/welcome/rhs301/001/content/01_lesson/06_page.html
  _University course page. Authority: medium-high (.edu, teaching material)._
  Ivey **microskills hierarchy** (pyramid, base → top): foundation = Rogerian conditions
  (genuineness, empathy, UPR) + ethics + multicultural competence → **attending behavior** (eye
  contact, vocal quality, verbal tracking, body language) → **observation skills** (verbal/nonverbal
  mismatches) → **open & closed questions** → **reflecting/encouraging/paraphrasing/summarizing &
  reflection of feeling** → (higher) the five-stage interview → **influencing skills**
  (self-disclosure, confrontation, interpretation). Master lower skills before higher.

- **[S6] iResearchNet — *Allen Ivey* (History of Counseling).**
  https://psychology.iresearchnet.com/counseling-psychology/history-of-counseling/allen-ivey/
  _Reference encyclopedia. Authority: medium._
  Ivey developed microcounseling/microskills (~1967–1971) to isolate teachable units of helping
  behavior. Approach validated by 450+/600+ data-based studies per the publisher's claims (treat the
  exact count as marketing — directionally well-supported).

- **[S7] counseling.education — *Reflecting & Listening Skills*.**
  https://counseling.education/counseling/skills/reflecting.html
  _Counselor-educator teaching site. Authority: medium (orientation/wording)._
  **Reflecting content (paraphrase):** repeating back a version of what the client said (shows
  listening; weaker alone). **Reflecting feeling:** names the client's emotion, requires a wide
  feeling vocabulary matched to intensity. **Summarizing:** broadest — condenses several
  paraphrases/reflections, used at session start/end. **Interpretation:** going *beyond* what the
  client said — adding the counselor's read of meaning/needs (an influencing skill, higher up the
  hierarchy, more risk).

- **[S10] Duan, Wei & others / *A Comparison of Empathy and Sympathy Between Counselors-in-Training
  and Their Non-Counseling Academic Peers*, The Professional Counselor (NBCC journal).**
  https://tpcjournal.nbcc.org/a-comparison-of-empathy-and-sympathy-between-counselors-in-training-and-their-non-counseling-academic-peers/
  _Peer-reviewed counseling journal (National Board for Certified Counselors). Authority: high for the
  empathy/sympathy distinction specifically._
  **Empathy** has affective + cognitive components — experiencing *and* comprehending another's
  emotional state (Vossen, Piotrowski & Valkenburg, 2015). **Sympathy** "like empathy, involves an
  understanding of another's emotion and includes an emotional response, but it consists of feelings
  of **sorrow or concern** for the distressed or needy other rather than merely feeling the same
  emotion" (Eisenberg et al., 2010). Clinical stakes: Clark (2010) warns that **"the misapplication
  of sympathetic responses as empathic responses can interfere in the counseling relationship"** —
  clients need empathic, not sympathetic, responses.

## Open threads / where sources disagree or hedge
- **"Necessary and sufficient"** (Rogers, 1957): the strong claim that the six conditions are *all*
  that's needed is **contested** — most integrative clinicians treat the conditions as necessary but
  not sufficient. Flag in the reading.
- **Alliance causation:** the r≈.28 association is robust but **correlational**; whether a strong
  alliance *causes* improvement or partly reflects early improvement is debated. Don't overclaim.
- **Person-centered efficacy:** evidence is mixed vs. CBT (S1); good rapport ≠ proven superiority.
- **Microskills study counts** (450–600 studies): publisher figures; cite as "extensively studied,"
  not a hard number.
