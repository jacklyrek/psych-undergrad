# Sources — Elective module: Supporting Survivors of Sexual Abuse & Assault

Evidence base for [`../wiki/aux-sexual-abuse-support.md`](../wiki/aux-sexual-abuse-support.md) and its
concept pages. Each entry has a one-line authority note. This is an **elective/ad-hoc module** (off the
12-unit syllabus spine; `aux-` namespace); it borders **Unit 8 (Crisis, Risk & Trauma-Informed Care)**,
**Unit 3 (mandated reporting / confidentiality limits)**, and **Unit 6 (PTSD and the trauma- and
stressor-related disorders)**.

This is the most evidentially treacherous topic in the repository. Three hazards shaped the sourcing:

1. **Advocacy numbers circulate detached from their studies.** Prevalence figures for sexual violence
   are widely quoted without the survey, year, or definition that produced them — and the definitions
   differ enormously (legal "rape" ≠ CDC "contact sexual violence" ≠ "sexual harassment"). Every number
   below is tied to the instrument that produced it. Where a commonly-cited figure could not be traced
   to its primary source, it is **excluded rather than repeated**.
2. **Parts of this field are actively litigated.** CSAAS admissibility, the recovered-memory debate, and
   Title IX's regulatory status are contested in courts and journals *right now*. These are flagged as
   contested in-line, with both sides cited, not smoothed into a consensus that does not exist.
3. **Some primary sources block automated access.** RAINN's statistics and guidance pages returned HTTP
   403 to retrieval and **were not read directly**; RAINN is therefore cited only as a *service/hotline
   resource* [S16], never as the authority for a statistic. The corresponding claims are sourced to CDC
   [S1][S3], BJS [S4], and peer-reviewed work instead. Where a source's *full text* was not consulted,
   the entry says so.

**Tiering note:** federal agencies (CDC, BJS, DOJ, VA), peer-reviewed journals, and diagnostic/legal
authorities carry the substantive claims. Advocacy and practitioner organizations (NSVRC, 1in6, CAASE,
SVRI, university SVPRO offices) are used for **practice guidance and service description** — what a
rape crisis center does, what a secondary survivor faces — and are marked as such. No claim about
mechanism, prevalence, or treatment efficacy rests on a non-peer-reviewed source.

## Scope, definitions, and prevalence

- **[S1] Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — _About Sexual Violence_ (Violence Prevention).**
  <https://www.cdc.gov/sexual-violence/about/index.html>
  — *The U.S. public-health agency's definitional page. Source for the operative definition — **"sexual
  activity when consent is not obtained or freely given"** — the category structure (contact sexual
  violence; verbal sexual harassment; technology-facilitated sexual violence; child sexual abuse), and
  the headline lifetime-prevalence figures: **nearly half of women** and **more than 1 in 6 men**
  experienced contact sexual violence; **more than 1 in 5 women** and **1 in 31 men** experienced
  completed or attempted rape; **1 in 26 men** were made to penetrate. Also the age-of-onset finding:
  **nearly 80% of female rape survivors were first victimized before age 25, almost half before 18.***

- **[S2] CDC — _National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS)_, program page and the
  2016/2017 report series.**
  <https://www.cdc.gov/nisvs/about/index.html> ·
  <https://www.cdc.gov/nisvs/documentation/NISVSReportonIPV_2022.pdf>
  — *The instrument behind nearly every U.S. sexual-violence prevalence number, including [S1]'s.
  Nationally representative random-digit-dial survey of non-institutionalized English/Spanish-speaking
  adults, launched 2010; most recent data collection **2016/2017**, reports issued from 2022. **Its
  design is the caveat that matters:** telephone-based self-report excludes institutionalized
  populations (prisons, some care settings) and under-18s, and the 2016/2017 sample was **too small to
  produce national estimates for transgender people** — a stated limitation, not an absence of harm.
  The program page itself carries no detailed statistics; the numbers come from the report PDFs.*

- **[S3] CDC — _About Child Sexual Abuse_ (Child Abuse and Neglect Prevention).**
  <https://www.cdc.gov/child-abuse-neglect/about/about-child-sexual-abuse.html>
  — *Source for the CSA definition — involvement of a person under 18 in sexual activity they cannot
  comprehend or consent to, explicitly **not requiring physical contact** — the prevalence estimate
  (**at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys**, which CDC itself says "likely underestimate the true
  impact"), the perpetrator finding (**about 90% known and trusted** by the child or family), and the
  revictimization figure: women with CSA histories face **two to 13 times increased risk** of sexual
  violence in adulthood.*

  > **Discrepancy noted, not smoothed:** the widely-circulated "1 in 4 girls, **1 in 13** boys" figure
  > comes from 2013–2014 self-report data, while CDC's current page says **1 in 20** boys. The module
  > cites CDC's current figure and flags that male CSA prevalence estimates are especially
  > definition-sensitive and especially likely to be depressed by non-disclosure [S20].

- **[S4] Bureau of Justice Statistics — _Criminal Victimization, 2023_ (NCJ 309544).**
  <https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cv23.pdf>
  — *The federal victimization survey, and the authority for reporting rates. **46% of rape/sexual
  assaults were reported to police in 2023** — the highest share since 2010 — with a **margin of error
  of ±8.2%**. The wide interval and year-to-year volatility are the reason this module quotes a range
  and a direction ("most go unreported; the share reported moves") rather than a precise fraction.
  NCVS measures a different construct than NISVS (crime-framed incidents vs. behaviorally-defined
  experiences), which is why their numbers do not reconcile — a definitional gap, not a contradiction.*

- **[S5] National Sexual Violence Resource Center — _New NISVS Data on Sexual Violence and Sexual
  Identity: Key Findings and Prevention Recommendations_.**
  <https://www.nsvrc.org/blog_post/new-nisvs-data-sexual-violence-and-sexual-identity-key-findings-and-prevention/>
  — *NSVRC is the federally-funded national clearinghouse for sexual-violence resources; this post
  summarizes CDC's own NISVS sexual-identity breakouts. Source for the orientation disparities:
  **4 in 5 bisexual women** experienced contact sexual violence and **nearly half were raped**;
  **about 3 in 5 lesbian women and gay men** experienced contact sexual violence. Secondary summary of
  a primary federal dataset — used for the disparity pattern, with the transgender-sample limitation
  from [S2] stated alongside it.*

## Child sexual abuse: grooming, disclosure, and the CSAAS problem

- **[S6] Winters GM, Jeglic EL, et al. (2024). "The prevalence of sexual grooming behaviors among
  survivors of childhood sexual abuse." _Child Abuse & Neglect_ 154:106842.**
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39059229/> ·
  PDF: <https://enoughabuse.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Winters-et-al.-2024.pdf>
  — *Peer-reviewed empirical study from the research group that built the Sexual Grooming Model.
  Source for the **five-stage grooming sequence** (victim selection; gaining access and isolating;
  developing trust with the child **and the protective adults**; desensitization to touch and sexual
  content; post-abuse maintenance of compliance and secrecy) and for the finding that **99% of adult
  CSA survivors endorsed at least one grooming behavior**, averaging **~14** behaviors each.*

- **[S7] Summit RC (1983). "The child sexual abuse accommodation syndrome." _Child Abuse & Neglect_
  7(2):177–193.**
  — *The origin of CSAAS and its five categories: **secrecy; helplessness; entrapment and
  accommodation; delayed, conflicted and unconvincing disclosure; retraction.** Historically
  important and clinically influential. **Explicitly a clinical description, not a validated
  diagnostic instrument** — Summit himself later objected to its use as proof of abuse. Cited here for
  what it says and for why its misuse matters; the critique is [S8][S9].*

- **[S8] Schmidt CW (2013). "Sex, Lies, and Statistics: Inferences From the Child Sexual Abuse
  Accommodation Syndrome." _Journal of the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law_ 41(3):412–420.**
  <https://jaapl.org/content/41/3/412>
  — *Peer-reviewed forensic-psychiatry critique. The load-bearing objection: CSAAS is **clinically
  derived and not falsifiable as applied** — because both disclosure and non-disclosure, both
  consistent accounts and recantation, are treated as compatible with abuse, it cannot discriminate
  abused from non-abused children and therefore **cannot be evidence that abuse occurred**.*

- **[S9] Shelton DE. "The Admissibility of 'Child Sexual Abuse Accommodation Syndrome' Testimony in
  Criminal Cases." SSRN.**
  <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5154015>
  — *Judicial-perspective review of CSAAS admissibility. Documents the appellate trend against CSAAS
  testimony, anchored by **_State v. J.L.G._ (N.J. 2018)**, in which the New Jersey Supreme Court held
  CSAAS testimony generally inadmissible as lacking scientific reliability — while carving out
  **delayed disclosure**, the one component with independent empirical support. **Abstract- and
  summary-level consultation; full text not read.** State law varies; treat the direction as the
  finding, not a nationwide rule.*

- **[S10] London K, Bruck M, Ceci SJ, Shuman DW (2005). "Disclosure of child sexual abuse: What does
  the research tell us about the ways that children tell?" _Psychology, Public Policy, and Law_
  11(1):194–226. See also the APSAC Handbook chapter on disclosure.**
  <https://depts.washington.edu/uwhatc/PDF/articles/APSAC%20Handbook%20chapter%20Disclosure.pdf>
  — *The standard empirical review, and the source of the field's central correction: **delayed and
  partial disclosure are common and well-supported**, but **recantation is not the norm** — most
  children who disclose in a competent interview do **not** later retract. This splits CSAAS into a
  supported half (secrecy, delay) and an unsupported half (routine recantation).*

- **[S11] Alaggia R, Collin-Vézina D, Lateef R (2019). "Facilitators and Barriers to Child Sexual Abuse
  (CSA) Disclosures: A Research Update (2000–2016)." _Trauma, Violence, & Abuse_ 20(2):260–283.**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6429637/>
  — *Peer-reviewed systematic update covering 33 studies. Source for the **ecological model of
  disclosure barriers** (individual, family, community, cultural/societal) and for the finding that
  disclosure is typically a **process over time, often nonverbal or indirect at first**, rather than a
  single narrated event.*

- **[S12] National Child Traumatic Stress Network — _What to Do If Your Child Discloses Sexual Abuse:
  A Guide for Parents and Caregivers._**
  <https://www.nctsn.org/resources/what-do-if-your-child-discloses-sexual-abuse-guide-parents-and-caregivers>
  — *NCTSN is the SAMHSA-funded national trauma network and the standard U.S. authority for child
  trauma practice. Source for the caregiver-response rules: **believe the child; stay calm** (a shocked
  or angry reaction is read by the child as directed at them); **do not promise secrecy** before or
  after hearing; **do not lead or interrogate** — let the child use their own words; **reassure that it
  is not their fault.** **Landing page consulted; the detailed fact-sheet PDF was not machine-readable
  in retrieval,** so the specific guidance above is corroborated against [S13] and [S18] rather than
  quoted from the PDF alone.*

- **[S13] National Children's Alliance / Children's Advocacy Center model — forensic interviewing.**
  <https://www.nationalchildrensalliance.org/media-room/national-statistics-on-child-abuse/> ·
  NCAC protocol: <https://files.calio.org/Other%20NCAC%20pubs/Child_forensic_interview_structure_ot_NCAC04_190901.pdf>
  — *The CAC model and its forensic-interview protocol. Source for the **contamination rule**: a
  forensic interview is a single recorded conversation by a trained neutral interviewer using a
  standardized protocol, precisely so the child's account is **not shaped by repeated leading
  questioning** from untrained adults — and for the documented problem that a child may have already
  recounted the allegation to as many as **six** untrained people before reaching the CAC. This is the
  evidence behind "report it; do not investigate it."*

## Responding to a disclosure

- **[S14] Ullman SE (2014). "Social reactions to sexual assault disclosure, coping, perceived control,
  and PTSD symptoms in sexual assault victims." _Journal of Community Psychology_ 42(4):495–508.**
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24910478/>
  — *Ullman's program of research is the empirical backbone of this module's central clinical claim.
  Source for the mechanism: **negative social reactions to disclosure predict greater PTSD symptoms**,
  while **positive reactions predict greater perceived control over recovery, which in turn predicts
  fewer PTSD symptoms.** Perceived control is the mediator — which is why *choice* is the active
  ingredient in a good response, not sympathy.*

- **[S15] Relyea M, Ullman SE (2015). "Unsupported or Turned Against: Understanding How Two Types of
  Negative Social Reactions to Sexual Assault Relate to Postassault Outcomes." _Psychology of Women
  Quarterly_ 39(1):37–52.**
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0361684313512610>
  — *The factor-analytic split that makes the finding usable: negative reactions are **not one thing**.
  **Turning Against** (blaming, doubting, stigmatizing, treating the person differently) and
  **Unsupportive Acknowledgment** (distraction, taking control, egocentric responses) are distinct, and
  **Turning Against is the more strongly PTSD-predictive** of the two. This is what lets the module say
  which well-meant responses are merely unhelpful and which actively harm.*

- **[S16] RAINN — National Sexual Assault Hotline (800-656-HOPE) and survivor/supporter guidance.**
  <https://rainn.org/>
  — *The largest U.S. anti-sexual-violence organization and operator of the national hotline; cited as
  a **service and referral resource only.** **Its pages returned HTTP 403 to retrieval and were not
  read directly**, so no statistic or guidance claim in this module rests on RAINN. Where similar
  practice guidance is asserted, it is sourced to [S12], [S17], or [S30].*

- **[S17] Sexual Violence Research Initiative (SVRI) — _What to say and what not to say to a survivor
  of sexual assault._**
  <https://www.svri.org/what-to-say-and-what-not-to-say-to-a-survivor-of-sexual-assault/>
  — *SVRI is the leading global research network on sexual violence (originally WHO-incubated).
  Practitioner-facing guidance, used for the concrete phrase-level do/don't list — "I believe you,"
  "it's not your fault," "thank you for telling me" versus "are you sure?", "why didn't you fight
  back / report it / tell someone sooner?", "what were you wearing?", "why are you still upset?"
  Guidance tier, not evidence tier; the *reason* these harm is carried by [S14][S15].*

- **[S18] Tener D, et al. (2023). "Psychologist attitudes towards disclosure and believability of
  childhood sexual abuse: Can biases affect perception, judgement, and action?" _Child Abuse & Neglect_.**
  <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0145213423004945>
  — *Peer-reviewed evidence that the harmful response is not hypothetical **inside the therapy room**:
  **over a third of CSA survivors reported harmful therapist responses to their disclosures**,
  including disbelief, belittlement, and blame, and inadequate clinician response predicted the abuse
  staying hidden, damaged alliance, and therapy dropout. **Abstract- and summary-level consultation;
  full text paywalled.***

- **[S19] Campbell R, Wasco SM, Ahrens CE, Sefl T, Barnes HE (2001). "Preventing the 'Second Rape':
  Rape Survivors' Experiences With Community Service Providers." _Journal of Interpersonal Violence_
  16(12):1239–1259. See also Campbell R (2006), _Violence Against Women_ 12(1):30–45.**
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/088626001016012002> ·
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1077801205277539>
  — *The foundational work on **secondary victimization** ("the second rape") — harm done by the
  systems a survivor turns to for help. Source for the finding that most survivors who engaged legal
  or medical systems did not receive needed services, and that system contact left many feeling
  guilty, depressed, anxious, and reluctant to seek further help, with secondary victimization
  **positively correlated with PTSD symptoms.** This is why the referral, not just the response, is a
  clinical act.*

- **[S20] Counseling Today (American Counseling Association) — "Supporting disclosure for adult male
  survivors of child sexual abuse" (2021); 1in6.**
  <https://ct.counseling.org/2021/06/supporting-disclosure-for-adult-male-survivors-of-child-sexual-abuse/>
  · <https://1in6.org/>
  — *ACA's practitioner publication (the profession's own outlet) plus the leading U.S. organization
  for male survivors. Source for the male-survivor disclosure gap — commonly cited as **two decades or
  more** between abuse and first disclosure — and for the gendered barriers (masculinity norms, fear of
  being seen as a "failed man," fear of being assumed to become a perpetrator, confusion about arousal
  during abuse). Practitioner/advocacy tier; the **magnitude of the delay is a commonly-reported
  clinical figure, not a precise meta-analytic estimate** — cite it as a pattern, not a statistic.*

## What sexual trauma does

- **[S21] Möller A, Söndergaard HP, Helström L (2017). "Tonic immobility during sexual assault — a
  common reaction predicting post-traumatic stress disorder and severe depression." _Acta Obstetricia
  et Gynecologica Scandinavica_ 96(8):932–938.**
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28589545/> ·
  <https://obgyn.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aogs.13174>
  — *The most-cited empirical study of peritraumatic tonic immobility in sexual assault. Prospective
  cohort at a Stockholm emergency clinic: of **298 women**, **70% reported significant** and **48%
  extreme tonic immobility** during the assault; at six months (n=189), **38.1% had PTSD** and **22.2%
  severe depression**, with TI predicting both. This is the single most clinically useful finding in
  the module — the empirical answer to "why didn't you fight back?"*

- **[S22] de la Torre Laso J (2024). "The Reality of Tonic Immobility in Victims of Sexual Violence:
  'I was Paralyzed, I Couldn't Move'." _Trauma, Violence, & Abuse_ 25(3).**
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/15248380231191232>
  — *Peer-reviewed review corroborating TI as a **common, involuntary, evolutionarily conserved
  defensive response** to inescapable threat — not a choice, not consent, and not evidence against an
  account. **Abstract-level consultation; full text paywalled.** Used to establish that [S21] is not a
  lone finding.*

- **[S23] Burgess AW, Holmstrom LL (1974). "Rape trauma syndrome." _American Journal of Psychiatry_
  131(9):981–986. Admissibility: New York Unified Court System, _Guide to NY Evidence_ §7.05.**
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4415470/> ·
  <https://nycourts.gov/JUDGES/evidence/7-OPINION/7.05_%20Rape_Trauma_Syndrome.pdf>
  — *The historically pivotal description of post-rape reactions in two phases — an **acute phase** of
  disorganization and a **long-term reorganization phase** beginning roughly 2–3 weeks out. Predates
  **PTSD's 1980 entry into DSM-III**, which substantially absorbed it; survivors today are assessed and
  treated under the PTSD framework, and **RTS is history plus a courtroom term, not a current
  diagnosis.** The NY evidence guide is cited for its explicit characterization of RTS as **"a
  therapeutic concept"** with limited evidentiary use.*

- **[S24] Janoff-Bulman R (1979). "Characterological versus behavioral self-blame: Inquiries into
  depression and rape." _Journal of Personality and Social Psychology_ 37(10):1798–1809.**
  <https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf8f4a1d49479660a825c1ba138b88c5054c9b83> ·
  ERIC: <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED167888.pdf>
  — *The origin of the **behavioral vs. characterological self-blame** distinction: behavioral blame
  attributes to a **modifiable** source (what I did) and preserves a sense of future control;
  characterological blame attributes to a **non-modifiable** source (what I am) and carries
  deservingness. Janoff-Bulman proposed behavioral self-blame as **adaptive** — a control-maintenance
  strategy.*

- **[S25] Frazier PA (1990). "Victim attributions and post-rape trauma." _Journal of Personality and
  Social Psychology_ 59(2):298–304. See also Frazier & Schauben (1989), _J. Social & Clinical
  Psychology_ 8(4):368–381.**
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2213494/> ·
  <https://guilfordjournals.com/doi/10.1521/jscp.1989.8.4.368>
  — *The empirical challenge to [S24], and the reason this module teaches the distinction as
  **contested**: in survivor samples, **both** forms of self-blame were associated with **greater**
  post-rape depression and distress. The taxonomy remains clinically useful for hearing *what kind* of
  blame a person is carrying; the claim that behavioral self-blame is **protective** does not hold.*

- **[S26] Rubin DC, Berntsen D, Ogle CM, Deffler SA, Beckham JC (2016). "Scientific evidence versus
  outdated beliefs: A response to Brewin (2016)." _Journal of Abnormal Psychology_ 125(7):1018–1021;
  and Brewin CR, Field AP (2024). "Meta-Analysis Shows Trauma Memories in PTSD Lack Coherence."
  _Clinical Psychological Science_.**
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5063074/> ·
  <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21677026241240456>
  — *Both sides of the **trauma-memory fragmentation debate**, cited together on purpose. Brewin holds
  that PTSD trauma memories are disorganized and lack narrative coherence; Rubin and colleagues, testing
  60 trauma-exposed adults on 28 coherence measures, found trauma memories **as coherent as** positive
  and important memories, with no PTSD deficit. Reviews from 2006–2016 call the evidence inconclusive,
  partly because "coherence" is operationalized inconsistently. **The clinical consequence is the
  point:** an inconsistent or non-chronological account is **neither proof of trauma nor proof of
  fabrication**, and a counselor is not equipped to adjudicate it.*

## Systems: medical forensic care, reporting, and advocacy

- **[S27] U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women — _A National Protocol for
  Sexual Assault Medical Forensic Examinations: Adults/Adolescents_, 2nd ed. (2013); pediatric
  protocol (2016).**
  <https://www.justice.gov/ovw/media/1367191/dl?inline=>
  — *The federal standard for the medical forensic exam. Source for the exam's **dual purpose**
  (health care **and** evidence collection), the principle of **survivor-centered, victim-directed**
  care — the patient may decline any part of the exam and still receive care — and for the general
  practice of collecting evidence **up to ~120 hours (5 days)** post-assault. **Windows and payment
  rules are set by jurisdiction and have widened in many states; verify locally rather than quoting
  120 hours as universal.***

- **[S28] International Association of Forensic Nurses — _About SANE_.**
  <https://www.forensicnurses.org/page/aboutSANE/>
  — *The credentialing body for forensic nursing. Source for what a **Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner**
  is and does, and for the SANE/SART model in which a nurse examiner, an advocate, and law enforcement
  operate as a coordinated team — the structural answer to the secondary-victimization problem in
  [S19].*

- **[S29] U.S. Department of Defense Sexual Assault Prevention and Response (SAPR) — _Reporting
  Options._**
  <https://www.sapr.mil/reporting-options> · <https://sapr.mil/duty-status>
  — *The authority for the military's two-track system: **Restricted Reporting** (confidential; access
  to medical care, advocacy, and counseling **without** triggering an investigation or command
  notification; may be made only to a **SARC, SAPR Victim Advocate, or healthcare provider**) versus
  **Unrestricted Reporting** (law-enforcement investigation and command notification). The cleanest
  real-world illustration that **reporting is not one binary decision** — and a live issue for any
  counselor who sees service members or veterans.*

- **[S30] Congressional Research Service — _Status of the Education Department's Title IX Regulations_
  (LSB11279); corroborated by law-firm practice alerts on the vacatur.**
  <https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11279> ·
  <https://www.franczek.com/blog/2024-title-ix-final-rule-and-regulations-vacated-by-federal-judge/>
  — *CRS is Congress's nonpartisan research service — the right authority for a regulation in flux. On
  **January 9, 2025**, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky **vacated the 2024
  Title IX Final Rule nationwide**, and the Department of Education returned to enforcing the **2020
  regulations**. **This is the module's most perishable fact.** The durable lesson is structural, not
  regulatory: on a campus, some employees are **confidential** and some are **mandated to report to the
  Title IX Coordinator**, and which one you are determines what you can promise a student before they
  start talking. **Verify the current rule before relying on any specific requirement.***

- **[S31] U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, National Center for PTSD — _Sexual Assault Experienced
  in Adulthood_ and _Providing Mental Health Care for Those Who Experienced Military Sexual Trauma_.**
  <https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/type/sexual_assault_adult.asp> ·
  <https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/type/providing_care_mst.asp>
  — *The federal clinical authority on trauma care. Source for **Military Sexual Trauma (MST)** as VA's
  term for sexual assault or harassment experienced during military service, and for the access rules
  that surprise most counselors: MST-related care is **free**, available to any veteran or service
  member **regardless of era, discharge status, or VA enrollment**, and requires **no prior report and
  no documentation** that it occurred. A concrete, checkable referral fact.*

## Treatment and the counselor's role

- **[S32] Resick PA, Nishith P, Weaver TL, Astin MC, Feuer CA (2002). "A comparison of cognitive-
  processing therapy with prolonged exposure and a waiting condition for the treatment of chronic
  posttraumatic stress disorder in female rape victims." _Journal of Consulting and Clinical
  Psychology_ 70(4):867–879.**
  <https://cptforptsd.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Resick_JCCP_2002_867.pdf>
  — *The landmark RCT, and a fact worth knowing: **CPT was developed by Resick specifically for sexual
  assault survivors.** Both **CPT and PE** produced large reductions in PTSD versus wait-list, and
  **did not differ significantly from each other** — the basis for offering a genuine choice between
  them rather than declaring a winner.*

- **[S33] Resick PA, et al. (2012). "Long-term outcomes of cognitive-behavioral treatments for
  posttraumatic stress disorder among female rape survivors." _Journal of Consulting and Clinical
  Psychology_ 80(2):201–210.**
  <https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/articles/article-pdf/id37854.pdf>
  — *The 5–10 year follow-up of [S32]'s cohort (144 located, 87.5% reassessed). Source for the
  durability claim: treatment gains were **maintained over the long term**. This is what licenses
  telling a survivor that trauma treatment works and holds — a claim worth being able to make
  accurately.*

- **[S34] Cloitre M, et al. — _Treating Survivors of Childhood Abuse and Interpersonal Trauma: STAIR
  Narrative Therapy_, 2nd ed. (Guilford, 2020); systematic review in _Journal of Mood and Anxiety
  Disorders_ (2024).**
  <https://www.guilford.com/books/Treating-Survivors-of-Childhood-Abuse-and-Interpersonal-Trauma/Cloitre-Cohen-Ortigo-Jackson/9781462543281>
  · <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2468749924001169>
  — *The standard **phase-based** protocol for adult survivors of childhood abuse: **eight sessions of
  skills training** in emotion regulation and interpersonal functioning **before** any trauma-focused
  narrative work. Its rationale is the module's clinical thesis in miniature — chronic early
  interpersonal trauma disrupts affect regulation and relationships, so **capacity is built before the
  story is touched.** Review consulted at abstract level; book not read in full.*

- **[S35] Herman JL (1992/2015). _Trauma and Recovery._ Basic Books. Summarized in Herman JL (1998),
  "Recovery from psychological trauma," _Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences_ 52(S1):S98–S103.**
  <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1046/j.1440-1819.1998.0520s5S145.x>
  — *Already a Unit 8 anchor resource; cited here for the **three stages of recovery** — **safety and
  stabilization → remembrance and mourning → reconnection and integration** — and for the rule that the
  stages are **not linear**; movement back and forth is expected. The organizing frame for "what
  happens in trauma treatment, and what stage a foundations-level counselor is actually working in."*

- **[S36] Otgaar H, et al. (2025). "The recovery and retraction of memories of abuse: a scoping
  review." _Frontiers in Psychology_ 16:1498258; with the opposing view in Brand BL, et al., "Tilting
  at Windmills: Why Attacks on Repression Are Misguided."**
  <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1498258/full> ·
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7961625/>
  — *Both sides of the **"memory wars."** The consensus that does exist and that this module teaches:
  **suggestive techniques** — hypnosis, guided imagery, age regression, repeated suggestive
  questioning, symptom-checklist-driven conviction that abuse must have occurred — **can generate
  confident false memories**, and their use has produced malpractice liability and de-licensure.
  Whether memories of real abuse can be inaccessible and later recovered remains **genuinely
  disputed**; that dispute does not license the techniques. Cited for the practice rule, which both
  camps accept, not for a verdict on repression.*

## Supporting a survivor you love (non-clinical)

- **[S37] University of Utah Sexual Assault Awareness & Response — _Secondary Survivors_.**
  <https://sexualassault.utah.edu/secondary-survivors/>
  — *University victim-services office. Source for the **secondary survivor** concept — the friend,
  partner, or family member of someone who was assaulted — and the principle that their own distress
  is real and valid but must not become the survivor's responsibility to manage. Practice-guidance
  tier; the underlying mechanism is [S14][S15]'s finding that the survivor's outcomes turn on the
  reactions they receive.*

- **[S38] CAASE (Chicago Alliance Against Sexual Exploitation) — _How to Support a Partner Who Was
  Sexually Assaulted_; HelpGuide — _Reconnecting with Sex and Intimacy After Sexual Trauma_.**
  <https://www.caase.org/support-a-partner-who-was-sexually-assaulted/> ·
  <https://www.helpguide.org/mental-health/ptsd-trauma/sex-and-intimacy-after-sexual-trauma>
  — *Advocacy and consumer-health guidance for partners: changes in sexual interest and response are
  common; triggers may be unknown until encountered; the survivor sets the pace, with an explicit
  standing option to **stop, change, or start again**. **Lowest-tier sources in this file — orientation
  and framing only.** No clinical or efficacy claim rests on them; where the module makes a mechanistic
  claim about triggers or avoidance it cites [S31] or Unit 8's
  [`concept-neurobiology-of-trauma`](../wiki/concept-neurobiology-of-trauma.md).*

- **[S39] Multiple peer-reviewed and practice sources on cultural barriers to disclosure: "Navigating
  silence: cultural, familial, and immigration influences on the sexual violence experiences of Asian
  female college students," _BMC Public Health_ (2025); MCASA, "Sexual Violence and Immigrant
  Survivors."**
  <https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12889-025-24487-1> ·
  <https://mcasa.org/newsletters/article/sexual-violence-and-immigrant-survivors>
  — *Source for the culturally-patterned barriers that make "just tell someone" a naive instruction:
  honor- and shame-based norms, family reputation weighed against individual well-being, taboos around
  discussing sex, concerns about marriageability, community ostracism, language access, and — for
  undocumented survivors — **fear of deportation**. Connects directly to
  [`unit04-multicultural`](../wiki/unit04-multicultural.md). Qualitative and practice-tier evidence
  describing patterns, **not** prevalence claims about any group.*
